# TMB — Story & World Design Bible
### Working draft — Session 1 (pre-canon, everything challengeable)

---

## 1. Overall Reaction

**What's genuinely strong here:**

- **The refusal to isekai the protagonist.** "They choose to go" is the single best structural decision in this document. It changes the entire emotional register of the game — this isn't a victim's story, it's an investigator's story that turns into an adventurer's story. Keep this above almost everything else you're willing to cut.
- **The shield as a mechanic that IS the theme.** A binary, all-or-nothing barrier that protects but also isolates, and that must periodically fail on purpose — that's rare. Most games have "the story wall" and "the gameplay system" as separate things. Here they're the same object. Protect this idea; it's your signature mechanic.
- **Scale as wonder, not as gimmick.** Explicitly not wanting a cartoon shrink ray, wanting a raindrop to be terrifying and a mushroom forest to feel magical without being magic — that's a clear, consistent aesthetic thesis, and it's the thing that will actually differentiate TMB from Smalland/ARK in marketing screenshots, not the taming system.
- **Dad as a contested figure rather than a rescue target.** "Your father saved us" vs. "your father ruined everything" is a genuinely good structural mystery because it's not about *what happened*, it's about *how to feel* about what happened — that survives spoilers, replays, and casual players who only get half the story.

**What's weak or risky right now — said plainly, since you asked for it:**

- **This is not one game's worth of systems, it's four or five.** Count what's here: full creature taming + husbandry, base building, faction diplomacy, empire management, multiplayer co-op, an espionage mystery, a time-distortion mystery, and a coming-of-age father story. Any ONE of those is a legitimate flagship pillar for an indie game. All of them together, at ARK-scale polish, is not a scope problem you can write your way out of — it's a production problem, and it will eventually push back on the writing (see §17). I'd rather tell you that now than watch the story get bent later to match whatever got cut.
- **Time distortion is doing a lot of narrative labor for how little gameplay it currently touches.** Right now it mostly explains contradictory satellite images and justifies one vision. That's a fair amount of conceptual cost (players *will* ask "wait, so can I—") for a payoff that's currently just flavor. §7 gives you a rule set that keeps it honest, but you should also decide how much it actually needs to matter mechanically, or it's pure risk with no reward.
- **The insect-first identity is on a collision course with the human-first plot machinery.** Spies, councils, factions, rivalries, empire diplomacy — all of that is *people* content. Every hour you spend writing council politics is an hour not spent on "what does it feel like to convince a trap-jaw ant to trust you." That's not a reason to cut the human plot, it's a reason to be deliberate about ratio, especially early. If a player's first six hours are dominated by human faction talk, you've already lost the "insects are the point" pitch.
- **The five-second shield window, used as your only crossing mechanic, will get old fast over a 40+ hour game.** It's a great *dramatic beat* for maybe a dozen key moments. As the *only* way anyone ever leaves or enters, used dozens of times over a playthrough, it risks becoming a chore or a running joke rather than tension. §5 proposes a fix that keeps the drama for when it matters.
- **You're overlapping thematically with Beyond Extinction more than the opening avoids.** You've already solved the *opening* collision (no lab accident) — good. But both games now center on: a brilliant, complicated scientist whose choices ripple into someone else's life, and a reality-bending sci-fi event that the family has to make sense of afterward. That's fine as a shared authorial fingerprint, but worth being conscious of so TMB doesn't quietly become "Beyond Extinction but tiny." The insect-scale survival identity is different enough to carry it, but keep an eye on tone — TMB reads more adventurous/hopeful in this doc, which is a good differentiator; I'd lean into that rather than letting the father-mystery pull it dark.

None of this means shrink the ambition. It means: pick a spine now, in this planning stage, and be ruthless about what's core vs. what's "season two." §14 and §17 try to help you do that.

## 2. The Core Story, In My Words

TMB is about a young adult who has spent their whole life being told a story about their father that they never fully believed, and who finally gets a thread to pull. The official version — disappeared, presumed dead, some vague classified explanation — never sat right with them, and a near-fatal car crash hands them one concrete, impossible detail from the edge of unconsciousness. Instead of running from that experience, they chase it: with research skills, computer skills, and their father's own half-finished notes, until they prove something that shouldn't be provable — that there is a place that doesn't show up on any map, and that their father's strangest research was real.

What they find, once they build the technology to actually reach it, is not a monster or a prison — it's a *society*. A community of people living at insect scale, behind a shield their father helped build, surviving through a culture of insect domestication he helped invent. The island reframes the entire mystery: it's not "where is my father," it's "who did my father become when nobody from home was watching." Some people there loved him. Some blame him. Nobody has a clean answer, and the protagonist has to build their own picture of him out of contradictory testimony while living inside the world he shaped.

Underneath that personal mystery is a survival-adventure game about becoming capable in a world where every blade of grass is a landmark and every insect is either a threat, a tool, or a partner, depending on how well you understand it. The player earns trust, earns skill, earns territory — first surviving, then taming, then building, then leading — and the story keeps handing them reasons to go further out, dig deeper, and ask harder questions, without ever putting a ticking clock over the sandbox. The shield isn't just a wall; it's a recurring choice about risk, and the moments it fails are where story and gameplay collide hardest.

Braided through the middle distance is the slower reveal that something about this island bends time as well as space, that someone close to the settlement has been quietly compromising it for reasons that aren't simple greed, and that "getting home" was never actually the only goal worth wanting. By the time the player understands what their father really did — and doesn't — the question the game leaves them with isn't "did I rescue him," it's "what am I going to build here, now that I know."

## 3. The Best Version of the Opening

Your sequence is already close to right. Here's a tightened version with a few changes flagged:

1. **Normal life, brief.** Home + one university/lab-adjacent scene. Establish: protagonist's competence (a small, real problem they solve — not a montage, one specific electronics/code task), the mother (warm but visibly tired of the subject of Dad), one friend/NPC who knows the protagonist is "still obsessed with the disappearance thing" — this is efficient characterization *and* tells the player this obsession predates tonight.
2. **The journal, seeded not solved.** Protagonist re-reads a passage from the father's journal they've read a hundred times — not new information, just tone-setting. *Recommendation:* don't reveal a literal coordinate or drawing here. Save concrete clues for after the crash, so the vision feels like it adds something the journal alone never gave them.
3. **The crash.** Mundane cause (weather, distraction, another driver — keep it *ordinary*, because a mundane crash makes the vision that follows feel more intrusive/wrong by contrast).
4. **The vision — 60-90 seconds, pre-rendered.** I'd anchor it around **one landmark image + one sensory detail + one human element**, not a checklist. E.g.: the shield as a specific visual signature (not just "a glow" — give it one memorable shape/color/behavior you'll pay off later), a single fragment of sound or word that shouldn't be explicable (the father's voice saying something oddly specific, not "help me" — something *particular*, like a name, a number, or a phrase only the family would recognize), and one image the protagonist consciously misremembers/gets slightly wrong at first (a detail they later realize they saw incorrectly — this is a great way to seed the time-distortion theme without explaining it yet).
5. **Hospital.** Doctors explain it away. *Recommendation:* have the mother be the one who pushes back hardest against the protagonist taking the vision seriously — not out of denial, but because she watched this obsession define her kid's whole life already and is afraid of watching it swallow them completely. That gives you conflict that isn't a strawman skeptic.
6. **Investigation, digital-first.** Maps, satellite, historical records — this is a great place for real, playable computer-based minigame content (see §17) rather than cutscene. The contradictory satellite passes should surface *here*, not later — it's your first real "something is wrong with reality itself" beat.
7. **Illegitimate access to restricted data.** I'd soften "hacks a secret satellite" to: the protagonist uses their father's old, still-valid research credentials/backdoor into a university or research-network data-sharing system he used to access oceanographic/geospatial data legitimately — more believable, and it's a nice, quiet, bittersweet character beat (using Dad's own leftover access to find Dad).
8. **The device.** Reconstruction with real friction — failures, near-misses, at least one experiment that goes wrong in a way that's scary rather than funny (tonally, you want the player to feel the stakes of this tech before they ever use it on themselves).
9. **Preparing the expedition.** This is where I'd put your first real NPC-relationship beats outside the mother — someone finds out what they're planning and either tries to stop them or insists on helping. Good place to plant the seed of whichever companion solution you land on in §10.
10. **The voyage — the world gets stranger before the island appears.** Use every item on your list (GPS, compass, clocks, stars) but pick 2-3 to actually dramatize rather than listing all of them; more is not scarier here, specific is.
11. **First shrink, arrival, first sight of the shield.** End the opening *before* first contact with the settlement — that first conversation deserves its own beat, not a tacked-on final scene.

## 4. The Scale Problem — Three Systems

### System A — Two-Stage Discrete
Normal (~1.7-1.9m) → single shrink directly to insect scale (~7-10mm).

- **Insects:** Every creature only needs to be designed/tuned for one relative scale. Simplest possible creature pipeline.
- **Shield:** Works cleanly — one "inside scale," one "outside scale," shield is the literal boundary between them.
- **Gameplay:** Fastest to a satisfying core loop; least "in-between" content to build.
- **Multiplayer:** Trivial — everyone is always the same scale, so shared physics/combat/collision never has to reconcile two players at different sizes.
- **Technical:** Lowest cost by far. One asset density tier, one LOD strategy, one physics scale.
- **Downside:** You lose the *staged wonder* of shrinking twice — the "wait, I'm about to feel small again" beat only happens once.

### System B — Three-Stage (as originally proposed)
Normal → outer-island stage (~7-10cm) → main insect stage (~7-10mm) → optional micro-stage (~0.7-1mm).

- **Insects:** Needs at least two fully-tuned creature scales (things that are wildlife at 10cm are set-dressing or non-threats at 10mm, and vice versa) — roughly doubles creature design/animation/AI workload, and the micro-stage would need an almost entirely separate bestiary (mites, dust, water-tension creatures).
- **Shield:** Which scale is the settlement calibrated to? You'd need to explain why the shield only functions at one specific scale, which is doable (see §5) but adds a rule you have to keep consistent everywhere.
- **Gameplay:** Best "sense of escalating wonder," and gives you three distinct visual identities to market. But it also means three separate "getting good at this scale" onboarding arcs.
- **Multiplayer:** Real problem — if players can independently choose their current scale, you need physics, combat, and interaction rules for cross-scale play (can a 10cm player even perceive/interact with a 10mm one meaningfully?), or you force the whole party to scale-sync, which undercuts player freedom.
- **Technical:** Highest cost. Effectively three games' worth of environment/creature tuning, or a very smart procedural system to avoid hand-building three worlds.

### System C — Bounded Continuous Scaling
One discrete "arrival" shrink (Normal → insect-scale range), then the device allows **fine, continuous adjustment within a narrow band** (say, 4mm-20mm) as an ongoing, story-justified gameplay tool — not a second full world tier, just a dial.

- **Insects:** Bestiary is still built around one general scale band, but individual encounters get a genuine puzzle layer — go slightly bigger to survive a fight you're not equipped for, go slightly smaller to fit through a gap or avoid detection, at a resource/risk cost.
- **Shield:** You can make the shield itself only stable within that band, which is a clean, simple rule and reinforces why the settlement lives where it does.
- **Gameplay:** This is the version that actually delivers on "the device can control the target scale" as a real mechanic rather than a one-time plot beat — every playthrough, the player is *using* the thing you built, not just remembering it happened once.
- **Multiplayer:** Bounded range keeps cross-player scale differences small enough to stay physically and narratively coherent (a friend 30% bigger than you is a gameplay variable, not a different game).
- **Technical:** Moderate cost — you need scale-aware collision/animation blending within the band, but you're not building second and third full environment tiers. This is *additive complexity on top of System A*, not a multiplier of it.

### Recommendation
**System A as your production baseline, with System C layered on top as the signature mechanic — treat System B's outer stages as scripted, mostly-linear sequences (the boat approach, maybe a late-game "microscope-world" side area) rather than fully open, fully-tuned world tiers.** This gets you: one insect-scale world to actually build well, a real payoff for "the device controls target scale" that players use constantly rather than once, and it keeps the door open for a true third scale tier as post-launch content once you know the core game is fun. Don't promise the 0.7mm mite-scale world in your pitch document — keep it as a stretch goal you're free to be excited about privately.

## 5. The Shield

**What it is:** Not a force wall in the "energy shield" sense — reframe it as a **localized scale-stability field**. The premise: the anomaly that surrounds the island means matter and creatures don't hold a stable relative scale near its boundary without active correction. The shield doesn't keep insects out because it's a fence; it keeps them out because *anything crossing the boundary passes through a zone where scale itself is briefly unstable*, and the field is what keeps everything inside the settlement locked at a consistent, livable scale. This single idea earns you almost everything you asked for:

- **Why Dad built it:** Without it, the settlement's own scale would drift or flicker, which is lethal (imagine involuntarily growing or shrinking mid-step). It's survival infrastructure, not a luxury wall.
- **Why it can't stay perfectly safe forever:** Any field strong enough to hold a stable scale bubble needs continuous power. Dad's original power source is finite and can't be manufactured locally — so the shield's *reliability*, not its existence, degrades over 21 years. This also gives you a built-in reason resource expeditions matter narratively, not just mechanically.
- **Why it can't be "half open":** A stability field either holds a boundary or it doesn't — there's no physically coherent version of "partially open" that wouldn't tear apart anything caught in the gradient. This is your best answer to "why can't people just walk through a door" — there's no door, because the field IS the wall, and it's binary by nature, not by choice.
- **How people/creatures cross:** This is where I'd revise your original pitch. Have **two tiers**, not one:
  - **Everyday transit — small local gates.** Short, low-power pulses at dedicated points (an "airlock," to use your word), used constantly for routine foraging/expedition traffic, guarded, with a much smaller field-collapse radius and shorter window. Low drama, high frequency — this is how the game avoids every single trip out feeling apocalyptic.
  - **Full shield drop — rare, high-stakes.** Reserved for large parties, mounts, emergencies, or when the small gates are damaged/offline. *This* is where your five-second countdown lives — and because it's rare, it stays dramatic instead of becoming routine. I'd keep 5 seconds; it's tight enough to be tense, long enough to be fair, and it's a great number for a visible on-screen countdown.
- **What happens when something gets in:** Build this as a real, repeatable game system — a "breach" event with escalating tiers (a lost beetle that's mostly just chaos and property damage, up through something genuinely dangerous that requires the whole settlement to respond). Outcomes should range from containment, to combat, to — sometimes — a taming opportunity if the player handles it well. That gives breaches narrative texture (comic relief some of the time, real danger other times) instead of being one fixed "disaster" beat that gets old on repeat.

## 6. Dad's Backstory — Three Versions (don't commit yet)

### Version 1 — The Accidental Prisoner
He was shrunk and pulled through the anomaly during a private, unsanctioned experiment. Arrived to find no prior population — built the settlement essentially from nothing, using shrunk insects for labor and survival, discovering domestication out of necessity rather than curiosity. Never returned because the same finite power problem that constrains the shield also constrains any return trip — he simply ran out of the resources a return would cost. Carries real guilt: an early test-insect population that reverted/escaped its controlled scale caused a lasting ecological problem the settlement still deals with. **Where he is now:** most likely still there, aged by the settlement's actual timeline, a founding-father figure who's both revered and privately guilt-ridden.

### Version 2 — The Willing Exile
He fled deliberately — not an accident, an escape. He'd been part of a classified program before the disappearance (echoing the family's unsatisfying "official explanation"), and once he understood what his scale technology could become in the wrong hands, he vanished on purpose to keep it — and himself — out of reach. He helped found or strengthen the shield partly as protection *from the outside world finding him*, not just from insects. **Where he is now:** could easily be alive and *still actively working to keep the island hidden*, which sets up real tension when the protagonist's own expedition risks undoing 21 years of his caution.

### Version 3 — The Complicated Founder (my favorite of the three, see below)
The island already had people when Dad arrived — a small, struggling population, possibly accumulated across different decades or centuries due to the same time-distortion effect (a shipwreck survivor, an earlier lost researcher, etc.), barely surviving. Dad didn't create this society, he *modernized and possibly saved* it — the shield, insect domestication as an organized practice, real infrastructure — all things he brought that the existing culture didn't have. This is what makes "your father saved us" and "your father ruined everything" both true depending on who's talking: he clearly kept people alive who might otherwise have died out, but he also irreversibly changed a culture that had its own way of surviving before him, and some of those changes had costs nobody fully anticipated. **Where he is now:** here's where I'd push the story somewhere more interesting than "waiting to be found" — what if he's alive, but not part of the settlement's current leadership? He could have drifted toward a different faction entirely (see §13) as his own values or disagreements with the settlement's direction evolved over two decades. Meeting him wouldn't be a simple reunion; it'd be a confrontation between the father the protagonist imagined and the man who's spent 21 years becoming someone shaped by choices the protagonist wasn't there for.

**Why I'd lean toward Version 3 (or a 2+3 blend):** it's the only one that gives the island a history that isn't *just* "the place Dad built," which matters enormously for making the setting feel real and lived-in rather than a diorama assembled around your protagonist's family drama. It also organically explains contradictory oral histories, plays beautifully with time distortion, and gives you the most morally complicated — and most narratively productive — version of "found him, but it's complicated."

## 7. Time Distortion — A Closed Rule Set

To keep this from becoming a plot-hole generator, six hard rules:

1. **Time never runs backward, anywhere, for anyone.** No retrocausality. This alone eliminates 90% of the paradox risk.
2. **What varies is *rate*, not direction.** Regions nearer the anomaly's core run at a different (often slower) forward rate relative to the outside world — think gravitational time dilation, reflavored as an effect of the same scale-distortion field that powers the shield, not literal relativity. Any given location has one consistent rate at any given moment.
3. **The rate can drift slowly over years/decades**, as the anomaly's boundary geometry shifts — this is your clean, physical explanation for satellite passes disagreeing (different passes catch the boundary's optical distortion in different states, sometimes masking the island, sometimes not) without needing the island to be "invisible" by fiat.
4. **Instant rate changes ("flares") are rare, dangerous, and traceable** — never a free plot tool, always tied to a discoverable physical trigger (spikes in shield power draw, device activation events, etc.). This is your license for the one or two truly strange moments in the story (the crash vision being the first) without opening the door to "anything can happen, anytime."
5. **No transit to a point earlier than your own present.** You can experience time differently, but never travel to "before now" for yourself. This is what keeps Dad's disappearance from becoming a time-loop mess.
6. **21 outside years ≠ 21 island years, but don't overcorrect.** I'd land Dad's subjective time somewhere meaningfully less than 21 years but not absurdly so — enough that he's recognizably aged, not a stasis-preserved twentysomething. That keeps the eventual reunion emotionally grounded rather than gimmicky.

**The crash vision, under these rules:** it was a flare — a brief, real, bidirectional information bleed, most plausibly triggered by residual signature from the original device (something in the crash — electrical discharge, the exact geographic alignment, sheer coincidence of timing) resonating with the anomaly. Dad, having lived adjacent to that technology for two decades, is unusually likely to perceive a flare when one happens; the protagonist's unconscious, low-brain-activity state made them momentarily receptive too. Keep the *mechanism* a little soft here (you don't need to over-explain why unconsciousness specifically) — sci-fi convention gives you room for one genuinely mysterious "why him/her, why then" beat, as long as you're disciplined everywhere else.

## 8. The Spy — Four Plots

**A — The True Believer.** A trusted settlement council member has periodic contact with an outside research/defense organization (possibly the same one from Dad's Version 2 backstory). Not selling out for money — genuinely believes the tech is too dangerous to stay hidden forever and that controlled disclosure beats an eventual accidental one. Clues: consistently argues against outside expeditions in ways that happen to serve outside interests; equipment inconsistent with their stated history. Reveal: an old comms rig disguised as scrap. Consequence: forces a real settlement-wide reckoning about trust, and raises the external threat level for the back half of the game.

**B — The Compromised Kid.** A younger settlement member (or friend-group member) has been manipulated by an outside handler exploiting something they love — not spying for money or ideology, just desperate hope of rescue or help for someone they care about. Sympathetic, tragic, not evil. Clues: unexplained absences, guilt-driven behavior, over-eagerness about specific expedition routes. Reveal: they've been sending proof-of-life signals, not selling secrets, without realizing they're being used to triangulate the island. Consequence: a genuine moral dilemma for the player's friend group about how to protect, not punish, them.

**C — Dad Was Framed.** The "classified project" hint from the opening was real — Dad was suspected of being the actual leak back on the mainland, and the true informant used his credentials. His disappearance was partly flight from a false accusation (or an active hunt for the real source). A present-day spy is a continuation of the same operation. Strong for tying the normal-world mystery and island mystery into one thread, but it's the twistiest option and asks the most of the player to track.

**D — The Faction Defector.** Someone from a rival internal faction (say, the control-not-coexist camp) is quietly sabotaging shield maintenance or leaking timing/location details — not for an outside power, but to force a leadership change or a forced merger on the island itself. Keeps espionage entirely internal, which is lower-stakes but tighter in scope.

**Recommendation:** Run **B as the emotional red herring and A as the real answer** — this delivers exactly the "player suspects the wrong person" mystery you said you like, with a genuinely sympathetic false suspect rather than a cheap fake-out. Fold **C in as a late, deeper layer** (the current spy's organization turns out to have roots in whatever really happened to Dad back home) rather than a separate plotline — it pays off as connective tissue instead of a fourth thing to track. Keep **D** in your pocket as faction-tension texture (§13), not the main spy arc — it's good material, just a different scale of stakes.

## 9. Starter Cast (7 recurring characters)

**1. The Rival-Turned-Friend Tamer** — early-20s, settlement-raised, exceptional insect handler, initially cold/competitive with the protagonist. Personal reason to resent Dad (a real cost their own family paid from an early experiment gone wrong), which gives their eventual friendship real weight instead of being automatic. Goal: prove themselves the settlement's best rider; secretly wants to see the outside world. Skills: handling, riding, species knowledge.

**2. The Shield-Keeper Mentor** — 40s-50s, worked directly alongside Dad, best living source of firsthand information, genuinely ambivalent about him (real admiration, real resentment). Pragmatic, gruff, protective. Skills: engineering, power systems. Possible secret: knows more than they've said about where Dad actually is.

**3. The Cautious Scout** — protagonist's age, survivalist rather than idealist about insects — respects them as real threats, not just resources or partners. Grounds the "insects are dangerous, not cute" tone the sandbox needs so taming never feels risk-free. Good rival-to-ally arc.

**4. The Council Voice of Caution** — settlement leadership, controls expedition permissions and resource allocation. Not a villain — just institutionally scarred by decades of losses, which makes the player's ambition a real source of friction rather than a strawman obstacle. (Strong candidate to also be Spy Plot A's true believer.)

**5. The One Who Wants to Go Home** — an older islander, shrunk decades ago against their will, never made peace with it, fixated on reversing the process. Becomes an ally on the tech-progression side but a source of real friction over resource priorities — restoration research vs. settlement survival.

**6. The One Who Doesn't Want to Leave** — younger, settlement-born, has no nostalgia for the "normal world" and doesn't share the protagonist's urgency. Represents the generational gap and gives natural pushback to the "empire builder shaking up the status quo" arc.

**7. The Trader/Fixer** — deals across factions, morally gray, great source of exposition, quest hooks, and black-market shield-fuel plotlines. Also a strong secondary red-herring for the spy mystery.

*(A possible 8th slot — the twin sibling / device companion — is covered in §10, since their design is entangled with that question rather than a standalone cast decision.)*

## 10. The AI Companion Question

The "unselected gender becomes an AI" mechanism is clever as a *symmetry* solution, but it's solving a production problem (avoid two campaigns) with a fictional device that has no real in-world justification — it risks feeling like a meta-mechanic leaking into the story rather than an earned relationship.

**Better split: separate the emotional-companion need from the gameplay-assistant need — they don't have to be the same character.**

- **For the relationship:** make the second character a **fraternal twin sibling**, raised alongside the protagonist, carrying the same missing-father history, who is *always* the opposite gender regardless of which protagonist the player picks — this preserves your symmetry goal without an AI hand-wave, and gives you a much richer relationship than a HUD voice: shared grief, shared obsession, and real potential for divergence (different arrival route, different scale approach, different faction allegiance) that literally embodies the philosophical splits (Characters C/D/E) in someone the player already loves, rather than in strangers.
- **For the gameplay assistant / tutorializing / hint system:** give the protagonist a small AI research-construct embedded in the shrinking device itself — Dad's own leftover tooling, or something the twin builds — which is a great practical solution for tutorialization, lore delivery, and dialogue-during-solo-play, entirely independent of which gender the player picked.

If you want to preserve the *exact* original pitch instead, "a person already on the island the protagonist has a fated bond with" is workable too, but I'd be cautious — it competes with the crash-vision mystery for "why do we have a connection to this place," and I think the twin does more work for less narrative cost.

## 11. Creature Taming Lore

**Stages:** observation → scent/feeding introduction → pheromone research → trust-building → handling → harness fitting → riding → advanced training (command sets, combat discipline, endurance conditioning).

**Why species should behave differently, in-world terms:** eusocial insects (ants, bees) already run on pheromone-based communication and hierarchy — frame Dad's original breakthrough as learning to *synthesize convincing signal chemistry*, essentially getting provisionally accepted into a colony's signal system rather than "earning love." That makes these your natural early-game, teachable creatures. Solitary predators (spiders, mantises, robber flies) don't have that social structure to hack — taming them should be a completely different system: not trust, but conditioning through repeated non-lethal confrontation and hunger-reward chains, with specialized gear, real risk of regression, and a distinctly "this creature tolerates you, it doesn't love you" feel. Some species (specific dangerous specialists — a defensive wasp, something with real venom) should stay essentially untameable by design; it protects the sense that the world outside the shield is genuinely dangerous, not a checklist.

**Progression, not collection:** make each species unlock a genuinely different verb (climb, dig, fly, haul, tank, scout) rather than a reskin, and let breeding surface trait inheritance (temperament, size, specialization) as an endgame optimization layer rather than a launch requirement.

## 12. Player Progression

Investigator → inventor → expedition leader → tiny survivor → creature tamer → explorer → settlement member → leader → builder → faction founder → empire builder.

Gate every stage on **both** a narrative beat (trust earned, mystery piece solved) **and** a mechanical unlock (new crafting tier, new taming category, new shield access) — that's what makes the two tracks reinforce each other instead of running in parallel, which is exactly the "gameplay and story must support each other" principle you flagged as essential.

## 13. Factions (avoiding clean good/evil)

1. **The Shield Settlement** — pragmatic, coexistence-leaning, institutionally cautious after real losses.
2. **The Wildcatchers** — a splinter group (possibly descended from an earlier time-drifted arrival) who reject heavy technology, live closer to the insects, deep taming culture built on reverence rather than utility — a great source of advanced teachers for the player.
3. **Iron Hive** — believe insects are dangerous resources to dominate, not partners; more militarized, exploit insects for labor/war without the trust-based ethos. Coherent worldview born from real losses, not cartoon villainy — good rival power, not a strawman.
4. **The Return Faction** — fixated on reversing the scale tech and leaving; internal political pressure over whether resources go to restoration research or survival infrastructure.
5. **The Outside Organization** — the spy's employer; technically legitimate-sounding, genuinely believes the tech needs "responsible" containment. Your external endgame threat.
6. **Super-colonies as territorial powers** — not sapient, not talking, but genuinely large-scale rival territory on the map (a massive ant empire, a wasp queen's dominion) that the player fights, avoids, or — very late game — partially works with. This keeps insects as real ecosystem participants and not just wildlife dressing, per your own explicit goal.

## 14. Main Story Arcs (8-9)

**Arc I — The Missing Scientist.** Normal world, journal, crash, vision, hospital, obsession begins. *Reveals:* the island might be real. *New mystery:* why it isn't on any map.

**Arc II — The Impossible Island.** Research, contradictory data, device reconstruction with real failures. *Reveals:* scale-manipulation tech is real, Dad built it. *New mystery:* what happened to him after he got there.

**Arc III — Crossing Over.** Expedition, voyage, first shrink, arrival, first sight of the settlement. *Systems:* base scale-shift, first survival loop, first (dangerous, untamed) insect encounter. *Reveals:* people live here and knew Dad. *New mystery:* why is time behaving strangely.

**Arc IV — Becoming Small.** Earning trust, learning the culture, first taming, base-building intro, meet the main cast. *Reveals:* the shield's real mechanics; Dad's contested reputation surfaces. *New mystery:* "saved us" vs. "ruined everything."

**Arc V — The Shield and the Spy.** Resource expeditions, first breach event, first rival-faction contact, the false-suspect spy thread begins. *Systems:* expedition/outpost basics, faction reputation, advanced taming.

**Arc VI — Time and Truth.** Real time-distortion evidence surfaces (ruins, other time-drifted arrivals), the false spy suspect is confronted and cleared, the real spy begins to surface. *Reveals:* deeper Dad history crystallizes toward one version. *New mystery:* where Dad actually is now.

**Arc VII — Empire and Alliance.** Player expands their own outpost network, faction conflict escalates, the outside organization makes a real move, a major shield crisis hits. *Systems:* full empire-building, large-scale defense, co-op content peaks here.

**Arc VIII — Finding Dad.** Confrontation, not simple reunion — the real man vs. the imagined one, a forced philosophical choice about the settlement's future. *Reveals:* the full truth about the anomaly's origin and Dad's actual role.

**Arc IX — Legacy (epilogue layer).** Aftermath; player's choices shape a lasting world-state; sandbox continues indefinitely with new goals rather than a hard stop.

## 15. The Endgame — Three Options

1. **The anomaly is older than Dad.** His discovery was really a rediscovery of something with a longer, stranger history — opens the door to other anomaly sites and sequel/expansion content without needing to blow up the island.
2. **The island was always a refuge, not a prison.** Leaving was technically possible all along at real cost — the actual conflict was never "can we escape," it was "what do we owe the people time-stranded here." Resolves with the player choosing to stay and build, which supports continued sandbox play beautifully.
3. **There are other islands.** Other anomaly pockets, other lost people, other tiny societies — the protagonist becomes a bridge between them, explains why the outside organization cared so much, and hands you a clean structural hook for expansions/DLC "new islands" without contradicting anything.

**Recommendation:** blend **2 and 3** — emotionally resolved (not apocalyptic, not a rescue-and-leave ending) while structurally open for more content. Keep **1** in your pocket as the boldest pure-lore twist if you want a bigger single gut-punch reveal instead.

## 16. The Multiplayer Story Problem

Cleanest fix: **every player has their own missing person, not necessarily "Dad" specifically** — a parent, mentor, or relative who also vanished into the same island across a different era, which your Version 3 backstory and time-distortion rules already support organically (of course more than one person has fallen through). This preserves real personal stakes for every player at the table, avoids the immersion break of "we're all the same person's kid," and avoids cheapening co-op friends into nameless helpers. It also means the island's "collects lost people across time" theme isn't just lore — it's literally how your multiplayer works.

## 17. Technical Reality Check

- **Crash vision:** pre-rendered AI image-to-video, as you already planned — right call, keep it.
- **Dad's history:** journals, audio logs, environmental storytelling (ruins, old equipment) instead of expensive flashback cutscenes.
- **Time-distortion evidence:** mostly environmental storytelling plus a handful of static illustrated vignettes, not full cinematics.
- **Creature variety at scale:** a shared, parameterized "wildlife AI" base (one behavior-tree framework, per-species parameter sets) rather than bespoke code per bug — this is exactly the kind of thing a shared visual-scripting/engine layer pays for itself on if you're building one across your projects.
- **Faction/council politics:** branching dialogue trees with portrait art, not animated conversations.
- **Shield breaches:** a proceduralized spawn-table system with escalating tiers, not hand-scripted incidents.
- **Multiplayer netcode:** the single biggest technical risk in this whole document when combined with full empire-scale building. I'd scope true multiplayer as a later milestone, not a v1 requirement, and build the story so it works cleanly solo first (per §16).
- **Scale system:** build one real insect-scale world (System A) well; treat outer/micro stages as scripted or stretch-goal content, not core production scope (per §4).
