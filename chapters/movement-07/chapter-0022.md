# Chapter 22: No Sun

At ten to six, Jack was sitting on the living room floor with a crib in pieces around him and a screwdriver in his teeth.

It had been leaning against the hall closet in its flat cardboard box since the week before the activation. Nobody had touched it. That morning he couldn't sleep, couldn't sit still, and couldn't do anything useful with one hand. So he had dragged it out and opened it before he could think too hard about why.

Sarah lay on the couch above him with the tablet propped against her knees. The greenhouse cameras filled one half of the screen. The other half showed the crib instructions.

She frowned at the screen. "Step four. Insert tab A into slot B. Define tab A."

Jack took the screwdriver out of his mouth. "The one I'm holding with my teeth."

"That's a screwdriver."

"Then I've been doing step four wrong for ten minutes."

Aiden's voice came on the channel, soft. He and Unit Four had been in the greenhouse since five, with the outside cameras up on Aiden's bench and the blackout blinds still down on the western glass. "Door thermometer says twelve. It barely moved all night."

Mark answered from the station. "And outside?"

"Dark. Not a star anywhere."

By half past six, Jack had both end panels on the crib and one rail, and the end of the western road was coming out of the dark on the tablet the way it had the morning before, the white maintenance cart, the last stretch of asphalt, the wall of stems behind it. But the light was wrong. It didn't grow from one side. It just seeped in from everywhere at once, dull and even, like a room lit through a sheet.

The drops were there. There were fewer of them. The long rows of small beads along the edges of the blades, the ones that had looked like strings of glass yesterday, were thin and patchy. Only the leaf tips still carried their big heavy drops, the same as before.

Sarah leaned closer to the screen. "Aiden, there's less dew. And I can't tell where the sun's supposed to be."

"There's less dew along the edges. On a clear night the ground gets cold, and the air drops its water on it. Clouds keep it warm, like a lid. The tips still drip. That's the plant's water. The plant doesn't care about clouds."

Mark's voice was dry. "So the clear mornings are the wet ones."

"The clear, cold mornings. Yes."

High in the stems, the silk retreat hung pale and still.

Six forty came. Then six forty-two.

Nothing touched the grass. No gold, no light catching in the drops. The gray just went on being gray.

Lena read it out the way she read everything. "Six forty-two. No sun."

Mark sounded almost pleased. "There's your rule."

Jack turned a bolt with his good hand, slowly, and didn't take his eyes off the tablet. Six forty-five. Six forty-eight. The retreat didn't move.

Sarah wrote something in her notebook and underlined it. "If it waits for the sun, it could wait all day."

Mark didn't sound worried. "Then we have all day."

At six fifty-three, the bag moved.

It wasn't the slow push of the other mornings, legs first, then the body, then a long pause. The spider came out of the silk in one smooth motion and was on the stem, and then it was going down the stem, and it didn't stop.

Aiden's voice dropped to a whisper. "It's not sitting."

Jack had stopped with a crib rail in his hand.

The spider moved along the nearest blade in short bursts. Three steps, stop. Three steps, stop. It turned its whole front end in tiny jerks, left, right, down, the big front eyes staring over everything below it like a pair of searchlights. Two small dark feelers by its mouth flicked up and down as it went.

It reached a drop as big as a barrel and stopped. It lowered its face right into the water.

Sarah's hand found Jack's shoulder. "What's it doing?"

Aiden answered before Jack could. "It's drinking. They drink from dew. I've watched the little ones do it on the seedlings."

The spider drank for a while. Then it lifted its face and moved on.

Two drops along, there was a gnat stuck in the water, small and dark, about the size of a cat. The spider passed right over it. It didn't stop or turn.

Aiden breathed out. "It's dead. Or it's too cold to move. It doesn't see it."

Sarah's pen stopped. "It's right there."

"It doesn't see still things. Not really. It sees things that move."

A little further down the blade there was another gnat, in a smaller drop. Jack saw it twitch. One wing, then a leg. Then it kicked against the water.

The spider swung its whole front toward it.

It didn't jump this time. It crept, three steps, two, one, its body pressed low to the blade. Then it struck, so fast that Jack saw it only as a change, the spider in one place, then in another with the gnat held in front of it.

The blade bent under the sudden weight. At its tip, the big drop shivered, slid, and came off.

It fell onto the empty end of the road and slumped into a low, trembling sheet, right over the spot where Aiden had crouched the morning before. It sat there, shivering, and then slid off the edge into the soil and was gone.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Aiden spoke, slowly, as though he were working it out as he went. "It's not hunting the road. It's hunting the drops. Everything that got stuck in them overnight starts kicking when it warms up. And it takes the ones that kick."

Sarah was writing again, fast. "Six fifty-three, out. Six fifty-six, drinking. Seven oh one, the gnat. No basking."

The spider climbed back up the stem with the gnat and stopped at the edge of the light, eating. Below it, in the gray, more insects were arriving. Jack could see them coming in out of the grass, flies mostly, heavy and slow, settling on the tips of the leaves where the big drops had been.

Aiden made a small sound. "The plant's water has sugar in it. It dries sticky on the tips. They come for that."

Lena kept her voice flat. "So the drops feed the flies, and the flies feed the spider."

"And the spider drinks the drops. Yes."

One of the flies didn't land on a leaf. It came buzzing low over the end of the road, as big as a dog, and settled heavily on the white side of the maintenance cart.

Unit Four's voice came on the channel at once, not waiting. "Fly on the cart."

The spider turned to face the cart.

It finished the gnat. Then it came down.

It came down the stem and onto the soil, and from the soil onto the asphalt. It crossed the end of the road in short, low bursts. Three steps. Stop. Three steps. Stop. The fly on the cart cleaned its face with its front legs.

The spider was halfway across the road. Then it was beside the cart's rear wheel, pressed low.

Then the fly was gone.

The spider stood on the asphalt beside the cart for a long time, the fly held in front of it. It stood in the gray, in the open, right where the rope had run out to the hitch the morning before.

Unit Four's voice was very quiet. "That's where I was standing."

Mark didn't say anything about that. "Good call, Four."

"Thank you."

This time he didn't say sir.

The spider turned, walked back across the road at an unhurried pace, climbed the stem and went into the silk bag with its breakfast.

Jack realized he was still holding the crib rail. He set it down.

Sarah had turned back through her notebook. Her pen moved down one page, then another.

"Aiden. The door thermometer. What did it say the last two mornings, when the sun came up?"

Aiden didn't need to look. "Five. Then six. Yesterday it was six."

Sarah's pen tapped the page. "And today?"

"Thirteen, when it came out."

Sarah sat up a little against the cushions, and Jack didn't stop her. "Then that's it. On the cold mornings, the sun came up, and it came out, and it sat still in the sun first. On the seventh, it sat there a quarter of an hour. That wasn't patience. It was cold. It was warming up. Today there was nothing to warm up from. So there was no waiting."

Aiden made the same sound he had made in the greenhouse that first morning, half a laugh and half a gasp. "It's not the sun. The sun tells it when. The cold tells it how fast."

Jack picked it up from there. "And the cold mornings are the wet mornings. Most water, slowest spider."

Over the channel, from the station, he heard the squeak of a marker on the whiteboard. Mark read it out as he wrote.

"Cold. Clear. Gray."

Aiden cleared his throat. "Can I add one?"

"Go ahead."

"Covered. Paul's lid isn't optional. Anything that holds water out there has a lid on it. An open trough isn't a trough. It's just one more drop. A long one. Whatever falls into it and kicks is breakfast, and you saw where it goes for breakfast."

There was a long silence on the line. Then the marker squeaked again.

Mark's voice was level. "Lena. Get Paul on the radio. I want to see his trough."
