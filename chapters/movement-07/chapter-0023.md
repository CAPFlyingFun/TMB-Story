# Chapter 23: Covered

Paul's crew brought the trough into the yard behind the station a little before eleven, on the back of a flatbed, with four men walking beside it to keep it from sliding off.

It wasn't a gutter anymore. It was a long, low box of corrugated roofing sheet, riveted at every seam, standing on short steel feet. It was as wide as a door and came up to Jack's knee. Along one long side, a splash board rose higher than Jack's head. The lid lay folded in three hinged sections along the front edge.

Mark walked all the way around it once without saying anything. Then he knocked on the side with his knuckles.

"It's big."

Paul climbed down from the flatbed, wiped his hands on a rag, and nodded at Jack. "He says the drop's as wide as a bench. This holds a few of those, if you're ever lucky enough to get a few. Which you won't be."

He took Jack's pole off the truck and held it up. The yellow sponge was gone. In its place was a short padded fork, wrapped in rubber. A hand's width below it, a stiff rubber cup was clamped around the pole, open end down.

"That's your collar. Water comes down the pole, it hits that, and it drips off there instead of running into your glove. The fork goes under the leaf. You lift, you let go. One shake."

Lena came out of the station with a clipboard and looked the trough over. "What are we testing it with?"

Paul jerked his head at a blue plastic drum on the flatbed. "Treated water. From the holding tank. Same as the greenhouse gets. You're not spending one drop of the good stuff on a drill."

They hauled the drum up the steel stairs to the station's loading platform. Jack judged the height with his eye. Three meters, near enough. About where the big drop had hung over the end of the road.

Mark pointed at the gravel below. "Trough goes where the drop would land. Right under."

Paul and Unit Four set it there, the splash board on the platform side, the way Paul had built it to stand against the grass.

Mark stepped back. "Tip it."

Two of Paul's men tipped the drum over the edge of the platform. A long column of water came down, slapped onto the far edge of the trough, and didn't splash. It slammed down and spread, and half of it climbed the far lip like something alive and came over the side in a clear sheet. It went straight across the gravel and over Mark's boots, up to his shins, and clung there.

Mark looked down at himself. "That's twice this week."

Unit Four made a sound that he turned into a cough.

Jack was already looking at where the water had hit. "It didn't land under the platform. It landed out from it. It fell, and it kept moving forward."

Paul nodded slowly. "Then my board's on the wrong side. It wants to be where the water's going, not where it's coming from."

"A drum gets thrown. A leaf might throw it too, when it springs back. We won't know which way until we're there."

Mark was still looking at his boots. "Then the board goes on our side. Between the drop and the people. Whatever happens, it's in the way."

They turned the trough around. Paul's men refilled the drum from the holding-tank line and hauled it up again.

The second time, the water hit the splash board with a hollow boom, sheeted down it, and dropped into the trough. It rolled from one end to the other and back, slopping at the lips, and stayed in.

Lena wrote something on the clipboard. "How much?"

Paul crouched and looked along the waterline. "A fifth of one of your drops, maybe. Still more than a week of mornings with that sponge."

Mark nodded at the lid. "Then close it."

Unit Four took hold of the first lid section and swung it over. It went down onto the water, and the water rose up to meet it, the way it had climbed Jack's pole. When he went to lift it back to check, it didn't want to come. He pulled harder, and the lid came up all at once, dragging a heavy sheet of water up with it that hung from the underside and then dropped across his arms.

Unit Four didn't flinch. He held the lid where it was until the water had finished running off it.

Paul was already pulling a roll of rubber strip out of his pocket. "Not your fault. It sticks. Water at our size grabs anything flat. I'll run this along the edges, so it sits on the rim, not the water. And you close it slow. Like Aiden said about cups. Never lift it fast, never lift it at all out there. You close it, and you leave it closed."

Jack's wrist buzzed. Aiden's voice came through, tired and careful. "Doctor Bennett, the long test's done."

Jack stepped away from the noise. "Go on."

"There's something living in it. Not much. Not anything I'd lose sleep over. The kind of thing that's in every pond, at a very low level. Nothing boiling won't kill."

Jack thought of Aiden drinking the first cup, flat and tasting of nothing.

"And the cup you drank yesterday?"

"Was boiled. I'm fine. But that's the rule now. Boil it. Always. And whatever stands out there with water in it gets covered. It's not only things falling in. It's things growing in it."

Jack relayed it. Mark wrote Boil it, always on the side of the trough with a grease pencil, in letters a hand high, so nobody could miss it.

Paul ran his rubber strip along the first edge while Lena held the lid steady. He didn't look up. "Your security people. Anything yet on who did it?"

Jack glanced at Mark. Mark was looking at the trough. Jack answered for both of them.

"Not yet."

Paul pressed the strip down along the rim with his thumb, the whole length of it.

"Then I'll keep building."

In the afternoon, Mark wheeled his whiteboard out of the station and stood it on the gravel next to the trough. Under Cold, Clear, Gray, Covered, he wrote the numbers, one line at a time, and read them out as he wrote.

"Clear sky at first gray. Stars. Not a cloud. Door thermometer at eight degrees Celsius or under, when we get there. Aiden reads it, nobody else. Trough goes out in the dark, under the red lamp, board on our side. One leaf. One lift. One drop. Lid shut, slow. Everybody back off the edge at first sun, or at ten degrees on that door, whichever comes first. I don't care what's still hanging."

He capped the marker. "This isn't a water supply. It's a test. If it works, we've learned one thing. If it doesn't, we've learned another."

Jack read the list twice. It was good. It was better than good. It was the plan he would have made if he hadn't wanted the water so badly.

"Agreed."

Doctor Mercer came to the house at eight. Sarah had her notebook open on her knee again, three pages fuller than the night before. Doctor Mercer wrapped the cuff around her arm and watched the numbers come up.

Then she watched them a second time.

"Better."

Sarah looked up. "Better as in better?"

"Better as in a little. Better as in I'll take it. Don't celebrate."

"I wasn't going to celebrate. I was going to write it down."

Doctor Mercer turned to Jack. She peeled back the corner of his dressing, looked at the stitches, and pressed the tape back down. "Pink. No worse."

On her way to the door she stopped by the corner, where the crib stood with both ends and one rail on, leaning slightly to the left. She looked at it for a while.

"Who's building that?"

"Me. One-handed."

She picked up her bag. "It shows. If you're on that pole in the morning, wear the glove and the bag. Take them off the second you're back in the truck."

At half past ten, Lena's voice came over Jack's wrist. "Aiden's at the greenhouse. The clouds have gone. He can see stars from the door. Thermometer says seven degrees, and it's falling."

Mark's voice came in a moment later, on the same channel. He must have been listening. He was quiet for a while.

"Then we test it. First gray. One shake. Paul, load it."

Paul's voice was thick with sleep. "Already on the truck. I had a feeling."

The channel went quiet.

Jack set the alarm on his wrist for a quarter past four, and checked it, and set it again. Then he sat down on the couch next to Sarah. She had the notebook closed now, her hands folded over it, and she was looking at him.

He looked back. "Now you."

"Me what?"

"You did it to me last time. Now it's my turn. If your number goes up while I'm out there?"

"I call Doctor Mercer."

"Not me."

"Not you."

"And if you feel a headache? Or your eyes go strange?"

"I call Doctor Mercer."

"And if you see something on the cameras that I need to know?"

She smiled at him, slowly. "Then I call Mark. Because you'll be busy holding a pole, and you shouldn't be looking at your wrist."

Jack thought about arguing with that, and found he couldn't.

She leaned against his shoulder, and he put his good arm around her, and outside, past the rooftops, the stars were out over the grass, cold and clear, exactly the way they wanted them.
