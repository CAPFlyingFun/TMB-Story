# Chapter 3 — The Enclosure

Caleb had been listening to Gus Pelletier on the radio for three weeks. He had never once seen him. Gus kept the boat, and the boat lived down at the dock, and the dock was a quarter mile from the lab.

The man at the end of the station dock was shorter than the voice. He was about sixty. He had a gray beard cut close and a knit cap. His hands did not stop moving the whole time Caleb watched him. He was coiling a line into the bottom of the skiff. He did not look up when the four of them came down the ramp. He did not say good morning either.

Gus tipped his chin at the southwest, where the sky had a high white grain to it, like something combed. "Flat now," he said. "Won't hold. There's that behind it."

"I know," Ines said.

Gus went back to his coiling. "Course you do."

Caleb decided on the spot that he liked him.

The skiff was open aluminum. It had an outboard on the back and a bench across the middle. It was not big. With five people and the gear in it, it was full. Gus handed out life jackets before he touched the lines. He watched them all put one on. He did not start the motor until they had.

"Packs forward," Gus said. "Nobody stands up. If you drop something, let it go."

Marco saluted. Gus ignored it.

They went out past the end of the dock into water the color of a nickel. The morning was cold and gold and completely still. The sun was barely up. It came at them sideways, so every drop off the bow came back lit. Caleb sat on the forward bench with his knees around his pack. He could not stop grinning.

The bar was underwater. That was why they had the boat. High tide came a few minutes either side of eight. The four hundred meters of gravel he had walked on Tuesday were down there somewhere under the shine. By early afternoon it would be back. You could walk home on it then.

They were not going to be here in the early afternoon. That was Ines.

"Birds are on the roost at high water," she had said at the station, before they loaded. "They sit still. You can count them. And I want to be off that island before the wind comes around."

Gus took them around the point. It was a twenty-minute run. Caleb had thought the island would get bigger the way it did on the bar. Slowly. In front of you. For an hour. It did not. It came around the corner all at once and was simply there.

The landing was nothing like Tuesday. At high water the lee beach was only a few strides of gravel. Gus put them in almost at the top of it. Caleb could see the shore logger on its post from the boat. Going in, Gus dropped a small anchor off the stern. He let the line run out through his fist.

"Tide's going out all morning," he said. "She'd dry out where she sits. Then I'm walking her down the beach on rollers like a fool." He nodded at the line. "This way I pull her off when I want her."

"Is that why you do that," said Caleb.

"That's why I do that," Gus said.

They got off the bow one at a time. The gear went hand to hand. Nobody got wet above the boot.

Then they did the work, and they did it right, which was the point of coming.

Nora went to the logger with Ines. Caleb watched her make Marco stand back from the post while she did it. It was funny. Marco had not moved.

"Download first," Nora said. "Then the battery."

"It's a battery," said Marco.

Nora had the cable in her hand. She did not look up. "If you swap the battery first and something's wrong, the memory goes with it. Then we came out here for nothing."

"She's right," Ines said.

The file came off the unit clean. Nora read the record count. Then the last time stamp. Then she held her watch up next to the unit's clock and wrote both numbers down.

"Why," said Caleb.

"Because the record is only as good as the clock." Nora did not look up. "And a swap can knock a clock. So I want it before and after. We had one minute we cared about on Tuesday. Remember."

He remembered.

Only then did she let anybody near the battery. It was hers to swap, because it had been hers to carry since Tuesday. It weighed as much as a cat. She had hauled it to this island and back once without using it. She checked the clock again when the new one was in, and wrote that down too.

The count was easier than Tuesday. It was also stranger. Near high water the birds had nowhere to spread out. The whole spit was down to a gravel ridge the size of a driveway. Every shorebird on Tern Island was standing on it, shoulder to shoulder, facing the same way, asleep.

They stopped well short of the roost and used the binoculars. At high water the birds had nowhere to go if you put them up. Marco called them in blocks of ten. Caleb clicked the counter and wrote.

"Turnstones," Marco said. "Ten. Ten. Ten. Six."

"Thirty-six," said Caleb.

"Sanderlings," said Marco. "Ten. Ten. Four."

The three fat gray plovers were exactly where they had been on Tuesday. They were at the far end, doing exactly nothing. Caleb was unreasonably glad to see them.

They reset the transect walking back. It was a stake short. That was the one that had gone out of the bundle under Caleb's arm in the middle of the saddle. Everybody already knew about it. Nora had written the note to Ines on Tuesday night. She had been carrying it in her pack for four days. She handed it over on a beach.

Then she got out the six sample jars and wrote the date on all six.

"They're empty," Caleb said.

"They are," said Nora.

Caleb watched her cap the last one. "You carried six empty jars out here twice."

Nora put them back in the sleeve. "I carried them so that if there is ever anything to put in one, there is a jar. You're welcome."

They went up the trail at ten thirty. All of them went. Ines wanted to walk the top before the weather. Gus had finished with the anchor and had nothing else to do.

Caleb was in front. That was how he saw it.

It was strung right across the trail, in the gap between two alders, at the height of his face. The night had been clear and cold. Every drop of dew on the island had gone somewhere, and a lot of it had gone here.

It was a wheel of water. The spokes were bare and nearly invisible. The spiral between them was beaded all the way around. Hundreds of drops, each one holding a little sideways sun.

In the middle of it sat a big brown spider with a white cross on her back. She sat head down. She did not move.

Caleb stopped and put his arm out. Marco walked into his arm.

"What."

"Look," Caleb said.

Marco looked. Then he said, "Okay, that's a good one," in a completely different voice.

"She's had all night on that," Caleb said.

"So what do you want to do about it, Bennett?" Marco said. "It's across the trail."

"We go around," Caleb said.

"It's a spider," said Marco.

"We go around."

Ines came up and looked at the web for about a second. Then she stepped off the trail into the salal on the left. "Around," she said. Marco made a speech on the way past about new rules and who was making them. Then he went around.

Gus went around last. He went around wider than anybody.

The trail came out on top where the wind was, except there was no wind. The summit was the same hilltop of moss and rust Caleb had run onto on Tuesday behind a line of ants. There was still a thread of ants on the trail to the stump at the edge of the clearing. It was thinner than Tuesday, and all of it was going one way now.

And the box was still there.

He had seen it before. That was the thing. He had stood ten feet from it four days ago with his heart going and his head full of ants. He had looked at it the way you look at a parked car. Then he had gone back down.

So this was not finding something. This was coming back to something he had already walked past. That was worse.

Nora had never seen it at all.

She walked around it once before she said anything. It was gray aluminum, about the height of Caleb's knee. It was square, with a door on the front and a padlock through the hasp.

Everything else on that hilltop was going back to the island. The bent post had a collar of rust down one side. The lump of concrete had moss in its cracks. The ring in the top was eaten to lace.

"It's not rusting," Nora said.

"Aluminum doesn't," said Gus.

He had come up behind them with his hands in his pockets. For Gus that was the same as sitting down. He nodded at the box.

"That's from the old program," he said. "Before most of us."

Caleb crouched and looked at the seams. He had nothing else to do. On Tuesday he had not looked.

A black rubber seal ran all the way around the door. Caleb could see the line of it in the gap. It was even the whole way around. It was still soft enough to sit flat.

There was moss in every crack in the concrete four feet away. The seam of this door was clean. The rivets on the hasp had no rust running out from under them. At the bottom a cable came out through a fitting and hung down in a loop before it went into the ground.

The padlock was a combination lock. It had a dial on the face of it with numbers around the edge, and Caleb could read every number from where he was crouched.

He did not know what any of that added up to. He knew what it looked like next to everything around it. It looked cared for.

"Somebody keeps it shut," Caleb said.

Gus took his hands out of his pockets. "Somebody built it right."

Then Nora said, "There's a tag."

It was on the door, low. It was a little rectangle of aluminum held on with two rivets. The letters had been struck into it one at a time with steel punches. That is how a person does it by hand. They did not sit level. Some were deeper than others.

It said D.M. Below that was a year, and the year was from before the twins were born.

Gus put his hand on the padlock. He did not pull it. He thumbed the dial around a turn and let the whole thing drop back against the hasp, the way you check a gate you have checked a thousand times.

"Mercer," he said. "Your name's Bennett, though."

"Our mother's," said Nora.

"Huh," said Gus.

He took his hand off the lock. He went back down the trail to see about the boat, and the top of the island was quiet again.

Caleb watched Gus's back go down between the firs. He waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it.

"Mercer," Marco said, from over by the concrete. He said it the way you repeat a word you have no use for. Then he went back to what he was doing, which was nothing.

"Ten minutes," Ines said, from the trees. "I want to see that sky from the top."

Caleb turned around. Nora was still down on one knee at the tag. She read the year. Then she put her thumb under it and read it again.

She did not say anything at all, and he did not ask her why.

He noticed one more thing before they went down, and then he forgot about it.

All around the base of the box the needles and the moss had drifted up in a little windrow. They do that against anything that sits still outdoors. On one side the windrow was not there. There was a bare patch, pressed flat. It was about the size and shape of a person kneeling.

The weather came in exactly the way Gus had said it would.

The grain in the sky thickened all morning. By eleven it had gone to a sheet. There was a ring around the sun. By noon the ring was gone, because the sun was. The channel lost its shine and went flat gray.

Then the swell came in around the point, before there was any wind at all. Caleb had not known that was a thing that happened. The skiff began to knock against the pull of the anchor line.

Ines looked at all of it. "We're done," she said.

Nobody argued.

On the way down the trail Caleb nearly walked straight into the web. The dew had burned off hours ago and the whole thing had gone invisible. What saved him was the salal. He saw the trodden place where five people had gone around that morning, and he remembered why, about a step and a half before it mattered.

"Around," he said.

"Around," Marco agreed, sadly. "It's a whole thing now. We have a rule."

Back at the station Nora put the file on the lab machine before she took her coat off. She left the laptop on the bench. She wrote the serial number and the time in the log.

Caleb was fairly sure he knew who that was for. Their mother had asked for the file on Tuesday night, in a kitchen, with a spoon in her hand. Nora had been carrying that around too.

The rain started while they were loading the van.

Ines had her own car in the lot, because it was Saturday and the district office was on her way home. So it was the three of them in the van. Marco drove. Caleb got the front seat by being faster than his sister. Ines pulled out behind them and stayed.

The coast road runs south out of Alder Sound with the water on the left and a cut bank on the right. In October it is a bad road in the rain. The alders drop their leaves onto the outside of every bend, and the rain brings the grit down off the bank.

By one in the afternoon the wind had come around. It shoved the van sideways at every gap in the trees. Marco had the wipers on fast and both hands on the wheel. Caleb had never seen that before.

In the back seat, Nora was finishing an argument that was four days old.

"The ants were first," Caleb said.

"The barometer was right," said Nora.

"The ants were right and first."

Nora had her head back against the seat and her eyes shut. "The ants did not tell you a number."

Caleb twisted around in his seat to argue with her face to face. "They told me a direction."

"Uphill is not a number, Caleb."

"Marco. Tell her uphill is a direction."

"Uphill is a direction," Marco said. "I'm driving."

"See," said Caleb.

"That is not," said Nora, "a ruling."

They came around a left-hand bend. There was a gap in the trees on the water side. The oncoming pickup had a boat on a trailer behind it. The boat still had its outboard hanging off the back. Caleb noticed it the way you notice a thing you have spent all morning around.

Coming into the gap the trailer went out once, and came back, and went out further.

Caleb watched it swing across the center line.

Marco said, "Hold on," in an ordinary voice. He braked. He steered right.

The tires let go of the road. It was not like sliding. It was like the road stopped being under them. The van went off the shoulder. The guardrail came up and hit it along the whole right side with a noise like a machine shop. Then the front end went down into the ditch at the foot of the bank. The van went over onto its side and stopped against a tree.

Everything that had been noise stopped.

Caleb was out of his seatbelt. He did not know how. He was up on his knees on what had been the side of the van. He was reaching back between the seats with both hands for his sister. She was not moving. He was saying her name and he could not hear himself saying it. His left hand would not close. He did not notice that either.

Then the dark.

It was not the dark of his eyes being shut. It had weight in it. It had a temperature. It was somewhere.

There was a sound above him and all around him, like rain coming down hard on a drum. It had a beat in it. The same short run of knocks came around and around, over and over, and never once broke step.

Under the sound there were other people. He could not see them. He could feel that they were there.

His hands were on a cable. It was thick as his thumb, and cold, and wet, and shaking under his fingers. Somebody else had hold of it further along and was pulling against him. He was leaning back into it hard enough that his heels were dug in. The ground under his heels was wet. It smelled like turned earth and rain.

A man's voice went up over the drumming. It was hoarse. It had been shouting a long time. It was not shouting at Caleb.

"Get them under. Hold the line."

There was a man's back. It was turning away from him, going toward the sound.

Caleb knew who it was.

He had no reason to know. He had never seen that back in his life. There was one picture of the man in the house. In it he was facing the camera on a dock in the sun, squinting. It had stood on that shelf for as long as Caleb could remember. This was nothing like that.

He knew anyway. Nothing in him had the right to be that certain, and he was.

Then rain on the van.

Rain on the van. Glass in his lap. His left hand lying there wrong. The smell of hot engine and wet leaves came in through where the windshield used to be. He was down in the corner of his own seat with his sister's hair in his hand.

Something was coming toward him out of all that noise. It was a long way off. Then it was not far at all.

Marco was saying his name.
