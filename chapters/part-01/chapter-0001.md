# Chapter 1 — The Bar

At six in the morning the boathouse smelled like diesel and wet rope and the coffee Marco had made too strong on purpose, and Caleb Bennett was losing an argument about a backpack.

"Mine's heavier," he said.

"It isn't," said Nora.

"I've got the water."

"I've got the jars, the stakes, the tape and the spare battery, which weighs as much as a cat." His sister unzipped her jacket and took out a luggage scale. A little black one with a hook, the kind people use at airports. She had brought a luggage scale to work.

Marco Sato, who was twenty-four and drove the van and had so far carried nothing at all, put his coffee down to watch this properly. "Oh, she came prepared," he said. "She came to *end* you."

Nora hooked Caleb's pack and lifted it. "Six point two." She hooked her own. "Six point one."

"Ha," said Caleb.

"A hundred grams. Would you like a medal, or should we just tell people?"

"Loser carries the winner's water all day," said Marco, who had appointed himself referee of a contest nobody had asked him to referee. "That's the rule. I just made it up. It's binding."

"Van," said Dr. Ines Marchetti, from the door, and that was the end of that.

They drove out in the dark with the heater roaring and the packs on their knees, three weeks into a job Caleb still could not believe was a job. Field assistant. Seasonal. Three months. He had got in on a summer of trail crew and a letter from his old biology teacher that he had never been allowed to read. Nora had got in on a spreadsheet nobody had asked for, nine years of the station's bird counts, sent to Ines with a note that said *you may find this useful.* Ines had, apparently, found it useful.

Ines drove and did not waste words on it. Marco talked enough for the van.

"Big day, kids. First crossing on foot. Anybody scared?"

"We're not kids," said Nora.

"You're eighteen. You're technically a rumour."

By the time they reached the car park at the mainland end, the sky had gone the colour of the inside of a shell, and the bar was coming up out of the water.

That was the thing about the bar. It was not there, and then it was. Four hundred metres of gravel, four football fields, straight out from the car park to the island, and for a few hours around low water you could walk it. The tide was still falling and the gravel shone like somebody had polished it, and Tern Island stood at the far end, dark trees on a dark hump, getting bigger.

Ines walked the middle, where the gravel stood up in a dry crest with driftwood along it, and talked over her shoulder. "Stay on the crest. Two-thirds across there's a dip, near the island end. We call it the saddle. That's where the water comes in first. Remember what it looks like dry." She stopped so they could see it: the crest sagged for thirty steps and the gravel there was darker, still wet. "We are off this bar by half past twelve, whatever's finished. Back at the station by one. Questions."

"Is that a question or a statement," said Marco.

"It's a statement."

Caleb had a question, which was whether the smooth grey mud on the sheltered side of the bar would hold a person. He tested it by stepping on it.

It would not.

He went in to the shin with both feet, and when he pulled the first one out the mud kept the boot for a second and let go with a noise like a kiss, and the water ran in over the top. He stood there with his arms out, filthy to the knee at seven in the morning on his first crossing, while Marco laughed so hard he had to hold his knees.

"And Bennett goes down. First ten minutes. Somebody call his mother."

"I'm calling his mother," said Nora, and held up her phone. She had already taken the picture. Caleb saw her thumbs move and knew exactly where it was going.

Ines did not laugh. She waited until he had squelched back onto the crest and said, "Now you know," and walked on. It took Caleb half the bar to work out that she was not annoyed. She was pleased. She had wanted somebody to step in it early, where it was safe.

***

The island at low water was enormous. From the car park it was a hump with trees on it. Up close it kept unfolding: the beach at the island end of the bar, a long line of dark weed and silver logs where the sea had left its laundry; the trail going up through alders into the firs; the spit on the far side, a low gravel finger with pools in it and birds all over it.

Ines split them at the beach. She and Nora went up to the shore logger, a grey box on a post at the top of the lee beach with a wire running down into a pool and a second little box beside it that Nora called *the pressure logger* and Marco called *the other one.* Download the memory, read the trace, swap the battery. Caleb and Marco took the count sheets and the binoculars to the spit.

At the trailhead Marco walked face first into a spider web strung between two alders, still beaded with dew, and did a dance about it that involved the whole trailhead. Caleb went around the next one. It was not that he was scared of spiders. It was that the web had taken all night, and it was the best thing anybody had built on the island.

The count was three stops along the spit, ten minutes each. Marco called the birds and Caleb clicked the counter and wrote them down. Black turnstones, small dark ones that ran about in a gang and made a noise like a rattle. Sanderlings, which ran at the water and away from it and at it again, like they had bet each other. Three fat grey plovers that Marco said were "uncommon here, write that down, Ines will be thrilled," and which were the most relaxed birds Caleb had ever seen. The sun came up properly and the spit warmed, and this was, he thought, the best job in the world.

The pollinator sweep was less of a triumph. You walked the line of flowers along the top of the beach, the yellow sticky ones and the last purple asters, swung the net through them twenty times, and looked in the bag. The first bag had a spider, a grass seed and some dew in it.

"It's early," Marco said. "Nothing's up. Write down zero."

"Zero feels bad."

"Zero is science."

By ten there were things up. A bumblebee, huge and slow. Two striped flies that Caleb logged as *honey bees* until Marco looked in the bag and said, "One pair of wings, Bennett. That's a fly in a bee costume," and Caleb crossed it out and wrote *fly (bee costume)* and felt, for no reason he could explain, wonderful.

At half past ten, which was low water, they ate lunch on a silver log at the island end of the bar, and Caleb put his sandwich down for one second to open his water, and a gull took half of it. Not snatched. Took. It landed, considered, picked up half a sandwich and left, like a man collecting a parcel.

"Hey," Caleb said, to a bird.

"That's his sandwich now," said Marco. "Law of the beach."

Caleb went after the crust the gull had dropped, out of principle, and that was how he came to be kneeling in the weed at the foot of a drift log, and how he saw the ants.

Big ones, black, with legs the colour of rust, coming out of the log. Not wandering. Walking, in a line one or two ants wide, up the slope of the beach and away from the water, and every one going up was carrying something pale and curled, the size of a grain of rice. Some carried grubs as big as themselves, held out in front, like a person carrying a rolled-up carpet.

He lay down on his stomach in the weed without checking what he was lying on, and watched.

The line went up the beach, across the sand, and into the grass at the edge of the trees, and kept climbing. Coming down the same line, a hand's width to the side, was a second line carrying nothing, walking back to the log for more. They did not hurry and they did not stop. One of them was carrying another ant, curled up small underneath her like a sleeping child, and the one being carried did not appear to mind.

"They're moving house," Caleb said. "In the middle of the day. They're taking the babies up the hill."

"That's nice for them," said Marco, from the log.

Caleb got up and followed the line. Up the beach, into the grass, up a bank and onto the trail, where the ants had cut a path of their own across the human one and gone on uphill through the alders.

"Transect's that way," Marco called, pointing the other way with his sandwich.

"I know."

"You're walking the other way."

"Also know that."

He followed them up. The trail climbed through the firs and came out on top, where the wind was and the trees opened, and the ants went to an old stump at the edge of the clearing and poured into it. That was where they were taking the babies. A dry stump on the summit, as far from the water as the island went.

The summit was a hilltop of moss and rust. A bent metal post with nothing on it. A lump of concrete with a rusted ring. And, squatting in the grass, one grey metal box about the height of his knee, square and clean and dry, with a padlock on it, the only thing up there that looked like anyone had touched it since the nineties. Caleb looked at it the way you look at a parked car. Then he looked at the ants some more, because the ants were the interesting thing, and went back down to the transect, which was that way.

He noticed the spit before he understood it.

It was quiet. An hour ago the turnstones had been rattling all along it; now there was nothing but wind. He looked for them and saw them lifting, all together, off the far end of the spit and going away downwind over the water, low and fast, and not coming back. The sanderlings were gone. The plovers were gone. The pools at the tip of the spit, half empty at lunch, were full, and water was running up into the weed line in long thin sheets that did not slide back as far as they had come.

The wind had turned. He put his face into it. South now, with weight in it, smelling of rain that had not arrived.

He looked up the beach, and the ants were still going up the hill.

Something in his stomach went cold and stayed cold. It was not a fact. It was the feeling you get when everyone in a room has stopped talking and you were the one not paying attention.

"Marco. Where's Ines?"

"Up at the logger. Why?"

"The birds have gone."

"Birds do that."

"It's not time for them to do that."

He was walking, and then running, up the beach toward the trail, and he met Nora coming down it with the station's little laptop open in one arm like a tray and the look on her face that meant a number would not behave.

"Where's Ines," she said.

"Where's Ines," he said.

"She went to look at the saddle. Caleb, listen. The water this morning, at low, was sitting fifteen centimetres higher than the table says it should have been. Same minute. It's on the trace. And the pressure's dropped four in the last hour." She turned the screen so he could see a line going down like a cliff. "Four in an hour is a lot. And the wind's gone round."

"The birds have gone," Caleb said. "All of them, together, just now. The pools are filling and it's not time. And the ants have been moving the babies up the hill since breakfast."

They looked at each other.

"Your ants," Nora said slowly, "and my numbers agree."

"Yeah."

"That's not a sentence I thought I'd say today."

Ines was on the crest at the island end of the bar, looking at the saddle, with her radio already in her hand. They both talked at once. She held up one finger. There was a short silent fight about who would go first, which Nora won because she had the laptop. Caleb hated it. He waited until she had finished and then said, "And the ants," like a footnote.

Ines did not say *you may be right.* She took the laptop and read the trace herself, one line at a time. Then she looked at the sky, and at the spit where the birds were not, and at the saddle, where there was still nothing but wet gravel, and pressed the button on the radio.

"Gus. Ines. What's the wind doing at the station."

A man's voice came out of it, slow and old and completely unbothered. "Gone south on me the last half hour. Freshening. Glass is dropping. You want the boat?"

"Stand by with it. Don't launch." She let go of the button. "Front's come in early," she said. "Six hours early. The table's not wrong. It's just not today's table." She looked at her watch. "Ten past eleven. We were walking at half twelve. We're walking now. Battery stays. Count stays. Marco, packs."

"Ninety minutes," said Nora, who could not help it.

"Ninety minutes is margin. I don't spend margin. Move."

It was surprising how fast three weeks of being new turned into knowing what to do. Nora put the count sheets, the laptop and the printed table into the dry bag and sealed it, then took all the wet things, the gloves and the dripping net bag, into her own pack so nothing with writing on it would get wet. Marco took two packs and put one on backwards. Caleb got the net poles and the bundle of stakes, the long awkward things, and went to the front, because Ines pointed at him and said, "You walked the crest this morning. You know where the soft is. Go first with the pole. Say what you're standing on."

The crest was dry all the way to the saddle. At the saddle there was water on the gravel now, thin as a window, sliding in from the windward side and across to the other, and the gravel under it was ticking. The whole bar was ticking and bubbling like something cooking, as the sea came up through the stones from underneath.

"Shin," Caleb said. "Firm. Stones. Keep to the right of me, the left goes soft."

"Eleven fifty-two," said Nora behind him, in a voice a little higher than she probably wanted. "Shin."

The water was cold enough to hurt his teeth through his boots. It pulled at the pole sideways, because the two sides of the bar were filling at different speeds and the water on the saddle was going somewhere. A stone rolled under his foot; he put the pole down hard and it held. One stake slid out of the bundle under his arm and went away across the water, turning, and he let it go, because Ines had said the thing about margin.

Marco had stopped talking. That was how Caleb knew it was real. For thirty steps there was no commentary at all, only breathing and the tick of the gravel and Nora saying "Shin. Eleven fifty-four," and then the ground came up under his boots and he was on the crest on the mainland side, and the saddle, when he turned to look at it, was already a different colour.

They came off the bar onto the car park wet to the shins and stood there, and then somebody laughed, and then all of them were laughing, the stupid helpless kind that arrives thirty seconds after being scared. Marco sat down in a puddle on purpose. Nora leaned on the van with the dry bag against her chest like a baby.

Ines came last, walking, not wet above the ankle, and went past them to the driver's door.

"Good catch," she said. "Both of you."

"It was the ants," said Caleb.

"It was the pressure," said Nora.

"It was both," said Ines, "which is why I'm keeping you," and got in the van.

***

In the van, with the heater roaring and the smell of wet everything, Nora took out the luggage scale.

"No," said Caleb.

"Rules are rules. Marco made them."

"They're binding," said Marco, from the front.

She hooked his pack. "Five point nine." She hooked her own, and it took two hands. "Six point five."

"That's water. That's the gloves and the net. You took all the wet stuff. That's not the pack, that's the sea."

"I said heavier."

"You said heavier this *morning*, when it wasn't."

"I said heavier," said Nora, "and it's heavier. I didn't say when. I didn't say why. I said."

"You said," said Caleb.

"Four hundred grams," said Marco. "Bennett loses to a wet glove."

Ines drove, and said nothing at all, and Caleb was fairly sure, from the back of her head, that she was smiling.
