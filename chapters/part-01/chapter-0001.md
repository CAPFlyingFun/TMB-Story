# Chapter 1 — The Wet Engine

The little motor on the test barrel had a note to it, and the note was wrong.

Caleb Bennett stood with one hand on the cowling and listened. Not to the exhaust, which was loud and stupid and told you nothing. To the tone underneath it, the one you felt in your palm more than heard. A healthy outboard idling in a drum of water sounded like a sewing machine. This one sounded like a sewing machine with a cough it was trying to hide.

He thumbed the throttle up a hair. The tell-tale, the thin jet of cooling water that spat from the side of the housing, ran steady. It was not the water. He thumbed the throttle down and the cough came back.

"Thermostat," he told the motor. "Don't argue."

Above him, under the eave of the yard office, the wasps were doing nothing at all.

The nest was a grey comb the size of his open hand, hung on a single stalk in the corner where the eave met the wall. Open cells, most of them empty now. A dozen wasps stood on its face in the cool and did not move. Once in a while one of them walked to the edge, thought about it, and dropped into a slow flight with its long back legs trailing under it like landing gear. October had most of them beaten. The first hard frost would finish the job.

He had been meaning to deal with it since August. He had said so at dinner more than once. Somebody had left a note on the office door about it in September, in the kind of handwriting that expected to be obeyed. He had taken the note down and put it in his pocket and gone back to work.

A pickup rolled through the gate and backed toward the wash bay, too fast, and he shut the barrel motor down, lifted it off onto the rack, and went to meet it.

The man who got out was fifty or so, with the forearms of somebody who had been strong once and had let it go. He had a small outboard in the bed under a blue tarp. When he pulled the tarp off, the motor was still wet.

"Stalled on me off the point," the man said, before Caleb had asked anything. "This morning, first light. Following sea. Took a wave over the back end and she wouldn't restart. I've got a nephew coming Saturday and I promised him fishing." The words went up at the end, a half-step, the way a man's did when he was asking himself the same question. He looked at the motor and not at Caleb. "So how do you write something like that up?"

Caleb lifted it out of the bed himself. Six horse, pull-start, older than he was. Light enough to swing over the tailgate one-handed. He set it on the stand by the barrel and popped the cowling, and the smell of the bottom came up with it, old seawater and harbour mud.

Fine grey silt lay inside the cover in a layer you could write your name in. There was more of it packed into the water intake, and a green thread of weed in the prop. Waves carried spray. Bottoms carried mud.

He said nothing. He took the pull-cord handle and drew it out.

It came a hand's width and stopped dead, like it had hit a wall. He let it go back and pulled again. Same spot. Same wall.

That settled it, and he stood for a second with the handle in his fingers letting it settle. A wave over the back of the boat wet the plug and drowned the fuel side; the engine coughed and died and would not catch, but the rope still came out free. The rope only locked like this when water had filled the cylinder from below. When the whole motor had gone under and stayed there long enough for the sea to climb back up the exhaust.

"Wave came right over." The man was looking at the motor.

"Mm." Caleb found the plug wrench on the bench without looking, fitted it, and backed the spark plug out. Water beaded on the tip. The metal around the thread had already gone the colour of a rusted nail. Rust wanted hours. It did not want a morning.

He turned the plug in the light so the man could see it if he wanted to, and then he looked at the man's right hand, which was resting on the tailgate. There was a red groove worn across the inside of the fingers where a cord handle had been yanked, and yanked, and yanked.

So the man had known. He had pulled that rope fifty times with the cylinder full and the rope not giving, and he had known where the water came from, and he had driven here anyway with a story.

Caleb did not care about the story. He cared about whether the boat was still sitting in the water.

He set the plug on the bench. "Here's the thing," he said, to the motor. "Wave this morning, I drain her and blow her out, and you're fishing Saturday. Under for a day or two and cranked wet, I've got to go through the whole motor tonight or she's scrap by Monday." He wiped his hands on the rag at his belt. "Which motor did you bring me?"

He let it sit. He did not look up. Above the office door one of the wasps let go of the comb and drifted down to the puddle the barrel had slopped under the stand and stood at the edge of it, drinking.

The man let out a breath through his nose.

"She sank at the slip," he said. "All that rain last week. I hadn't been down to check her." He rubbed his face. "Found her Sunday with the gunwale under. Hauled her, pulled the motor, and I've been yanking that cord on my own dock for two days like an idiot. It's my brother-in-law's. I told him I'd been keeping an eye on it."

"Boat's out of the water now?"

"On the trailer. Bailed."

"Then that's the important part." Caleb picked the plug back up. "I'll write the ticket the way the plug says. What you tell your brother-in-law is your business."

The man looked at him properly for the first time. "You're Rachel Bennett's boy."

"Half of them."

"Mercer, wasn't it. Your dad. I knew him to nod at, down at the fuel dock." He said it the way people in Port Alder said it, carefully, like stepping onto a float that might not hold. "That was a hard thing."

"Research trip," Caleb said. "The boat was lost off the coast. Nobody was ever found. Eighteen years ago." The words came out in their order the way they always did, and he was already reaching for the oil can, because the words did not need him for anything. "She'll run. Come by tomorrow after four."

"You and your sister," the man said, easier now, the way people got once the hard thing was behind them. "You've got that twin thing, I bet."

"Nope," Caleb said, and pulled the plug wrench off the bench.

He texted his mother that he would be late, and then he pulled the cord with the plug out and a fan of grey seawater shot out of the plug hole and across the wash bay, and the man, who had stayed to watch, laughed once out loud without meaning to.

It took the rest of the light and then some. Oil down the hole, cord pulled slow until the fan was a spit and the spit was nothing. Plug dried and gapped. The fuel bowl off and the milky water poured out of it, and the bowl dried, and back on. The intake picked clean and the weed cut off the prop. The gear oil drained, and what came out was the colour of coffee with too much cream in it, which was a different problem and an older one: fresh oil would hold it to Saturday, and the seal could wait for the order. Crows went over toward wherever crows went at dusk, and the yard lights came on. By the time he clamped the motor onto the barrel and pulled, it was dark, and a wind had come up off the water, and the halyards on the boats up on stands had started tapping against the masts, a rhythm with no pattern to it.

The motor caught on the fourth pull, ran rough, ran rougher, and cleared its throat, and settled into something that was almost a sewing machine. The tell-tale came on strong.

He stood and listened to it for a minute with his palm on the cowling. There was something in there, deep, that he did not like. Two days of salt had been in there, and salt did not stop working because you had put oil on it.

"I'll know when you've run a while," he told it, and shut it down.

He looked up on his way out. The wasps were all on the comb again, every one of them parked, not one moving, like a clock somebody had stopped. The cells of the nest in the yard light were small and exact, each one a doorway with nothing behind it.

He left them there and walked home up the hill.

***

The kitchen smelled of onions and the good kind of burning. His mother was at the counter with her back to the door and a sheet of paper in her hands, and by the time he had his boots half off she had folded it in three and slid it into the back pocket of her jeans and turned around with a spoon.

"You smell like a marina," Rachel Bennett said.

"I am a marina." He kissed the side of her head and went to the sink. "Where's the other one?"

"Setting the table. Allegedly."

"Present," said Nora, from the table, where she was sitting with a fork in her hand and nothing set. She had her laptop open next to her plate, which was a thing their mother had given up on in tenth grade. "You're late."

"Drowned outboard."

"Whose fault?"

"Owner's."

"Was it," she said, and went back to the screen.

He dried his hands. The shelf by the kitchen door held the things that had always been on it: a jar of pens, a dish of keys, a cracked ceramic gull one of them had made in second grade, and a photograph in a wooden frame of a man on a dock in a green jacket, squinting. Caleb's eyes went to the gull and the keys and the pens, and slid past the frame the way a boat slides past a piling, near enough to touch, not touching, and came back to the table.

Rachel put the pot down between them. She still had her work fleece on, the one with the little embroidered Tidewater Instruments logo on the chest, which meant she had come straight from the bench and started cooking without sitting down. She looked tired and fine. Mom was fine.

"So," she said. "The drowned outboard."

He told it while they ate. He told it the way he had seen it: the silt, the cord hitting the wall, the plug with the rust on it, the groove across the man's fingers, and the way the man's voice had gone up half a step when he said *this morning*, which he had noticed and put away until the cord agreed with it.

Nora listened with her head tilted, the way she did when she was rebuilding a thing from the other end.

"So the story was impossible before he opened his mouth," she said.

"The story was fine. It's a good story. Boats get pooped all the time."

"A wave puts water in the top. He had water in the bottom. Water in the wrong chamber for the story." She shrugged, one shoulder. "You didn't need his voice. You needed the rope."

"I had the rope. I also had the voice." He pointed his fork at her. "You'd have stood there with the rope and made him wait while you pulled up last week's rainfall."

"I'd have been right."

"You'd have been right at midnight."

Rachel reached across both of them for the bread. "Eat."

They ate. Nora shut the laptop halfway, which was her version of manners.

"He knew Dad," Caleb said, after a while. "To nod at. The fuel dock."

His mother tore a piece of bread in half. "Lots of people did."

"He said the thing. So I said the thing."

"Research trip," Rachel said. "The boat was lost off the coast. Nobody was ever found. Eighteen years ago." She put the bread on his plate. "That's the whole of it. Pass your sister the salt."

He passed his sister the salt.

"He also," Caleb said, "asked if we had the twin thing."

Nora put the salt down. "Of course he did."

Their mother looked between them. "What did you tell him?"

"I told him no."

"That is not the answer."

They looked at each other across the table. It was a thing they had been doing since they were nine, before anything stupid, together.

"One," Nora said.

"Two," Caleb said.

"We built it the hard way," they said together, flat, in the same breath, and then sat there refusing to look at each other while their mother laughed into her hand.

"That does not help your case," Rachel said.

"It's not a case." Nora picked the fork back up. "It's a fact. People think it comes free. It didn't come free. It came from him breaking my arm on the trampoline and me not telling anyone for two days."

"You had a bet on."

"I had a bet on."

Rachel wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and got up for the water jug, and the kitchen was warm, and the window over the sink had gone black and showed the three of them back to themselves, and Caleb thought that this was fine, all of it.

"Tomorrow," he said, stretching until his back cracked, "I am dealing with those wasps."

Nora did not look up from her plate.

"One," she said.

He opened his mouth. He closed it.

"Two," he said.

"That's the fourth time we've counted for the wasps," his sister said, and ate her bread.
