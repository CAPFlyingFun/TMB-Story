# Chapter 1 — The Wet Engine

Caleb Bennett could tell what was wrong with an engine by listening to it, the same way you can tell somebody is sad by the way they say "I'm fine."

The little motor clamped to the test barrel was saying "I'm fine" right now. It was lying.

"Thermostat," Caleb told it. "Don't argue with me."

The motor argued. It coughed, once, like a dog that has swallowed a sock, and then went back to pretending. Caleb patted the cover. He would get to it. First he looked up, because looking up was a habit, and above the office door the wasps were having a very slow afternoon.

They lived in a grey paper nest the size of his open hand, hung from one little stalk in the corner where the roof met the wall. In August there had been dozens of them, busy as a kitchen. Now, in October, there were maybe twelve, and they stood on the face of their nest like old men on a porch, not saying much. Every so often one of them would walk to the edge, think about it, and drop off into a slow, wobbly flight with its long back legs dangling underneath, like an airplane that had forgotten to put its wheels up.

The biggest one Caleb called the Mayor. The Mayor never flew anywhere. The Mayor supervised.

Caleb had been meaning to deal with the nest since August. He had said so at dinner more than once. Somebody had even left a note about it on the office door in September, in the kind of handwriting that expected to be obeyed. Caleb had taken the note down, folded it into his pocket, and gone back to work. The first hard frost would take care of the wasps for free. Until then, he did not see why anybody had to be the bad guy.

A pickup truck came through the gate too fast and backed toward the wash bay. Caleb shut the barrel motor off, lifted it onto the rack, and went to see what the day had brought him.

What it had brought him was a man of about fifty with a sunburned neck and a small outboard motor in the back of his truck under a blue tarp. When the man whipped the tarp off, the motor was still wet.

"Stalled on me off the point," the man said, before Caleb had asked a single thing. "This morning, first light. I'm sitting there, the sea's coming up behind me, and a wave comes right over the back of the boat. Right over. And she won't start after that. I've got my nephew coming Saturday and I promised him fishing." He looked at the motor and not at Caleb. "So how would you write something like that up?"

It was a good story. Caleb liked it. He especially liked how the man's voice had climbed a little at the end, the way a voice does when it is asking itself a question.

He lifted the motor out of the truck himself. Six horsepower, pull-start, older than he was, light enough to swing over the tailgate one-handed. He set it on the stand and popped the cover off, and the smell that came out was not the smell of a wave. It was the smell of the bottom of the harbour. Old salt water and mud.

Inside the cover was a layer of fine grey silt you could write your name in. There was more of it packed into the water intake, and a green thread of seaweed wound around the propeller.

Waves carry spray. Only the bottom carries mud.

Caleb did not say that. He took hold of the starter rope and pulled.

The rope came out the width of his hand and stopped dead, like it had hit a wall. He let it back. He pulled again. Same spot. Same wall.

Here is the thing about a wave. A wave gets a motor wet on the top. It coughs, it dies, it sulks, but the rope still pulls free. A rope only stops like that when the engine is full of water from the inside. And an engine only fills from the inside when the whole thing has gone under and stayed under long enough for the sea to climb up into it and make itself at home.

"Right over the back," the man said again, to nobody.

"Mm," said Caleb. He found the plug wrench on the bench without looking, and backed out the spark plug. Water beaded on the tip. The metal around the thread had already turned the orange-brown of a rusty nail. Rust takes hours. It does not take a morning.

He turned the plug in the light so the man could see it if he felt like seeing it. Then he looked at the man's right hand, resting on the tailgate. There was a red groove worn across the inside of the fingers, the kind you get from yanking a starter rope that will not give. Not once or twice. Fifty times. A hundred.

So the man already knew. He had known before he drove in. He had just been hoping, the way people do, that the story would be true if he told it to somebody who looked like he might believe it.

Caleb did not mind the story. The story was not his problem. His problem was whether the boat was still sitting at the bottom of the harbour.

He set the plug down. "Here's the thing," he said, to the motor, because it was easier on everybody if he said it to the motor. "Wave this morning, I drain her out and you're fishing Saturday. Under water for a couple of days and cranked wet, I have to go through the whole motor tonight or she's scrap by Monday." He wiped his hands on his rag. "So. Which motor did you bring me?"

Then he waited. He did not look up. Above the office door one of the wasps let go of the nest and drifted down to the puddle the test barrel had slopped on the concrete, and stood at the edge of it, having a drink.

The man let out a long breath through his nose.

"She sank at the dock," he said. "All that rain last week. I hadn't been down to check on her. Found her Sunday with the water up over the seats." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I hauled her out, pulled the motor, and I've been standing on my own dock yanking that rope for two days like an idiot. It's my brother-in-law's boat. I told him I was keeping an eye on it."

"Is the boat out of the water now?"

"On the trailer. Bailed."

"Then that's the important part." Caleb picked the plug back up. "I'll write the ticket the way the engine tells it. What you tell your brother-in-law is between you and him."

The man looked at him properly for the first time. Something in his shoulders came down about an inch.

"You're Rachel Bennett's boy."

"Half of them."

"Mercer, wasn't it. Your dad." He said it carefully, the way people in Port Alder always said it, like stepping onto a dock that might not hold. "I knew him to nod at, down at the fuel dock. That was a hard thing."

"Research trip," Caleb said. "The boat was lost off the coast. Nobody was ever found. Eighteen years ago." The words came out in their usual order, the way they always did, and he was already reaching for the oil can, because the words did not need him for anything. "She'll run. Come by tomorrow after four."

"You and your sister," the man said, easier now, the way people get once the hard part is behind them. "You've got that twin thing, I bet. Finish each other's sentences."

"Nope," said Caleb.

He texted his mother that he would be late. Then he braced the motor, took hold of the rope with the plug still out, and pulled.

A fan of grey harbour water shot out of the plug hole, sailed clean across the wash bay, and hit the man's truck.

"There's your wave," Caleb said.

The man laughed, one bark, out loud, before he could stop himself. Then he got in his wet truck and drove home.

It took the rest of the daylight and a good bit of the dark. Oil down the hole. Rope pulled slow, again and again, until the fan of water was a spit and the spit was nothing. Plug dried off. The fuel bowl off and the milky water poured out of it. The intake picked clean and the seaweed cut off the propeller. Crows went over the yard toward wherever crows go at dusk, and the yard lights buzzed on, and a wind came up off the water and set every loose rope on every boat tapping against its mast, tick, tick, tick, with no rhythm at all.

When he finally clamped the motor onto the barrel and pulled, it caught on the fourth try, ran rough, ran rougher, cleared its throat, and settled into a hum like a sewing machine. The little jet of cooling water came on strong.

Caleb stood with his palm on the cover and listened. Somewhere deep inside there was still something he did not like. Two days of salt had been in there, and salt does not stop working just because you have covered it in oil.

"I'll know when you've run a while," he told the motor, and shut it off.

On his way out he looked up. The wasps were all back on the nest now, every single one, not one of them moving, like a clock somebody had stopped. In the yard light the little cells of the nest were neat and exact, each one a tiny doorway with nothing behind it.

"Night, Mayor," Caleb said, and walked home up the hill.

***

The kitchen smelled like onions and the good kind of burning.

His mother was at the counter with her back to the door and a sheet of paper in her hands, and by the time Caleb had one boot off she had folded it in three and slid it into the back pocket of her jeans and turned around holding a spoon.

"You smell like a marina," said Rachel Bennett.

"I am a marina." He kissed the side of her head and went to the sink. "Where's the other one?"

"Setting the table. Allegedly."

"Present," said Nora, from the table, where she was sitting with a fork in one hand and absolutely nothing set. Her laptop was open beside her plate, which was a battle their mother had lost back in the tenth grade. "You're late."

"Drowned outboard."

"Whose fault?"

"Owner's."

"It always is," said Nora, and went back to her screen.

Caleb dried his hands. The shelf by the kitchen door held the same things it had held his whole life: a jar of pens, a dish of keys, a lumpy clay seagull one of them had made in second grade, and a photograph in a wooden frame of a man on a dock in a green jacket, squinting. Caleb's eyes went to the pens, and the keys, and the seagull, and slid right past the frame the way a boat slides past a piling. Close enough to touch. Not touching. Then he sat down.

Rachel set the pot in the middle of the table. She still had her work fleece on, the navy one with *Tidewater Instruments* stitched over the heart, which meant she had come straight from the bench to the stove without sitting down in between. She looked tired and she looked fine. Mom was fine.

"So," she said. "The drowned outboard."

He told it while they ate, and he told it the way he had seen it: the mud, the rope hitting the wall, the rusty plug, the groove across the man's fingers, the way the man's voice had climbed a little when he said *this morning*, which Caleb had noticed and put in his pocket until the rope agreed with him.

Nora listened with her head tilted, the way she always did when she was building a thing back up from the other end.

"So the story was impossible," she said, "before he even opened his mouth."

"The story was fine. It was a good story."

"A wave gets a motor wet on top. His was wet on the bottom. Water in the wrong place for the story." One shoulder went up. "You didn't need his voice. You needed the rope."

"I had the rope. I also had the voice." He pointed his fork at her. "You'd have stood there with that rope and made the poor man wait while you looked up last week's rainfall."

"And I'd have been right."

"You'd have been right at midnight."

Rachel reached across both of them for the bread. "Eat."

They ate. Nora closed her laptop halfway, which was her idea of manners.

"He knew Dad," Caleb said after a while. "To nod at. Down at the fuel dock."

His mother tore a piece of bread in half. "Lots of people did."

"He said the thing. So I said the thing."

"Research trip," Rachel said. "The boat was lost off the coast. Nobody was ever found. Eighteen years ago." She put the bread on his plate. "That's the whole of it. Pass your sister the salt."

He passed his sister the salt.

"He also," Caleb said, "asked if we had the twin thing."

Nora put the salt down. "Of course he did."

Their mother looked from one of them to the other. "And what did you tell him?"

"I told him no."

"That is *not* the answer."

They looked at each other across the table. It was a thing they had been doing since they were nine years old, right before doing anything stupid, together.

"One," said Nora.

"Two," said Caleb.

"We built it the hard way," they said, at the exact same moment, in the exact same flat voice, and then sat there refusing to look at each other while their mother laughed into her hand.

"That does not help your case," Rachel said.

"It's not a case." Nora picked her fork back up. "It's a fact. People think it comes free. It did not come free. It came from him breaking my arm on the trampoline and me not telling anyone for two days."

"You had a bet on."

"I had a bet on."

Rachel wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and got up for the water jug. The window over the sink had gone black and showed the three of them back to themselves, warm and small and lit up, and Caleb thought that this was fine. All of it.

"Tomorrow," he said, stretching until his back cracked, "I am dealing with those wasps."

Nora did not look up from her plate.

"One," she said.

He opened his mouth. He closed it again.

"Two," he said.

"That's the fourth time we've counted for the wasps," his sister said, and ate her bread.

"They're good wasps."

"You named them."

"I did not name them."

"You named the big one."

Caleb looked at the black window for a while.

"The Mayor," he said, "has been very reasonable."
