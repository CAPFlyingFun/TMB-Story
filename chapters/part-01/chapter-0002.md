# Chapter 2 — Sixty-Two Grams of Salt

Marco let them out at the bottom of the hill at a quarter to seven, because the van did not do the hill in the wet and nobody had ever asked it to.

"Out," he said, and leaned across to open Nora's door for her, which he had never done before. "Eight o'clock Saturday. Gus is running you out in the skiff. Ines wants the morning. Wear the boots."

"We only have these boots," said Caleb, from the back, in a voice that had been asleep until a moment ago.

"Then wear them wetter."

The van went away down the road with one tail light, and the twins stood at the bottom of their own hill with a pack each and looked up it.

It was not a big hill. It was a hill you did not notice on any other day. Tonight it had grown. The first rain of the front that had chased them off the bar was arriving in small handfuls, and the firs at the top were making the sound firs make when they are about to make a bigger one.

"Race you," said Caleb, and did not move.

"No."

"Good."

They went up together, slowly, the way you carry a full cup.

The afternoon had gone the way afternoons at the station went, only wetter. Nora had typed up the count as far as it had got and put the sheets in Ines's tray. Caleb had hosed the poles. Ines had written the day into the station's log in four lines, and in the box for cause Caleb had written *ants*, in pencil, when she was out of the room, and when she came back and read it she had looked at him for a second and let it stand. Then she had looked at the gloves and the net bag steaming gently in the corner of the lab under the skylight that leaked, and said, "Take those home. Dry them properly. Bring them back Saturday," and that was why Nora's pack, going up the hill, still smelled of low tide.

The porch light was on. The kitchen light was on. Their mother was home, then, which she often was not at seven, and Nora, who could not help it, filed that too.

Nora went in first. She always went in first. Caleb liked to arrive; Nora liked to have arrived.

Her mother was in the front hall. She was standing at the shelf by the door, where the keys lived and the photograph lived, with a sheet of paper in her hands, and as the door opened she folded it in three, quickly and neatly, the way you fold a letter that came in an envelope, and slid it into the back pocket of her jeans. Then she turned round with her whole face already smiling.

"Look at the state of you."

"Mom," said Nora, and let the pack down.

"Look at the *state* of you." Her mother took Nora's face in both hands, which were warm and smelled of onions, and turned it to the light, and kissed her on the forehead, and then looked past her to the door. "Where's the other one."

"Coming."

Caleb came in behind her, dripping, and saw nothing but his mother and the smell of dinner. He put his pack down on top of Nora's. "I fell in," he said, before anyone could say it for him.

"I know you fell in. I have a photograph." Their mother held him off at arm's length and looked him up and down like a job. "Boots off. Both of you. Not on the mat, on the step. Then the wet things go straight in the machine and you go straight in the shower, and I don't want to see either of you again until you're a colour."

"Which colour," said Caleb.

"Any colour that isn't that one."

She was still in the Tidewater fleece, zipped to the chin, straight from the bench. She had not changed. Nora noticed that the way she noticed the time on a clock she was not looking at.

Half an hour later, clean, in dry clothes, with her hair wet down her back, Nora stood on the back step in the dark with the outside tap running and did the boots.

You did not just leave field boots. The mud on Caleb's had dried grey and cracked like a riverbed and had to be knocked off on the edge of the step first, and then the boots went under the tap, and then the laces came out, and then the boots got stuffed with newspaper so they would dry into the shape of feet and not the shape of a boot that had given up. Caleb stood at her shoulder with the gloves and held the torch, mostly on her hands and sometimes, when he forgot, on the firs.

The gloves got rinsed and wrung. The net bag got shaken out over the step, and a great deal of Tern Island came out of it, weed and grit and a small dead fly in a bee costume that Caleb picked up and looked at for longer than a person should look at a dead fly.

"Put it down."

"He was my friend."

"Put your friend down and bring the bag in."

In the kitchen it was warm, and the window over the sink had gone black and turned into a mirror, so that when Nora hung the net bag and the gloves over the heater under it she was hanging them in front of a second kitchen where a second mother stirred a second pot. Their mother did not turn round.

"Talk," she said, to the pot. "Both of you. From the beginning."

That was when Nora looked at Caleb, and Caleb looked at Nora, and she held up one finger and then two, low, against her leg, where it was a thing nobody else in the world would see.

The two-count was old. Older than school. You counted to two before you did anything stupid together, and on two you each said what you were going to do, and if you said the same thing, it was decided, and if you did not, you did not do it. It had kept them out of the river when they were seven. Tonight, on two, they both said, "Fun one," at exactly the same time, and their mother, who had not turned round, said, "I heard that."

So they told her the fun one.

Caleb did the talking, because Caleb was the talking. He did the mud, with actions. He did Marco laughing so hard he had to hold his knees. He did the gull, which had grown since lunch and was now roughly the size of a dog and had looked him in the eye. Their mother laughed, properly, the laugh she had when she had forgotten to be tired, and had to put the spoon down.

"And then the ants," Caleb said. "Mom. Big ones. Black, with rusty legs. They were coming out of this log at the top of the beach in a line, and they were all *carrying* something. Babies. Little white ones, like rice. Some of them were carrying other ants. Just picked them up and carried them. And they went up the beach and up the hill, all the way to the top, to a stump, and they went in. They were moving house. In the middle of the day."

"Why," said their mother.

"Because the log was wet. Underneath. It was soaking, higher up than it should've been, and I think they knew before anybody."

"He lay down in the weed," said Nora. "On his stomach. He followed them up a hill. Marco had to shout."

"I could hear Marco fine."

"He shouted anyway."

Their mother had turned round now, with the spoon, and was looking at Caleb the way she looked at him when he was small and had brought something in from the garden in his hands. She pointed the spoon at the table. "Plates."

Nora did the numbers while Caleb did the plates, because the numbers were hers and Caleb would have got them wrong. The low water fifteen centimetres above the table, same minute. The pressure dropping four in an hour, the line on the screen going down like a cliff. The wind gone round to the south with weight in it. She said it plainly, as if it were a report, because it had been.

Her mother listened to all of it with her back to the pot. That was the first thing. She did not say *goodness* or *my girl* or any of the things she said when Nora explained a spreadsheet. She listened like a person at a bench.

"What did the gauge read at the low," she said. "The number. Not the table, the number."

Nora told her.

"Step, or ramp?"

"Sorry?"

"The pressure. When it dropped. Did it go down in a step, or did it slide? A step is an instrument. A ramp is weather."

"Ramp," said Nora, slowly. "Steady. Cliff, but a smooth cliff."

"Then it was weather." Her mother nodded once, to herself, and turned back to the pot, and then, without turning round again, "And did you get the file off it, or just the screen?"

"The screen. Ines called it before I'd finished. It's still on the unit."

"Get it Saturday."

That was three questions about a logger, Nora thought, and none yet about the saddle. She thought it the way she thought most things, in a column, and did not say it, and a moment later her mother put the spoon down and turned round properly and said, "And you crossed in *that*," in her real voice, and came and put a hand flat on the top of Nora's wet head and left it there, and the column closed.

"Ankle deep," said Nora, under the hand. "Then shin. Caleb went first with a pole and called the line. It was fine."

"It was fine," said Caleb, from the plates, "and it was also the best day of my life."

"Those can both be true," said their mother. "Sit."

They did not sit. They were not finished. Caleb got the sample jars out of Nora's pack, six little glass jars that had been carried to Tern Island and back and never opened, and set them on the table in a row, and Nora got the pencil, because you labelled a jar in pencil on a slip that went inside, since ink lifted and tape came off. Site. Stop. Date. Initials. She wrote *Sat* on all six and left the date blank, because a thing was not true until it was true.

It was while the jars were drying upside down on the towel that she weighed the net bag.

Nobody had asked her to. The net bag was hanging over the heater, dry now, stiff, with a white bloom of salt in every seam, and it smelled like low water warmed up, and Nora took it down and folded it and set it on her mother's kitchen scale, the little flat one that read in grams, and then took her kit sheet out of the front pocket of her pack.

"Oh no," said Caleb. "She's got the sheet."

The sheet was every piece of kit she carried, with its dry weight, in a column, because she had weighed everything on her first day on this exact scale, and nobody had asked her to do that either.

"Net bag. Three forty, dry." She read the scale. "Four oh two." She looked up. "Sixty-two grams of salt."

"Sand," said Caleb.

"Sand falls out. I shook it. Salt stays."

"Thirty-five grams to the litre, love." Their mother did not look up from the pot. "You'd need two litres of seawater held in a mesh bag to leave that behind, and you didn't dip it in the sea, you dragged it through wet weed. That's sand, and grit, and half a plant. Salt's ten of that. If you're lucky."

There was a pause, in which Nora did the sum and found it was right.

"Sixty-two grams of salt," she said again, because she liked it, and put the bag back on the heater.

"That," said Caleb, "is the most Nora thing that has ever happened, and I include the luggage scale."

Then they sat, because the food was on the table and their mother had used the voice, and for a while nobody said anything that was not about bread.

It was a small kitchen and the table was against the wall, and from where Nora sat she could see through the door into the front hall, to the shelf by the door, where the keys lived and the photograph lived. The photograph had been there for as long as she had been anywhere. A man on a dock, in a green jacket, squinting into sun, with a boat's rail behind him. Nobody looked at it. You looked at the keys. Her eye did what it always did, which was slide from the door to the keys to the coat hooks without stopping in between, and she thought nothing, because there was nothing to think.

"Somebody at the station," said their mother, tearing bread, "told me you two have that twin thing."

"Who," said Nora.

"Somebody. When I was out doing their units in August." Their mother waved the bread, vaguely, at the whole of Alder Sound. Tidewater did the station's loggers. It was how the twins had heard about the jobs. "That you finish each other's—"

"—sentences," said Caleb, and then looked annoyed with himself, because it was the one thing he could not stop doing when he was annoyed.

"We built it the hard way," said Nora, flat, to her plate.

"She bit me," said Caleb. "Twice."

"Once," said their mother. "The second time was you." And she was smiling, because she had been there, and for a moment the whole table was the same warm thing.

Then Caleb, with his mouth full, out of nowhere, in the way that was the whole of Caleb: "Did Dad ever work out there?"

The moth arrived at the window at the same moment, which was nothing, a fat brown moth banging at the glass, so that Nora looked at the moth and not at her mother, and heard rather than saw the fork go down.

"Out where."

"The station. Alder Sound. There's a corridor with all these photos, going back forever, people on boats, and it's the kind of place— I just wondered. If he was ever in one."

Her mother put the bread down. She put her hands flat on the table, one each side of her plate, and looked at Caleb, and her face was kind, and it was closed, the way a door can be both.

"Your father did research," she said. "He went out on a research trip. The boat was lost off the coast. Nobody was ever found. Eighteen years ago. That's the whole of it." She picked the bread back up. "Pass your brother the salt."

The salt was already at Caleb's elbow. Nora passed it anyway.

It was not new. They had heard it before, all their lives, the same short story, the way you know the words of a thing you were told before you could talk. What Nora heard, tonight, was the door in it. She had always heard the door. She had never once heard it open.

"I only meant—" Caleb began.

"Caleb," said Nora. "Eat."

He looked at her. It was a look she knew, because she had been getting it for eighteen years, and it said that she had gone over to the other side, again, without telling him why. She did not tell him why. She could not have said, at the table, that it was because of a piece of paper folded in three, and a question about a logger that had come before a question about a saddle. None of those was anything. She only knew that her mother had answered him with the door, and that if he pushed on it, something would happen that could not be un-happened, and she was not going to let it happen over bread.

So Caleb ate, and looked at her the way you look at weather.

"I'll be honest," said their mother, into the quiet, in her ordinary voice, as if nothing had been said at all, "I don't love you out on that bar."

"We were with Ines," said Nora.

"I know who you were with."

"She got us off ninety minutes early. She doesn't spend margin."

"I know she doesn't." Their mother was looking at the window, where the moth had given up and was walking, upside down, along the sill on the outside, on the reflection of their own table. "I don't love it. That's all. That's allowed."

And Nora and Caleb looked at each other, one look, and both of them understood the same thing at the same time, without a count: of course. Of course Mom did not love them out at a research station on the same piece of coast where a research boat had been lost. Of course she had wanted to know which island. Of course she had answered *Ok. Straight home after. Both.* inside a minute, when she never answered anything from the bench inside an hour. It was the most obvious thing in the world, and Nora, who had been carrying the third text around all afternoon like a stone in a boot, felt it go light, and was ashamed of herself, a little, and glad.

"It was fine, Mom," said Caleb, gently, for once. "Really."

"I know." Their mother stood, and took his plate, and put her hand on the back of his neck on the way past, for a second, and then it was dishes.

After the dishes there was the repacking, which Caleb wanted to leave until Friday and Nora wanted to do now, so they did it now.

You unpacked everything onto the kitchen floor, both packs, and you did not put a single thing back until you had looked at it. Dry bag. Headlamps, both tested, one flickering, batteries swapped from the drawer. The jars, dry, in their sleeve. Gloves, off the heater, stiff, worked soft again by hand. A note for Ines about the stake, which was on its way to Japan. The net bag, folded, still smelling of the island. The spare battery, the one that weighed as much as a cat and had gone all the way to Tern Island and back unswapped, into the bottom of her own pack, because it was still her job. Caleb sat on the floor with his back against the cupboards and handed her things in the order she asked for them and, for a while, did not talk, which was his way of saying he was still cross and had decided not to be.

"Six point one," she said, when she hooked her pack on the luggage scale, and looked at him.

"Don't."

"I'm not. I'm just telling you the number."

"Nora." He was nearly smiling. "You had your fun. You had four hundred grams of fun. Let the man live."

She let the man live. The packs went by the front door, side by side, ready for Saturday, in the hall by the shelf where the keys were, and she went up to bed with her hair still damp and the rain coming properly now, at last, in long pushes against the side of the house.

She woke at a quarter past eleven, thirsty, and lay for a minute listening to the firs, and then went down for water the way she had gone down for water her whole life, on the outside edges of the stairs, where they did not creak, because she had worked out where they creaked when she was six and had never had to work it out again.

The kitchen light was off. The hall light was off. There was only the porch light, coming through the glass in the door, laying a long yellow shape across the mat and the two packs and the floor.

Her mother was standing in it.

She was standing at the shelf by the door, in the dark, in the fleece, with the keys in one hand as if she had been going out, or had just come in, and was doing neither. Her other hand was at the small of her back. And she was looking at the photograph. Not at the keys. At the photograph, the man on the dock in the green jacket, squinting into a sun that had gone down eighteen years ago, that stood on that shelf all day every day where nobody, ever, looked at it.

Nora had lived in that house her whole life. She had never seen anyone look at it.

She stood on the stairs with one hand on the rail and did not go down, and did not go up, and did not breathe in any way that could be heard. Her mother did not move. The rain moved. The porch light lay across the packs by the door, ready for Saturday, and her mother stood between them and the door with her back to the stairs, looking, and went on looking, for longer than you look at anything you have already seen.

Nora went back up without the water.
