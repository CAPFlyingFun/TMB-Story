# Chapter 48: Slow Isn't Wrong

Mark's truck was at the door in nine minutes. Jack knew, because he had counted every one of them, standing in the hall with the bag in one hand and Sarah's coat in the other while Sarah sat on the chair by the front door and breathed.

She looked up at him between two of them. "You can put the coat down."

"I'm holding it ready."

"You've been holding it ready for six minutes."

"Seven."

Mark came in without knocking, took one look at the two of them, and took the bag out of Jack's hand.

"Truck's warm. Inner streets. Four minutes. Nobody talks to me while I'm driving."

Nobody did. The town went by in the dark, every blind down, every streetlight on along the inner streets, and Mark drove as if he were carrying a full glass of water on the dashboard. Sarah held Jack's hand in the back and squeezed it, hard, once, halfway, and didn't let go until it passed.

The medical center's lights were all on. The former paramedic was at the door, a big quiet man in a sweater, and he had the door open before the truck had stopped. Behind him, down the corridor, Jack could hear water running, and the woman from the kitchens saying something firm to a kettle.

Doctor Mercer was in the room at the end, with her sleeves rolled up, and she looked as though she had slept ten hours and eaten breakfast.

"Good. Onto the bed for a minute. Then you can do what you like."

She checked Sarah, quickly and gently, and put the belt on, and the tablet on its stand, and the sound came out of it, fast and soft, the sound they had listened to for weeks.

"Hundred and forty. He's fine. You're fine. You're well started."

She took her gloves off. "And it's going to be a while. A first baby takes its time. So here's the most important thing I'll tell you tonight. Rest when you can. Move when you want to. You know better than I do what helps."

Sarah let out a long breath. "How long's a while?"

"Long enough that Jack should take his coat off."

Jack looked down. He was still holding her coat.

It was a long night.

Sarah walked. Up the corridor and back, one hand on the rail and the other in Jack's. Every few minutes she stopped and leaned on the wall with her forehead against her arm, and breathed. Jack counted for her, out loud, slowly, because she had asked him to. Then he stopped counting, because she asked him to stop.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just be quiet for that one."

Nurse Hale came and went without a sound. She had the belt monitor on a long lead now, and the tablet on a little cart that she pushed along beside them. Every so often she glanced at it and said nothing. That was how Jack learned that nothing meant good.

At two, Sarah wanted the shower. She sat on a stool under it with the warm water running on her back, and Jack sat on the floor outside the curtain with his jeans soaked to the knee, and held her hand through the gap.

"Do you know what this reminds me of?"

Sarah's voice came through the curtain, tight. "If you say the rain..."

"The rain."

"I'm going to kill you."

Jack leaned his head back against the tiles. "Buckets of it. Hammering on the roof."

There was a pause. Then, very faintly, behind the water, she laughed. And then the next one came, and she stopped laughing, and gripped his hand until his fingers went white, and he let the rain go.

At three, she wanted the bed, and then she didn't. She knelt on the floor beside it with her arms folded on the mattress and her head down on them, and rocked, and Jack knelt beside her with his hand flat on her back. She had decided that one herself, and Doctor Mercer had looked at it, and nodded.

"Good. That's good. Whatever your body says."

It was a little before four when the tablet changed.

Jack heard it before he saw it. The sound from the speaker went thin, then faint, crackling, as if it were far away. On the screen, the line that had climbed and fallen evenly all night dropped, and wandered, and the number under it blinked and went blank.

His stomach turned over.

Sarah lifted her head off the mattress. "What's that? Jack. What's that?"

Nurse Hale was already there. She didn't hurry, and she didn't look at the screen. She knelt down beside Sarah and put her hand on the belt, low on Sarah's stomach, and Jack saw what she saw. It had slid. Kneeling, rocking, all that last hour, it had worked its way down and to one side.

"It's moved, that's all. You've moved, and it hasn't kept up."

She loosened the strap, and slid the probe back up and across, and held it there a moment with two fingers, listening.

The sound came back. Fast and soft and steady, filling the room.

On the screen, the line climbed. The number came back. A hundred and forty.

Nurse Hale tightened the strap and sat back on her heels. "There he is. He never went anywhere. The belt did."

Sarah put her head back down on her arms, and Jack heard her breathe out, long and shaking.

"Don't do that to me."

"I'll have a word with it."

Doctor Mercer had come to the door at the sound. She looked at the screen, and at the strap, and then at Jack. "You saw her fix it?"

Jack nodded. "I saw."

"Good. If it does it again, now you know."

She came and crouched beside the bed, so her face was level with Sarah's. "Sarah. You're doing well. You're also tired, and it's going to go on a while longer. I have one thing for the pain. It would take the edge off. It would also make you drowsy, and it might slow you down. I'm not saying yes or no. I'm telling you it's there."

Sarah turned her head on her arms and looked at her. "What would it cost me?"

"Some time, maybe. Some of your strength at the end, when you'll want it. And it can make him sleepy too, when he comes."

Sarah was quiet through the next one. When it had gone, she lifted her head.

"Not yet. Ask me again later. I'll tell you if I want it."

"Then I'll ask you later."

Jack didn't know what time it was when the window began to go gray. He only knew the blind had a pale line around its edge, and that somewhere outside, far off beyond the edge of the town, and enormous, the robin had started to sing.

He had heard it most mornings since the town shrank.

Sarah heard it too. She was sitting on the edge of the bed with her head on his shoulder, between two of them, and her eyes were closed.

"It's morning."

Jack kissed the top of her head. "Listen to him. Showing off."

"I wanted him here by morning."

Doctor Mercer checked her again at six, and again at eight. Both times she said the same thing, in the same calm voice. Jack was beginning to understand it was the voice she used when there was nothing to worry about.

"It's slow. Slow isn't wrong. Slow is just slow."

Sarah stared at her, red-eyed. "It's been nine hours."

"It has. You're doing it. You're just doing it at his speed."

"His speed is terrible."

"First babies. He's in no hurry because he's never had to be."

The woman from the kitchens brought tea that nobody drank, and toast that Jack ate without tasting. The former paramedic stood outside the door like a wall. Somewhere around nine, Jack realized he had not thought about the pumps, or the tanks, or the edge, all night. Only about the woman in front of him. He couldn't have thought about anything else if he had tried.

At ten, Sarah stopped walking.

She stood in the middle of the room with both hands on the end of the bed and her head down, and when the next one came it was different. Jack saw it. Longer. Harder. It went on and on, and when it went she didn't straighten up.

Her voice was very small. "I can't. Jack. I can't do this anymore. I'm too tired. I can't."

He didn't know what to say. He had a joke ready, he always had a joke ready, and he knew not to use it. He just put his arms around her from behind, and held on, and rested his forehead on the back of her neck.

Doctor Mercer was watching from the door. She came in and crouched beside them.

"Sarah. Look at me."

Sarah looked.

"When a woman tells me she can't do this anymore, do you know what that tells me?"

Sarah shook her head.

"It tells me she's nearly there."
