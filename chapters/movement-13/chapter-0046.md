# Chapter 46: Forty Weeks

Aiden held the first bean up to the grow light as if it were a jewel.

It was long and green and slightly crooked, and it had come off a vine that climbed a string from a bed of soil on the floor of the community center's back hall. There were sixty beds now, in rows, under lamps that buzzed. A month ago it had been the room where the town kept its folding chairs.

"That's one."

Jack looked along the rows. The vines were heavy with them. "There's a lot more than one."

"I know. I just wanted to hold the first one."

Aiden put it in the basket, very carefully, and picked the next one. By eight o'clock the basket was full, and there was a second one beside it, and Mark had come in from the back room to see what was taking everyone so long. He stood in the doorway and looked at the baskets for a while with his arms folded.

"Those are beans."

Aiden held one up to show him. "Those are beans."

"Huh."

He went back to the meeting. Jack thought that was probably the happiest he had ever seen him.

In the back room, Lena had her clipboard turned to the kitchens' page. Sarah was in the good chair with the list on her knee, and her feet up on a crate Paul had padded with a folded blanket, at Doctor Mercer's orders.

Lena waited until the baskets had gone past the window to the kitchens. "Food. I counted the stores again yesterday. On their own, careful portions get us to about the first days of May."

Paul frowned. "That's a week."

"On their own. They're not on their own anymore."

Lena tapped the page. "The greenhouse is picking every day now. The back hall starts today. From tonight, the kitchens cook what's grown first, and make up the rest from the stores. The stores go down slower. Not a lot slower. Every basket is a little less off the shelves."

Sarah's pen had stopped. "How much slower?"

"I don't know yet. A week of picking will tell me. It doesn't make the date go away. It moves it. And it keeps moving it, as long as Aiden keeps growing."

She looked around the table. "It's thin. But it holds."

Aiden had come in behind the baskets with soil on his knees. He looked as though somebody had handed him the town. "Then I need more beds. And I need seed. I've used nearly all of it."

Somebody knocked on the open door.

It was Mr. Pell, in his cardigan, with the former paramedic a few steps behind him, who had walked him over. Mr. Pell had a small paper envelope in his hand, folded over twice.

"I'm told you're short of beans."

Aiden blinked at him. "Short of seed."

"Same thing, in a year."

Mr. Pell held out the envelope. "My wife's. She saved them from the same plants every autumn for thirty years. I saved them last autumn because she would have. I didn't know what for."

Aiden took the envelope in both hands, the way he had held the first bean.

"I'll plant them today."

"Plant them well. They're fussy. They like to be talked to."

"I can do that."

"I know. The security men say you talk to everything."

Mr. Pell turned to Sarah, and his voice changed. "And you. How many weeks?"

"Forty on Wednesday."

"Then what are you doing in a meeting?"

Jack had been waiting a week to say it, and found he didn't have to. Sarah looked down at the list on her knee. Then she tore the top sheet off, and the one under it, and held them out across the table.

"Lena. You take it."

Lena looked at the pages as if they might be hot. "You're sure?"

"I'm not asking because I can't do it. I'm asking because I want to be thinking about one thing for a while, and it isn't pumps."

Lena took the pages. She put them on her clipboard and smoothed them down.

"Morning meeting's mine, then."

"And I'll know if you skip anything."

"I know you will."

Sarah turned to Aiden. "I'm keeping the Field Book."

"It's yours. It was always yours."

"Good."

That evening, Jack packed the bag.

Doctor Mercer had given them a list for it a month ago, and Sarah had packed it a month ago, and it had sat by the front door ever since. Jack had walked past it every morning. Tonight he took everything out and laid it on the bed and checked it against the list, twice, and then he started putting it back in a better order.

Sarah watched him from the doorway with both hands on her stomach.

"What are you doing?"

"Making it faster to find things."

"It was fast."

He held up the blanket. "This was at the bottom."

"The blanket's at the bottom because we don't need it until the end."

He looked at the blanket. Mr. Pell's wife's blanket, soft and white, folded small.

"That's a good reason."

"It's the reason. Put it back at the bottom."

He put it back at the bottom. Then he picked up the little knitted hat and turned it over in his hands. It was so small he could have closed his fist around it.

"Do you think he'll have my ears?"

"I think he'll have ears. I'm hoping for two."

"Mine stick out a little."

"I know. I married them."

She came in and sat down on the edge of the bed, slowly, the way she did everything now, and took the bag from him and started repacking it the way it had been. He let her. He sat beside her and handed her things.

The next three days went slowly. Sarah walked every evening, on the inner streets, with Jack beside her and Mark's truck idling two blocks behind them like an embarrassed parent. People came to their doors as she passed. Nobody asked her anything. They just watched her go by, and smiled, and sometimes the older ones nodded, the way Mr. Pell did.

On the thirtieth, at eight in the evening, Doctor Mercer came to the house.

She took Sarah's pressure, and wrote it down. She put her hands on Sarah's stomach and felt carefully, her eyes on the wall. Then she put the belt on, and the tablet on the arm of the couch, and they all listened to the fast soft sound and watched the line climb and fall.

"Hundred and forty. Head down. Pressure holding."

She took the belt off. "Forty weeks today. Congratulations. You've reached your due date."

Sarah let her head fall back against the cushion. "And nothing's happening."

"Nothing's wrong. That's different."

Doctor Mercer sat down on the chair she always sat on, and put her bag on her knees.

"A first baby is very often late. Here's what happens now. I come every evening instead of every few days. The belt goes on every evening. And if he isn't here at forty-one weeks, we sit down, the three of us, and decide together what comes next. There are gentle things first. We don't need any of them yet."

"Decide together."

"It's your labor. I'm only the one who's done it before."

Sarah turned that over. "What gentle things?"

"Walking. Patience. Then one or two things I can do with my hands, that you can say no to. We'll go through every one before you need to choose."

"And if I don't like any of them?"

"Then we talk about why. And then we decide."

Sarah nodded. "All right."

Doctor Mercer stood up and picked up her bag. At the door she turned around.

"And Jack. Stop repacking the bag."

He stared at her. "How do you know about the bag?"

"Everyone knows about the bag."

When she had gone, Sarah lay on the couch with her eyes shut and her hands on her stomach, and Jack sat on the floor beside her.

"Forty weeks."

"Forty weeks."

"He's late."

Jack thought about it. "He gets that from you."

Sarah opened one eye. "Say that again, in labor, and see what happens."
