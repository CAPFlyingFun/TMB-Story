# Chapter 47: Eleven at Night

Sarah lay on the couch with the morning meeting on her wrist and her other hand flat on her stomach, and did not say anything.

It was harder than listening to Jack cross the plate.

Lena's voice came small and clear from the back room, going down the list. Pumps, both lines running. The tanks, up again. The beans, a second basket and a third. Paul's roof over the line walk, one tarp torn by wind and mended. The Field Book, which Sarah already knew, because she had copied every sheet into it at first gray.

Then Lena reached the bottom of the page.

"That's everything."

Sarah sat up.

"The outpost. You didn't do the outpost."

There was a pause from the back room. Then a ripple of laughter, and Mark's voice, dry.

"Told you."

Lena sighed into the wrist. "Unit Four. The outpost."

Unit Four's voice came from the outpost, a little stiff, the way it always was on an open channel. "Line walked. Pumps running. The doorway lamp's on. Nothing's come out past it."

"Thank you. Sarah, go back to sleep."

"I wasn't asleep."

"Go back to whatever you were doing that wasn't sleeping."

Sarah lay down again, and found she was smiling.

Everyone asked. That was the thing about those days. Mark asked every morning at seven, on her wrist, before the meeting, in the same three words.

"Anything?"

"Nothing."

"Copy."

Paul came to the door on the third with a box of fuses he thought the medical center might want, and stood on the step holding it and very carefully not looking at her stomach.

"Anything?"

"Nothing, Paul."

"Right. Well. Fuses."

The woman from the kitchens sent a jar of soup with her grandchildren, who stood on the step and stared at Sarah as if she might go off like a firework. Mr. Pell sent nothing, and came to the window on his way past every afternoon, with Unit Four, and lifted a hand, and went on.

Jack was the worst. Jack asked with his eyes, every time she shifted on the couch, every time she breathed out a little harder than usual. That evening she caught him watching her drink a glass of water.

"Jack. I'm drinking water."

"I know."

"You're watching me drink water."

"I'm watching you. The water's just there."

He sat down on the floor beside the couch with the tablet. "I've been thinking about names."

She closed her eyes. "Here we go."

"Pump."

"No."

"It's strong. It's reliable. It works night and day."

"No."

"Grate."

She put a hand over her eyes. "Jack."

"Crossing Plate Bennett. We'd call him Plate."

She opened her eyes and looked at him, and he was grinning, and she couldn't help it. She laughed, and the baby kicked her hard under the ribs, as if he had an opinion.

"See. He likes it."

"He's objecting."

Jack put his hand where the kick had been, and the baby kicked it. Then his face changed, the grin going soft.

"You've got one. Haven't you."

Sarah looked at the ceiling.

"Maybe. I want to see him first. I want to look at him and know it's his."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we'll think of another one. Not Plate."

On the fifth, in the afternoon, she asked to see the room.

Nobody argued. Mark drove her himself, by the inner streets, four minutes exactly, and Nurse Hale had come in early off her night shift to meet them at the medical center door. She walked in front of Sarah down the corridor at Sarah's pace, without seeming to.

"It's all ready. It's been ready since the drill. Doctor Mercer comes in every morning and looks at it."

The room was at the end, where the corridor was quietest. It was smaller than Sarah had pictured. A bed, made up white, with a chair beside it for Jack. A window with its blind down. Against the wall, on a cart, the warming box Jack had made from a storage crate, humming very softly, a little light on the thermostat showing its temperature. Beside it, the breathing bottle Paul and Jack had built, filled with water to a line drawn in marker, the tubing coiled and clean. The belt monitor on its charger. And on the wall by the door, in Lena's handwriting, a sheet. The runners' list. Who boils, who fetches, who calls whom.

Sarah stood in the doorway and looked at it all.

This is where. Not someday. Here.

She walked in and sat down on the edge of the bed. It was firm and clean, and the sheet was cool under her hand.

Nurse Hale waited by the door. "Is it all right?"

"It's all right."

Sarah looked at the warming box, humming to itself. "Do you think we'll need any of it?"

"Probably not. Most of the time you don't. That's why you have it."

Sarah nodded slowly, and smoothed the sheet beside her, and her hands went still. She hadn't known, until then, that they had been shaking for days.

"Then it will do."

Doctor Mercer came every evening at eight, and put on the belt, and wrote it down. Forty weeks and two days. Three. Four. Five. A hundred and forty. Head down. Holding. Nothing wrong.

On the sixth, Doctor Mercer took the belt off and looked at the calendar on the wall.

"Tomorrow's forty-one. We'll talk tomorrow, if he hasn't made up his mind."

"He's comfortable."

"He's welcome to be. Just not forever."

Sarah went to bed at ten, and slept.

She woke in the dark, and lay still, and didn't know why.

Then she did.

It started low in her back and came around, a tightening, slow and wide, like a hand closing. It was not like the practice ones. The practice ones had been a squeeze. This had a shape. It built, and held, and held, and then let go.

She breathed out.

On the wall, the clock said eleven. Exactly eleven.

Of course. It was morning, for him.

Jack was asleep beside her, on his back, one arm flung over his eyes. She didn't wake him. She lifted her wrist, and opened the timer, and lay there in the dark and waited.

Twelve minutes. The hand closed again. She timed it. Forty seconds.

Ten minutes. Forty-five seconds.

She got up, slowly, and walked to the end of the hall and back, holding on to the wall, because Doctor Mercer had said walking helped and because she couldn't have lain still if she had tried. The house was very quiet. Outside the window, the inner street lay quiet under its streetlights, with the blinds down all the way along, and somewhere far off, beyond the edge of the town, the island's night went on making its sounds, every one of them enormous.

Eight minutes. Seven.

They were regular. They were coming closer. They were not going away.

It was a little after midnight. She stood in the kitchen with one hand on the counter, and breathed through one more, all the way to the end, and then she lifted her wrist and called Doctor Mercer. Not Jack. Not the station. Her.

Doctor Mercer answered on the second tone, wide awake.

"Sarah. Talk to me."

"I think it's him."

"Tell me."

Sarah looked at the timer, and read it twice before she said it. "Eleven o'clock, the first one. Then twelve minutes. Ten. Eight. Seven. Forty seconds at first, nearly a minute now. It starts in my back and comes around."

Doctor Mercer listened to all of it without a word. "That's him. Not yet, but that's him starting. You did right to time them. Wake Jack. I'll call Mark, and he'll have the truck at your door in ten minutes, and I'll be waiting for you at the center."

Sarah went back down the hall to the bedroom, and stood in the doorway, and looked at Jack asleep with his arm over his eyes.

She put her hand on his shoulder.

He was awake at once, up on one elbow, staring at her in the dark.

"What? What is it? Is it a drill?"

Sarah laughed, and then stopped laughing, because the next one was coming, low in her back, and she held on to the door frame and breathed.

"It's not a drill."
