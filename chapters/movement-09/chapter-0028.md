# Chapter 28: Eyeshine

Doctor Mercer came at eight and took Sarah's pressure twice, the way she always did now, as though the first number needed a witness.

"Holding."

Sarah let out a breath. "Holding is good."

"Holding is what I asked for."

Doctor Mercer wrote it down and closed the notebook. "Thirty-three weeks tomorrow. You know that."

"I know that."

Doctor Mercer turned around. "You're going out tonight."

"For an hour. In the truck, mostly. We're moving the cart."

"In the dark."

"That's the point. It's asleep in the dark."

Doctor Mercer peeled back his dressing, looked at the stitches, and pressed the tape down again. "Pink. Closing. If you open it on a rope, I'll sew it shut myself, and I won't be gentle."

She picked up her bag. At the door she stopped and looked back at Sarah. "And you sleep."

"I'll be on the couch."

"That isn't what I said."

Mark had written the plan on his board that afternoon. The white cart had stood at the end of the western road since the night it happened. The flies liked its white side, and the wet line had pointed straight at it. So the cart was moving thirty meters back, in the dark, while the spider was in its silk.

It had drizzled all evening. By eleven the rain had stopped, but nothing had dried. The streets shone under the streetlights, and the air was soft and damp and smelled of earth.

Aiden's voice came on the channel from the greenhouse door. "Eleven degrees. No stars. Everything's wet."

Mark answered from the driver's seat. "Copy. Lights off."

He drove the last stretch by memory again, with the red lamp pointed down at the road. Unit Four sat in the back with the tow rope coiled in his lap.

The white shape of the cart rose out of the dark. Mark stopped close behind it this time and backed the truck up until the hitch was a meter from the cart's front bar.

"Four. Tie it off."

Unit Four was already out. Jack got out after him and held the red lamp low while he worked, so that the light fell on the knot and nowhere else. The grass was a black wall three meters past the cart. Jack didn't look at it. He looked at the knot.

The clicking in the stems came back, slower tonight, softer. Then a new sound.

It was wet. A slow, heavy, sucking sound, like a boot being pulled out of deep mud, over and over, with no space between. It was coming from the grass. And it was getting closer.

Unit Four's hands stopped on the rope.

Mark's voice came from the open window, very low. "Stop."

Nobody moved. The sound went on. Wet, and slow, and close.

Mark lifted the red lamp from Jack's hand through the window and swung it, slowly, toward the end of the road.

Something was coming out of the grass.

At first Jack thought it was the ground moving. It was dark and gleaming and it rippled as it came, the whole surface of it shining wet in the red light.

Then the front of it lifted off the asphalt, and he saw two long stalks rise from it, and at the tip of each stalk, a small dark bead. Below them, a second, shorter pair reached down and touched the road, once, twice, the way a blind man touches a curb.

It slid out onto the asphalt. Its body kept coming out of the grass behind it. And kept coming. By the time the end of it came clear of the stems, the front was halfway to the crack.

It was as long as a bus.

Mark's voice didn't rise at all. "In the truck. Now. Slow."

Unit Four went in through the back door without a sound. Jack got in the front and pulled the door to, and didn't let it latch, because the latch was loud.

His wrist buzzed. Sarah's voice was almost nothing.

"Jack. What is that? I can't see anything. The feed's black. I can hear it on Mark's radio. What is it?"

Jack watched the thing slide across the end of the road in the red light. It went smoothly, without legs, without hurry, its stalks swaying ahead of it.

"It's a slug."

There was a pause on the channel.

"A slug."

"A big one."

"How big is a big one?"

"You know the buses at the depot?"

The pause was longer this time. "Okay."

Aiden's voice came on, careful and quiet. "Doctor Bennett. They come out on wet nights. Mild ones, after rain. It's looking for soft things. Dead things. Leaves, fungus, anything rotting. It doesn't hunt people."

Mark kept his eyes on it. "It doesn't have to hunt us to sit on us."

Nobody answered that.

The slug reached the crack where the trough had stood. It stopped there. The lower feelers touched the asphalt, all around, slowly, as if it were reading the road. Jack thought of the puddles that had lain there the morning before, and the dead gnat. Whatever had been left, it was finding.

Then it turned toward the cart.

Unit Four's voice was hardly there at all. "It's coming to the cart."

It came the last few meters. Its feelers found the cart's front wheel and touched it. Then the slug lifted the front of its body and slid up onto the wheel, onto the white side of the cart, its whole underside pressed against the panel. The cart rocked on its springs. Its shine spread across the white paint. The front of it reached the bar, and the tow rope Unit Four had tied there, and slid over the knot without stopping.

Jack heard Unit Four breathe out behind him. "It's holding."

Mark didn't take his eyes off it. "It'll hold."

The slug stayed on the cart for a long time. Eating, maybe. Whatever five mornings of flies had left on that white side. Its stalks swayed above the roof of the cart, catching the red light.

Sarah's voice came in again, very low. "Talk to me. Please. I can only hear it."

So Jack talked, low and even, the way she'd have done for him. Where it was. How it moved. The beads on the stalks, which she said were its eyes, and which he said he'd guessed.

After twenty minutes by Mark's watch, the slug slid down off the far side of the cart. It went back the way it had come, slowly, to the end of the road, and into the stems. The wet sound went with it, fainter and fainter, until there was only the clicking again.

Mark swung the lamp across the road.

From the grass to the crack, from the crack to the cart, and back again, the asphalt shone. A broad silver path in the red light, as wide as the truck, glistening and thick.

Mark looked at it for a long time.

"That's a wet line."

Jack looked at it too. "And we didn't spill a drop."

Unit Four got out, slowly, and looked at the rope. The knot was coated, shining. He touched it with one finger and pulled the finger back.

"It's sticky."

Mark started the engine, very low. "Leave it. We tow it anyway."

The cart came away from the end of the road with a soft wet sound, rolling behind them on its old tires. Thirty meters back, Mark stopped and switched off the engine. He didn't turn the truck around.

"We stay. First light's in seven hours. I want to see what comes for that."

He swung the red lamp back down the road, toward the trail, one last time.

Something shone back.

Not the trail. Points. A scatter of small bright points at the end of the road, low to the asphalt. They threw the red light back at him, bright as sparks, the way a cat's eyes do in headlights. Two big ones. Fainter ones beside them.

They moved.

Mark's hand closed over the lamp, and the road went black.

Jack couldn't see anything. Not the trail, not the cart, not the stems. Just the dark, and the faint orange smudge of the last streetlight far behind them in the mirror.

Mark's voice was the smallest Jack had ever heard it. "Nobody moves. Nobody speaks. Windows up. Slow."

Unit Four wound his window up a turn at a time.

Then the sound came. Not wet this time. Dry. A light, quick ticking on the asphalt, like fingernails on a table, a great many of them, starting and stopping. Coming up the road.

Sarah's voice was in Jack's ear, through the wrist terminal turned down as far as it would go. "Jack. What was that? Mark's lamp went out. What's out there?"

He couldn't answer. He didn't dare.

The ticking came alongside the truck.

Then something touched it.

The whole truck rocked, gently, on its springs, as though someone had leaned a hand against the back door. Then the side. Something brushed along the roof with a long soft scrape. Jack held his breath until his chest hurt. Beside him, Mark sat with his hand still over the dark lamp and didn't move at all.

The ticking went on past them.

Up the road. Toward the town.

Jack turned his head, very slowly, and looked in the mirror.

Far behind them, at the last lit streetlight, the orange glow was full of moths. He had never noticed them before. Dozens of them, turning and turning, big as gulls even from here. And in the dark beneath the light, for one second, he saw the bright points again, low on the road, turned up toward the moths.

Mark had seen it too. He lifted the radio to his mouth. "Lena."

Her answer came back just as quiet. "I'm here."

"The western road. Every streetlight. Off. Now."

There was the smallest pause. Jack knew what it cost her. She had left those lights on for people walking home.

"Doing it now."

The orange glow in the mirror went out.

There was nothing at all then. No light anywhere. Only the cold, and the clicking in the stems behind them, and somewhere up the road in the dark, between the truck and the houses, the dry, quick ticking. Starting. Stopping.

Then nothing.

Sarah's voice was hardly a whisper. "Jack. Where is it?"

Jack looked into the black where the town was.

"I don't know."
