# Chapter 29: Night Shift

Nobody moved for an hour.

Jack sat with his hands flat on his knees and listened. The clicking in the stems behind them. The hum that came and went. Once, a long way up the road, a soft scrape, like something heavy shifting its weight. Then nothing again.

Unit Four leaned forward between the seats, so low that Jack barely heard him. "The houses on the last block. Are the people in them awake?"

Mark kept his eyes on the black windshield. "I hope not."

Sarah came on the channel, very quietly. "Lena called the houses on the last block. Every blind is down. Every door's shut. That's the rule. They kept it."

Mark's hands stayed on the wheel. "Then they're safer than we are."

After that, nobody said anything until the sky began to change.

The cloud had stayed all night. It didn't go from black to charcoal so much as from black to a dull, soft gray that came from everywhere at once. At twenty past six, Aiden's voice came on from the greenhouse.

"Ten degrees. Still cloud."

Sarah's voice followed at once. "Feeds are coming back. Give me a minute."

It took her less than one.

"Jack. It's on the last block. On a lawn under the last streetlight, the one Lena turned off. It's eating something."

She sent the frame to his wrist. It was gray and grainy, but it was enough.

A low, broad shape crouched on a strip of grass beside a dark front porch, with a moth's wing sticking out from under it like a dropped umbrella. Its legs were thick and banded, and its body was covered in something like short gray fur. Two big eyes sat on the front of its head, above a row of smaller ones. It was a little bigger than the truck.

Aiden had the same frame. His voice was steady, which Jack knew meant he was working hard to keep it that way.

"That's a wolf spider."

Mark didn't look away from the road. "Another spider."

"A different spider. The one in the grass hunts by day. This one hunts at night. On the ground, no web. It feels its way with its legs, and it sees well in the dark. That's what we saw in the lamp. Their eyes shine, like a cat's."

"Where does it sleep?"

"In a hole. A burrow. Somewhere close, if it came that far."

Sarah's voice cut in. "It's moving."

It didn't creep. It ran. The frame on Jack's wrist blurred, and then the shape was off the lawn and onto the road and coming down the middle of it, straight toward them, very fast.

Mark's hand went to the key and stopped there.

It came past the truck in the gray at a dead run, close enough that Jack saw the gray fur stir as it went. One leg flicked the side mirror as it went. Then it was past the cart, and past the crack.

At the end of the road, where the asphalt met the soil, it stopped at a dark round hole that Jack had never noticed. A hole twice as wide as a door, a few meters from where the trough had stood.

It went down into it head first and was gone.

Nobody said anything.

Jack found his voice first. "We stood right next to that."

Aiden sounded almost apologetic. "It could have been there for months. They keep one hole, and they come back to it."

Mark was looking at the hole. "Every morning we've been out there. With our backs to it."

"In the dark, it was out hunting. They go a long way some nights. Last night the light up the road brought it our way. By day it stays down."

Behind the hole, in the stems, the silk retreat hung pale in the gray. As they watched, the jumping spider came out of it. Not slowly, not on a mild morning. It came down the stem in short, quick bursts and stopped at the edge of the asphalt, its whole front end turned toward the slug's trail, where the first flies had already begun to land.

It went out onto the road after them. Three steps. Stop. Three steps. Stop. It took a fly off the edge of the shine, and then another, following the trail on past the crack, toward the empty place where the cart had stood until midnight.

It stopped there and turned its front end toward the truck, and looked for a long time. Then it went back to the silk with its breakfast.

It passed within a few meters of the hole. Neither of them seemed to care about the other.

Mark watched it go. Then he started the engine.

"One works days. One works nights."

At the station, he cleared the board again, all but the four words in the corner. Lena came in with her clipboard. Paul was already there, sitting on an upturned crate, with the drawing for the second trough rolled across his knees. He had heard it all on the channel.

Mark wrote one line.

Night hunter. Western road. Burrow at the end.

Then another.

Streetlights off. Last block on every road.

Lena didn't argue. "I'll do the last streetlight on every road tonight. It saves power anyway."

Mark tapped the board. "The blinds held. The doors held. The rules worked where we kept them. The light we left on is what brought it up the road."

Sarah's voice came out of Lena's console. She sounded tired, and very clear. "And the wet. I've done all nine roads now. Every road end but the northern ones has something that comes out of the grass at night and leaves something wet. Slugs, mostly. Some things I don't have names for. Every morning, the ends of our roads are wet, and every morning something comes to drink."

Mark wrote it down. Seven roads. Every night.

Then he turned and folded his arms.

"So. Shaking is one leaf, on a good morning, a hundred liters if we're lucky, and every time it brings something down that kicks. And now there's something living in a hole at the end of the road. It's not a water supply."

Lena looked at her clipboard. She didn't need to. "Fourteen days. At the rate we're going. A little more if the notice holds."

Paul unrolled the trough drawing on his knees, looked at it, and rolled it up again without a word.

For a while nobody said anything.

Then Sarah's voice came again, slower now, the way she sounded when she was thinking out loud and didn't want to be interrupted.

"We keep looking at water that falls. Rain. Dew. Leaves. That's water falling onto an island that's suddenly enormous. It's like trying to drink out of the sky."

Mark frowned at the console. "Where else would it come from?"

"From the island. The water that was already here, before any of this. The island was built, Mark. It didn't grow. Somebody built every meter of it, and somebody drew it first. Where the rain goes. The pipes. The tanks. Somewhere there are the drawings."

Lena looked up from her clipboard.

"The station has the settlement's plans. Every pipe and cable inside the town. That's mine. But the island's own drawings were never the station's business. Nobody at the station ever needed them." She hesitated. "There's an engineering archive. Old. On the servers in the records building. I've never had a reason to open it."

Mark looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned back to the board, uncapped the marker, and wrote one more word under all the others.

Drawings.

He underlined it twice.
