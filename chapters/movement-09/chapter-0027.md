# Chapter 27: The Wet Line

Jack called Mark at ten. Mark didn't ask what it was about. He said he'd be there in five minutes, and he was there in four.

He came in with his coat still on and didn't sit down. Sarah ran the recording back to seven oh four and turned the tablet toward him.

Mark stood behind the couch with his hands on the back of it and watched the whole thing without a word. The puddles. The spider's face lowered into the first one. The next one. The wet line. Three steps, stop. Three steps, stop. The place where the line ran out, and the long turning of its front end, left and right.

When it climbed back into the silk, Mark reached over Sarah's shoulder and pressed play again.

He watched it a second time.

"Where were we?"

Sarah checked the timestamp. "In the truck. Halfway to the station."

"And Paul?"

"In his flatbed. Right behind you."

Mark straightened up. "Nobody was standing there."

Jack was sitting on the floor by the crib. "Nobody had to be. It didn't come for us. It came for the water."

"Same thing, if we're standing in it."

Nobody argued with that.

Sarah pulled up a different window on the tablet. It was a map Jack hadn't looked at in five days. The settlement, drawn from above, every street and roof, with a thin red line around all of it.

"That's the boundary overlay. From the night it happened. Whoever drew it drew it carefully, and nobody's touched it since."

She zoomed in on the western road until the red line sat right across the end of the asphalt. "It's the best map of the edge we've got. So I can measure."

She laid one finger on the end of the road and one on the cart, and moved the first finger along the wet line on the frame beside it. "It got about halfway. Halfway to the cart. Then the road was dry, and it stopped."

Mark looked at the map for a long moment. "On a sunny morning."

"On a sunny morning. The road dried behind you as you went."

"And on a gray one?"

Sarah didn't answer. She didn't have to.

Mark took his radio off his belt. "Lena. You awake?"

Lena's voice came back at once, as though she'd been holding the radio. "I'm at the station. I'm always at the station."

"Get Paul."

It took a minute. Paul's voice, when it came, was thick with sleep and not happy about it. "If this is about the corners, I know about the corners."

"It's about the corners. Sarah, tell him."

Sarah told him. She told it plainly, the puddles, the line, the halfway, and when she finished, the channel was quiet for so long that Jack thought Paul had gone back to sleep.

"That water came out of my trough."

Mark kept his voice even. "Out of the corners. Not your fault. Nobody knew."

"I knew it leaked. I said so. I didn't know what a leak meant."

Paul cleared his throat. "Second one doesn't get corners. I'll bend the whole thing out of one sheet. No seams where the water sits. That's two days, not one."

"Take three. Here's the rest of it."

Mark took a breath and counted them off, the way he had on the board. "Nothing gets left wet on that road. Ever. The trough goes back to the cart on a tarp, so whatever comes out of it goes into the tarp, and the tarp comes home with it. Your crew waits at the last streetlight. Not at the cart."

"I'm allowed at the cart."

"You were allowed at the cart. It got halfway to the cart. Next time it gets all the way."

There was a pause on the line. Then Paul's voice, quieter. "Last streetlight."

"And nobody stops watching."

Mark looked at Sarah. "Not when the lid closes. Not when we're in the truck. Somebody watches that road until it's dry."

"Me."

"You and Aiden. Take turns."

He looked at Jack, and then at the crib, and almost smiled. "Doctor Mercer's going to call it work."

"She's going to lose."

Mark turned to go. Sarah held up a hand.

"There's one more. I wasn't going to show you tonight."

She closed the map and opened another feed. "I've done five roads. The northern ones are fine. Dry, every morning. But this one's the southern road. The last block, where the houses are. Look at the curb."

She ran the recording from that morning at speed. In the gray before sunrise, a thin dark line lay along the gutter, from the last driveway, down the curb, and out across the end of the asphalt into the soil.

As the light came, small black shapes came down out of the grass and settled on it. Flies. A dozen of them. More. Heavy and slow, walking along the wet like it was a table laid for them.

Jack got up off the floor. "That's not dew."

"It's there every morning. The seventh. The eighth. The ninth. Not before. Somebody on that block started watering their garden at night after the restrictions went up. In the dark, so nobody sees."

Lena had been listening. Her voice came back sharp. "That's my hundred and fifty liters."

Jack looked at the radio. "Some of it."

"Some of it. A hose running for an hour is a few hundred. Do that on six or seven blocks and it's the difference."

She was quiet for a moment, counting. "I said somebody's tomatoes were dying. I didn't think somebody was saving them."

Mark was still looking at the flies. "That line goes into the grass."

"Yes."

"And something follows it back out."

Nobody said anything.

"Lena. A notice. Tonight. Short."

Mark said it the way he wrote on the board. "No water poured or sprayed outside. Not on gardens, not on cars, not in the street. Anywhere, any hour. No reason given except that it matters."

"They'll want a reason."

"Then tell them it's my reason."

Lena didn't argue. Ten minutes later, Mark was still in the hall with his coat on. The notice came up on Sarah's tablet, and on Jack's wrist, and on every screen in town.

Utility notice. Do not pour or spray water outdoors, anywhere in the settlement, at any hour. This includes gardens. Questions to the utility station.

Sarah read it twice. "That's a lot of people's tomatoes."

Mark put his radio back on his belt. "I'll send Four to the last block in the morning. He knocks. He doesn't shout. He finds out who."

"And then what?"

"And then he helps them carry their tomatoes inside."

He opened the door. Cold came in around him. "Plants can live in a window. Nobody can live on that road."

He went out, and the door closed behind him, and the house was very quiet.

Jack sat down on the couch beside Sarah. She leaned against him without looking up from the feed. The southern road was dark on the screen, the last streetlight a small orange smudge at the top of the frame.

"Five roads."

"Five roads. Four to go."

She yawned, and hated that she had. "I'll do them tomorrow."

"You'll do them now."

"I'll do one now."

She did two.

March eleventh came up gray. Aiden read the door thermometer at a quarter to six and said ten degrees, and low cloud, and no stars, and Mark said no shake before anybody asked. Jack had known he would.

At nine, Unit Four called in from the southern road. His voice was careful and a little embarrassed.

"Unit Four. I found them. It's three houses, not one. One of them's a man named Pell. He's eighty-one. He says his wife planted those beans. He's watered them every evening since she died. When the water notice went up, he started doing it after dark, so nobody would see. Nobody told him the dark was different now."

Mark's voice came back slowly. "What did you tell him?"

"That it was. Then I carried the beans inside. He wanted them by the window, where she used to sit."

Unit Four hesitated. "He asked me whether it was true. About the spider."

"What did you say?"

"I said I'd seen it. And I said it doesn't come for people. It comes for water. So we don't give it any."

There was a long silence on the channel.

Then Mark's voice. "Good. That's the rule. Say it exactly like that, every door."

Sarah looked at Jack over the top of the tablet.

"He's going to say that to the whole town, isn't he?"

"Every door."

She went back to the feed. The southern curb was dry. Not a fly on it. She watched it anyway, a long time, the way you watch a pot that has finally stopped boiling over.
