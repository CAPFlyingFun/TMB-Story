# Chapter 19: No Lights

At noon, Lena's voice went out over every speaker in town again, as calm as a bus timetable. Six weeks of food, at careful portions. Twenty liters of water, in two windows. Nobody goes past the end of any road. She read it twice. When the speakers clicked off, somewhere across the street from the utility station a man shouted something that sounded like a question, and got no answer.

Behind the station, in a gravel yard between two tanks, Mark made them practice.

He laid the rope out on the ground in a long straight line and tied one end to a railing. Then he clipped Jack, Aiden and himself onto it at intervals, with a harness each from the security stores.

"The railing is the cart. The gravel is the road. That drain over there is the edge. Nobody goes past the drain."

Aiden looked at the drain. "It's a very small drain."

"Then it'll be very easy to stay away from."

They walked up to the drain and back. Then they did it again. Lena had set a bucket of water on the far side. Jack reached out over it with the pole, slowly, and soaked the sponge, and brought it back without letting it swing. Aiden held the funnel, and Mark twisted the sponge in both hands until it ran clear.

The first time, most of it went on Mark's boots.

The second time, most of it went in the jug.

The third time, Mark said a single word without warning, loud and flat. "Stop."

Jack froze with the pole halfway up. Aiden froze with the funnel against his chest. A drip ran down the pole and hung from the end of it, and nobody moved until Mark spoke again.

"Good. That's the word. When I say it, you're a fence post. You don't look around. You don't ask why. You stop."

"For how long?"

"Until I say go."

Aiden swallowed. "What if you don't say go?"

"Then you're a fence post for a very long time."

They did it eleven more times. By the last one, Jack's arms were shaking, and the bucket was nearly empty, and he could have done it in his sleep. Which, Mark pointed out, was the point.

The young officer the radio called Unit Four came by in the late afternoon to learn the knots. He was barely older than Aiden, with a careful way of standing, as though he expected to be told he was in the wrong place.

Mark handed him the end of the rope. "You hold this. You don't let go of it. If I yell pull, you pull."

"Yes, sir."

"If you see something coming that we don't, you say so. You don't wait to be sure."

"Yes, sir."

Mark looked at him for a second longer. "And you don't call me sir."

"Yes, sir. Sorry."

Doctor Mercer came to the house at eight, as she had promised, and set her bag on the kitchen table. Sarah was already sitting with her sleeve rolled up.

"You rested."

"I lay on that couch all day. I watched cameras. I watched Jack drill water into a bucket on Lena's feed. I've never been so bored in my life."

"Good. Keep it that way."

She wrapped the cuff around Sarah's arm and watched the numbers. Jack watched her face. It didn't change.

"Same as last night."

Sarah looked up. "Same as better, or same as high?"

"Same as high. Not worse. I'd have liked better. I'll take same."

Then she handed Sarah a small cup and a test strip and pointed down the hall. When Sarah came back, Doctor Mercer held the strip under the kitchen light for a long moment.

"No protein. That's good. That's the one I was watching for."

Sarah let out a breath. "So I can stop being bored?"

"No."

Doctor Mercer turned to Jack and held out her hand, palm up. He gave her his left one without being asked. She looked at the stitches, then at him.

"I hear you're going to be holding a wet pole in the morning."

"News travels."

She was already pulling a surgical glove out of her bag. "Everything travels. It's a very small town. If that gets wet, you come straight to me. Not after you've made your report. Straight to me."

She worked the glove over his hand, then a clear plastic bag over that, and taped it at the wrist.

"Yes, Doctor."

After she had gone, Sarah sat on the couch with her tablet propped on her knees and made him go through it again. The time. The rope. The cart. The word.

"And if the spider's out early?"

"We stop. We wait. If it doesn't go back, we come home."

"And if the radio goes quiet?"

"We come home."

"And if you see a bigger drop two meters farther in?"

He looked at her. "You've been talking to Lena."

"Lena told me everything. Answer the question."

"I leave it there."

She held his eyes for a long moment, and then she nodded, and set the tablet down, and leaned against him.

Jack thought about leaving her on this couch with a number that wouldn't come down, and going to stand under a spider. He didn't like the shape of it. He liked the shape of three weeks of water even less.

"Then sleep. You can't hold a pole straight if you've been awake for two days."

He meant to argue. He was asleep before he had figured out how.

The alarm went at a quarter to five. Mark was outside at half past, with the motor running and the headlights off.

The edge of town was completely dark. The streetlights burned along the inner roads, the way Lena had left them, but every porch light near the edge was out, and every window along the western blocks had its blinds down. Mark drove by the streetlights as far as they went. He drove the last stretch slowly, by memory, with a small red lamp cupped in one hand and pointed straight down at the road just ahead of the wheels.

Nobody spoke. Aiden sat in the back with the jug between his knees and the funnel in his lap. Unit Four sat beside him, holding the coil of rope like it might try to leave.

The white maintenance cart came out of the dark ahead of them, pale and square. Beyond it, there was nothing. Just a deeper black where the grass began.

Mark stopped twenty meters back and cut the motor.

For a moment, the only sound was the engine ticking as it cooled.

Then the rest of it arrived. A clicking somewhere in the stems, dry and slow, like someone tapping a fingernail on wood. A faint, steady hum that came and went. Far away, a long low creak, like a tree leaning in the wind.

Unit Four's voice was very small. "Is that normal?"

Mark kept his eyes on the dark. "Nothing is normal. That's just loud."

They got out without slamming a single door. Mark tied the rope off at the cart's tow hitch, tested it with his whole weight, then tested it again. Jack clipped in at the front, Aiden behind him, and Mark last, nearest the cart. Unit Four stood at the cart with a turn of the rope around the hitch and the end wrapped twice around his forearm, ready to haul.

Jack lifted his wrist and spoke as quietly as he could. "Station, this is Bennett. We're at the cart."

Lena answered at once. "Station hears you. Two minutes, starting now."

Sarah came on the line, softer. "I'm lying down. Ask anyone."

Jack smiled in the dark. "Morning."

"Morning. Be boring."

They walked forward together to the end of the asphalt, the three of them in a line, and stopped where the road stopped. Then they waited.

The sky went from black to charcoal. Charcoal to gray.

Somewhere out on the island, a bird began to sing. It was an ordinary song, bright and sweet, the kind Jack had heard every spring of his life. It went on and on, until he could hear it through Lena's channel on his wrist as well as through the air.

Aiden whispered. "That's a robin, I think."

Mark didn't look away from the grass. "Is it close?"

"I don't know. I can't tell anymore."

Lena's voice came in, right on time. "Two minutes. You all still there?"

"Still here. Still boring."

Out of the gray, the stems took shape. The wall of grass. The pale curve of a blade bending toward them over the road. And along the blade, one by one, the drops.

They came out of the dark the way stars do, not appearing but simply being there once you knew how to look. Small ones along the edges, a whole row of them, like beads on a wire. Then larger ones further up. And at the very tip of the nearest leaf, hanging out over the last meter of asphalt, three meters up, a single drop as tall as Jack's chest, round and heavy and perfectly still.

Aiden let out a breath. "That one's the plant's. At the tip."

Jack looked at the pole in his hands. Then at the drop. Then at the distance between them.

Four meters. Maybe less.

Above it, high in the stems, where the gray hadn't quite reached, something pale hung in the shadows. It didn't move.

Mark's voice was barely a breath. "Ten minutes of gray. Then the sun."

Jack set his boots right at the line where the road ended, the toes of his boots on the last finger's width of asphalt, and lifted the pole toward the water.
