# Chapter 44: The Crossing Plate

The seven pumps were humming when the cage went down, one above another, each on its platform against the shaft wall. Jack watched them go by. The drums, brimming. The floats, riding. Paul's second line on the far side, the same again. It had been a month since the shaft was a black hole in the soil. Now it sang.

At the bottom, the gallery opened around them, as enormous as it had been the first time. Jack had stopped expecting it to get smaller.

Mark plugged a reel of Paul's wire into the socket on the cage and clipped the reel to his belt, and it paid out behind him as they walked. Sarah came in over it, close and clear, from the couch at home, with Doctor Mercer listening in from the medical center. "I've got all three of you. I've got the bottom pump camera. And I've got the old drawing on the other half of the screen. Turn left at the pump. Follow the edge of the channel downstream."

"Copy."

They walked along the channel edge with the water running beside them, broad and fast and loud. Mark carried the lamp rack over his shoulder like a ladder. Aiden had the gas meter clipped to his chest. Jack had the rope.

The crossing plate came out of the dark about where Sarah said it would. A flat iron grid laid straight across the channel from edge to edge, bar after bar, each bar as wide as a sidewalk, and between the bars, gaps just as wide. Through the gaps, Jack could see the water racing underneath, white where it broke against the iron.

Aiden stopped at the edge of it. "That's farther than it looked on the map."

"Everything is."

Mark set down the lamp rack. "One bar. The middle one. Rope first."

Jack tied the rope off to the end of the bar, a long way down its length to the solid edge, and pulled it tight twice. Then he clipped his harness on.

"You first?"

"I'm the one who promised."

He stepped onto the bar.

It was wide enough. Wider than a sidewalk. That wasn't the problem. The problem was the gaps on either side, with the noise coming up through them and the water flashing past underneath, so fast it made the bar seem to move. Jack fixed his eyes on the far end of the bar, the way he would on a road, and walked.

Halfway, the bar changed under his boots. The iron there was dark and shining, wet with spray thrown up from the water below, and the rough rust had a skin of green slime on it. His left boot slid.

He dropped to one knee and grabbed the rope with both hands, and the bar was cold and slick under his palm, and the water roared through the gap a step to his right.

Sarah's voice was in his ear at once. "Jack. Stay down. Don't stand up on that. Go across it on your knees. Mark, tell Aiden. It's the middle third. It's wet where the spray comes up."

He went on his knees, one hand on the rope, a body length at a time, until the iron under him was dry and rough again. Then he stood up.

"Thank you."

"Tell the others to crawl."

He reached the far side. He tied the rope off there, and pulled it tight. Aiden came, slowly, and crawled the middle third on his knees without being asked twice. Then Mark, with the lamp rack lashed to his back, crawling too, swearing under his breath the whole way.

On the far bank, they walked back upstream, toward the shaft, along the edge of the water. The far wall rose up out of the dark beside them, pale and smooth, all the way up past where the light could go. Low down in it, square-cornered and black, was the opening.

It wasn't just a hole. As they came closer, Jack saw the frame. Metal. A thick square frame set into the wall, rusted brown, with a door hanging in it. The door had stuck halfway open, its bottom edge sunk into a drift of grit and dust, and through the gap between door and frame there was nothing but black.

Mark stopped them twenty steps short. "Lamps."

He carried the rack forward alone and stood it a few steps from the gap, facing in, braced on its pole. Then he stepped behind it and threw the switch.

Eight lamps came on at once. The doorway went white. Light poured through the gap into the room beyond, farther than their helmets could ever have reached, for the first time in Jack had no idea how long.

Something moved inside.

Something pale and long and many-legged, far down the room, low on the wall. It didn't come toward them. It drew back, away from the light, rippling, and went into a shadow at the far end and didn't come out.

Aiden let out his breath. "It's going deeper."

Sarah's voice was steady. "I saw it. Nothing else is moving. Aiden, the meter."

Aiden stepped up beside the lamp rack, close to the gap but not in it, and held the meter out at arm's length into the room. He waited. The numbers on the little screen climbed and settled.

He read them out. Then he read them again.

"Oxygen's low. Lower than out here. Carbon dioxide's high."

He pulled the meter back. "Not deadly. But not air I'd want to work in. Not for long."

Doctor Mercer's voice came over the wire from the medical center. "Then nobody goes in."

Mark was already looking at the floor. "It's dead air, lying on the floor. And the floor is where we breathe. So we give it a draft."

There was air moving in the gallery. There always was, a slow cold draft along the channel that came and went. Mark unrolled the tarp from his pack, and the three of them stretched it from the edge of the door frame out into the gallery at an angle, weighting the bottom with chunks of grit, so that it caught the draft along the channel like a sail and turned it in through the gap.

The tarp filled, and sagged, and filled again.

A breath of air came out of the room past the other side of the door. Cold, dead, and smelling of rust and old stone.

"Now we wait."

They waited. An hour by Mark's watch, sitting on the gallery floor with their backs to the wall beside the door, while the gallery's air moved slowly into the room and the room's air moved slowly out. Every five minutes Aiden held the meter in through the gap and read it out.

Low. Low. Better. Better.

At the hour, he read it twice, and looked up.

"That's air."

Sarah let out a breath. "That's air."

Doctor Mercer was quiet for a moment. "Then go and look. And keep that box on."

Jack stood up, and walked around the lamp rack, and stepped through the door.

Inside, the room was narrower than the gallery, and lower. Lower enough that the lamps found the ceiling, a flat gray ceiling of old concrete still far above them, cracked in places, with long pale stains running across it. The floor was tiles, but not the gallery's great square tiles. Smaller ones. Older. Laid by hand, some of them, crooked, with the cement between them crumbling.

And on the wall just inside the door, high up, far above their heads, where anyone would have put it, before, there was paint. Faded, and flaking, but there. Large stenciled characters.

A number.

Mark tilted the lamp rack up toward it. In Jack's ear, Sarah caught her breath.

"Jack. That's it. That's the number on the drawing."
