# Chapter 42: The Field Book

By the end of the first week, the Field Book was the thickest thing in the house.

Sarah kept it on the arm of the couch, where she could reach it without getting up. Every sheet that came in, she read, and copied, and marked on her map. Little colored dots at the ends of the nine roads. Green for flies. Brown for slugs. A black dot with a ring around it for the night hunter, always at the western road, seen going home at first gray. A red dot for the day spider. And at the southern road, two small blue marks, close together, for the robin.

Jack sat on the floor with the map in his lap one evening and looked at it for a long time.

"It's a timetable."

Sarah didn't look up from the sheet she was copying. "It's a timetable. The flies come at first gray, every wet road, on every tip that hangs over asphalt. The day spider comes out when the light does. The robin comes at mid-morning, and only at the southern road, because the soil's open there and it can see. The slugs come on damp nights; you can see their trails at gray. The night hunter goes home at first gray, every time."

She turned the page. "And the northern roads are dry."

"Why?"

"Aiden thinks the ground falls away from the road there. The water runs off before anything comes to drink it."

She shrugged. "It's a guess. But it's a guess we can check now, because it's the same every day."

At the morning meeting, Paul reported the roof. Tarps on poles along the open stretch of the line walk, painted the color of dead grass, low enough to walk under bent over. Unit Four had walked it every morning since. The robin had landed near it twice. Both times it had hopped right past, cocking its head at the soil, and hadn't looked at the roof at all.

Mark nodded slowly. "It sees a roof."

Paul shook his head. "It sees dead grass. Which it's not interested in. I asked Aiden."

Mark's arms stayed folded. "It hides us. It doesn't stop it. Nobody forgets that."

Then Lena turned her clipboard to the kitchens' page. "Food. The kitchens counted again on Monday. With what the greenhouse is giving now there's water for it, careful portions go to the end of the month, maybe a few days past. Aiden's beans in the back hall are up. Nothing to eat off them for three weeks."

Sarah wrote it down. End of April. Beans, three weeks. "Then food is the next list."

"Food is the next list."

Doctor Mercer came on the ninth. She took Sarah's pressure and wrote it down, and then she sat back and looked at her for a moment over her glasses.

"Thirty-seven weeks. That's early term. If he came now, I wouldn't be worried."

Sarah let out a breath she seemed to have been holding for a month. "He's not coming now. He's comfortable."

"Head down. Pressure holding."

Doctor Mercer packed her bag. "Good. I'd still like him to stay where he is a little longer. Another two weeks would suit me fine. But if he doesn't, we're ready. That's a sentence I didn't think I'd say in this town."

On the tenth, at mid-morning, Mr. Pell's sheet for the eastern road came in, and Sarah read it twice and called Mark before she had finished.

Three blocks in from the end of the eastern road, behind the community center's back door, the kitchens kept their bins. On the road-end camera, in the gray daylight, there was a line on the asphalt, coming out of the stems and away up the street toward the town. A dark, moving line.

Ants.

Each one knee-high to a man. Dark and narrow-waisted, walking in single file, every one exactly behind the one in front, out of the grass, across the road end and up the street, three blocks, to the bins. Some were coming back the other way, carrying something pale in their jaws.

Mark saw it on Sarah's tablet in the hall, with his coat still on. "How long's that been there?"

"Mr. Pell says there were two at first gray. He drew them. Then there were ten."

Sarah pointed at the line. "Now there's that."

Aiden was on the channel. "They follow a scent. The first one to find food lays a trail on the way home, and the others walk on the trail, and every one that finds food lays more. It's not that the bins are interesting. It's that one ant found them, and told everyone."

"Then we take the bins away."

"And the trail. If the trail's still there, they'll keep coming."

They took the bins away. Paul's crew sealed every one and carried them inside, into the kitchen's cold room, and the kitchen staff swore at them and then helped. Then Paul came back with a tank of soapy gallery water on the flatbed and a pair of long brooms, and he and Mark scrubbed the street from the bins to the end of the road, slowly, from behind the line of ants, never in front of it.

The ants walked around the brooms. They walked to where the bins had been, and stopped, and walked in circles, and walked back.

By the twelfth, there were three left on the camera at first gray. By the thirteenth, none.

Mark wrote it on the board. Bins sealed. Inside. Nothing left out that smells.

Sarah wrote it in the book. Ants. Eastern road. One found us. Then everyone.

The outpost sheets came in every evening with Unit Four, folded in quarters, with whatever the camera Paul had left on the bottom pump had seen. Most of them said the same thing: line walked, pumps running, nothing on the soil, nothing on the walls. But twice in the week, the last line was different.

Centipede on the far wall. Came down, crossed the tiles near the channel, went back. Left by the opening.

And again, three days later.

Centipede on the far wall. Left by the opening.

Sarah read them both again. Then she laid them side by side on the couch and looked at them.

"They live in there."

Jack looked at the sheets. "Mark said nobody goes near it."

"I'm not going near it. I'm going to the records building."

She went the next night, the thirteenth, in the truck, with the good chair, and Jack carried it down the stairs the same way he had the first time, walking backward in front of her. The gray cabinet's green light was still on. She sat down in front of it and opened the drawings, and found the southern seam, and the gallery, and the far wall, solid all the way along, exactly as it had always been.

"Everything else down there matches. Every tile. Every rung."

She scrolled along the wall, slowly. "Except that."

Then she found the menu she hadn't looked for the first time.

Revisions.

There were three. The drawing they had been using was the newest. Sarah opened the oldest.

The gallery came up the same. The channel, the shaft, the tiles. The far wall.

But in this one, low in the far wall, exactly where the opening was, a door was drawn. A small square door, with a line through it to show it opened. And behind it, where the newest drawing showed nothing but solid ground, a chamber. A long narrow room, running back from the wall into the dark blue of the soil, with a label in the corner in small square letters.

Module.

And a number Jack had never seen on any of the other drawings.

He frowned at it. "The modules are whole sections of the island. That's a room."

Sarah didn't take her eyes off it. "Or a very small one. A module somebody stopped drawing."

Sarah didn't say anything for a long time. Then she opened the newest drawing again beside it. The wall. Solid. No door. No room. No number.

"It was there."

Her voice was very quiet. "It was on the drawing. And then it wasn't."
