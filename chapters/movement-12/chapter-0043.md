# Chapter 43: Not This Month

Sarah put the tablet on the table at eight o'clock and turned it so everyone could see.

She didn't say anything. She let them look. The gallery on the oldest drawing, the channel and the shaft and the tiles, the far wall. And in the far wall, a small square door, with a line through it. And behind the door, a long narrow room, and the label in the corner.

Module. And a number.

Then she swiped, and the newest drawing came up beside it. The same wall. Solid. Nothing behind it at all.

Mark was the first to speak. He didn't sound surprised. He sounded like a man who had been waiting a week to hear it.

"So it's a door."

Sarah folded her hands on top of her stomach. "It was a door. On one drawing. Three revisions. The oldest has it. The two newer ones don't. Nothing in the archive says why."

Lena leaned in and studied the label. "I don't know that number."

Aiden was looking at the room behind the door. "That's where the centipedes go. Every sheet. They come out of that wall and they go back into it."

Paul spoke from his crate without uncrossing his arms. "So what. Let them have it."

"That's the question."

Sarah looked around the table. "Here's what I know. Behind that wall, there's a room nobody's been in. It's a few minutes' walk from the pumps that keep this town in water. Every pump crew that goes down there works with their back to it. And something that hunts in the dark lives in it."

She paused. "I don't want to know what's in it because I'm curious. I am curious. But that's not why."

Mark looked at her for a long moment.

He rubbed his jaw. "I said not this month."

Then he looked at the calendar on the wall.

"It's April."

He set the eraser down. "Here's how. Daylight hours only. Three people. Lights. The gas meter goes first, at the door, before anybody does. Sarah on every camera. And the moment anything's wrong, anything at all, everybody comes back. Nobody argues. Nobody finishes what they were doing. They come back."

Jack nodded. So did Aiden.

Paul unfolded his arms at last. "And how do you get across the channel? It's wider than the station yard. I'm not building a bridge over that."

"You don't have to."

Sarah swiped again. The oldest drawing, the gallery, and a little downstream from the shaft, a long thin rectangle laid straight across the channel, with a tiny note in square letters beside it. Crossing plate. "It's on the oldest one. And the newest one. And it's on Aiden's scanner. We just never went that way."

Aiden pulled the scanner map up on his own tablet and zoomed. There it was, in gray dots, a long flat grid spanning the water, just beyond where the light from the bottom pump had reached.

"It's a grating. Like the one over the shaft. Laid over the channel so the people who built it could walk across."

He looked up. "The gaps are wide, for us. But the bars are wide too. You could walk across on one."

Paul grunted. "On one bar. Over that."

"With a rope. Tied off at both ends. Like the road."

Mark had already turned to the board. "What about the things that live there?"

Aiden had thought about that. "Every time the bottom pump's light has come on, the ones on the far wall have gone back into the dark. Every time so far. They like dark and damp. That's why they live in walls and under stones. A strong light at the door might send them deeper instead of out."

He hesitated. "Might. That's what I've seen, a few times, on a camera. It's not a promise."

Mark didn't take his eyes off him. "Then nobody treats it like one. The light's one thing. The rope, the meter, the three of us and coming back are the rest."

"How strong?"

"Stronger than a helmet."

Paul looked at the ceiling of the back room as if he could see straight through it to the machine shop. "Work lamps. Eight of them. On a frame, on a pole, so you can stand it in a doorway and stand behind it."

He nodded to himself. "Tomorrow."

Doctor Mercer had come to the meeting, as she sometimes did now, and had listened to all of it from the chair by the window. Now she turned to Jack.

"You're going."

"I'm going."

"Then you'll hear my conditions as well as his."

She held up one finger. "Sarah stays at home. Not the station. Home. Four minutes from my door."

A second finger. "Your wrist is never off. Not for a second. If she calls, you answer. If you don't answer, she calls me, and then I come and find you, and that's worse for you than any centipede."

Sarah's mouth twitched. "She means it."

"I know she means it."

Doctor Mercer held up a third finger, and then looked at it, and seemed to decide it wasn't needed. She put her hand down and picked up her bag. "That's all."

Paul's lamp rack was ready the next afternoon. Eight square work lamps, bolted in two rows to a frame on a pole twice Jack's height, with a battery pack at the bottom and a handle on each side. Paul switched it on in the machine shop, and the whole room went white, and everyone flinched.

Paul held a hand up against the glare. "Stand that in a door, and nothing sees past it but you."

That night, Sarah lay on the couch with the old revision on the tablet, the way she had lain with the gallery map before the first descent. She had the room open. The long narrow chamber. The door at the near end, in the far wall of the gallery.

And at the other end of the room, the drawing just stopped. The chamber's walls ran off the edge of the sheet, and in the corner, in the same small square letters, there was a line Jack hadn't noticed.

Continued.

"Continued where?"

Sarah shook her head. "That's the thing. There's no next sheet. Not in any revision. It just says continued, and then there isn't anything."

Jack sat down on the floor beside the couch, the way he always did, and looked at the word for a while.

"We're not going past the end of that room."

"No. You're going to the door, and into the room, and you're going to look. Then you come home."

She put the tablet down on her stomach and looked at the ceiling.

"And Jack. When you get to the end of it. If there's a door, you leave it shut."

"I leave it shut."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I'll leave it shut, Sarah."

"Good."

She closed her eyes. "Then I can sleep."
