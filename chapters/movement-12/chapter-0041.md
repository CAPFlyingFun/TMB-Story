# Chapter 41: What Eats What

Aiden had a scraping from the gnat's gut on a glass slide under the greenhouse microscope. The rest of it, as long as his arm, lay in a tray beside him. He turned the screen so Jack and Sarah could see without leaning.

Sarah had come in the truck. She sat on a stool by the bench with her back against a stack of seed trays, which was as much as Doctor Mercer had agreed to.

"That's its gut."

Aiden pointed with a pencil at a pale smear of something. "I touched a test strip to it. Sugar. The water the plants push out of their leaf tips at night, the same sticky water you see dried there at first light, when the flies come. That's what it lives on."

Jack looked at the smear. "So the plants feed the gnats."

"And the gnats get stuck in the drops, and they kick, and the spider takes the ones that kick."

Aiden sat back. "And then the spider drinks the drops. It's all one thing. It's one machine, and we're standing in the middle of it trying to steal the water."

Sarah didn't take her eyes off the screen. "How much of that do you know, and how much do you think?"

Aiden hesitated. "That's the problem."

He pulled a box out from under the bench. It was full of paper. Scraps, receipts, the backs of supply sheets, a page torn out of a seed catalog. On every one, in his small careful handwriting, was something he had seen. A date. A time. A slug, at the western road end, seven meters, eating something. Flies at the eastern road end, about forty, on the tips. A moth on the station's window, dead. A shape at the northern road, gone before he could say what.

"A month of it. Everything I've seen. Everything anyone's told me."

He looked at the box with something like despair. "But I saw it when I happened to be looking, and I wrote it down when I happened to have paper. I don't know what's normal. I don't know what's rare. I don't know if the slugs came because it was damp, or because I only ever look when it's damp."

Sarah was already turning to a clean page in her notebook.

"Then we look at the same things, at the same times, the same way, every day. And we write it down the same way."

Aiden blinked at her.

"Nine road ends. Nine cameras. Four times a day."

Her pen moved. "First gray, because that's when the drops are there and everything comes out. Mid-morning. Late afternoon. And dark, as long as there's a light to see anything by. Every time, somebody watches each camera for ten minutes and writes down everything that moves. What it is, if they know. How big, against something we know. What it's doing. Where it goes."

"On one sheet."

"On one sheet. The same sheet, every time, every road."

She looked up. "And nobody goes out to look. Nobody gets closer. It's watching. Only watching."

Jack heard Doctor Mercer's word in that and didn't say anything.

Aiden looked at the box of scraps, and then at Sarah's page, and Jack watched his face change, the way it had over the jumping spider the first morning. Not fear this time. Something else.

"Who watches? That's thirty-six watches a day."

"People who can't carry hoses."

Sarah tapped the page. "People with bad backs. People who are eighty-one and don't want to move house. The woman from the kitchens, with her grandchildren at home and bored out of their minds. They watch from their couches. I watch from mine. I keep the book."

"What do we call it?"

Sarah shrugged. "It's your box."

Aiden thought about it. "The Field Book. That's what it is. A field book. Except we can't go into the field."

"Then it's a field book from a couch."

"It's the Field Book."

Lena put the sheet together that night and sent it round: a plain grid with the road names down one side, four columns of times across the top, and a box for what, how big, doing what. Mark looked at it on his wrist, and then signed his name at the bottom of the first one, under the northern road, mid-morning.

"I'll take one. Somebody should who's seen the things."

By the next morning the sheets were coming in. Mr. Pell watched the southern road at first gray, and wrote in a small, very upright hand. Two slugs, long as a bus. Flies, too many to count, on the tips. A thing like a black car, walking. He meant the ground beetle. Sarah read every sheet, and copied every line into the book, and drew small marks on her map of the road ends.

At mid-morning on the sixth, she was watching the southern road herself.

Jack was in the kitchen when she called him. Not loudly. That was what made him drop the cup.

"Jack. Come here. Now. Slowly."

He came. She had the tablet held up in both hands.

The southern road end, in full daylight. The asphalt, the crack, the soil beyond it. The hose line running off between the stems toward the grate, with Unit Four's orange stakes standing beside it.

And on the soil, past the end of the road, standing in the open between two clumps of grass, there was a bird.

It filled the frame. It was taller than any building in town. Brown on top, and a breast the color of rust, and one round black eye, as wide as the station's front door, turned sideways toward the ground. It stood perfectly still. Then it hopped. The ground under the camera shook with it.

Jack had heard it sing every morning since the town shrank. He had never once thought about what it was.

"It's the robin."

Sarah didn't move. "It's hunting."

The robin cocked its head. Then again, the other way. That one huge eye looked at the soil, at something Jack couldn't see. Then it looked at something he could.

Beside the hose, about a third of the way out, the ground beetle was walking. Black and shining. The thing that had made four grown men freeze in the stems. It walked along the line, its feelers sweeping, exactly the way it always did.

The robin struck.

It was too fast to see. One moment its beak was in the air, and the next it was down, and when it came up again the beetle was in it, legs going. The robin beat it twice against the soil. Then it tipped its head back once, swallowed, and looked at the soil again.

Then it hopped twice more, closer to the hose, and cocked its head at the stakes.

Sarah's voice was very quiet. "Where's Four?"

Jack was already on his wrist. "Unit Four. Where are you?"

The answer came back at once, calm. "At the outpost. Inside. With the door shut. I heard it land."

"Stay there."

"I'm staying."

The robin looked at the stakes for a long time. At the orange cloth on top of each one, flapping a little. Then it lost interest, the way the beetle had lost interest in the wet joint, and it opened its wings, and the whole camera frame went dark with them for a second, and it was gone.

Sarah put the tablet down very carefully on the couch beside her.

"Every morning. Four, and whoever's with him. Walking that line in the open. In daylight."

"In daylight. Because the night's when the hunters come out."

"The night's when the hunters we knew about come out."

She picked up her pen. Her hand wasn't quite steady. She wrote anyway. Robin. Southern road. Mid-morning. Took the beetle. One strike. "Aiden was right. It's one machine. And we didn't know where the top of it was."

At the morning meeting, Mark stood in front of Sarah's sheet for the southern road with his arms folded for a long time.

"Line walk goes under the stems where it can. Where it can't, it goes fast and it goes together."

He turned to Paul. "And I want a roof over the open stretch. Anything. Tarps on poles. Something a bird looks at and sees a roof."

Paul looked at the sheet, and at the drawing of the robin Mr. Pell had made beside his tally, careful and frightened and very good.

"A roof I can do."

Sarah wrote it in the book. Then she turned to a fresh page and wrote the date at the top, April seventh, and a line under it.

Day two of the Field Book.
