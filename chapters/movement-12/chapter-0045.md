# Chapter 45: Continued

Mark brought the lamp rack in through the door and stood it just inside, and the room came out of the dark in front of them, long and narrow and low, running straight back into the island.

Nobody said anything for a while. They just looked.

It was older than the gallery. Jack didn't know how he knew that, only that he did. The gallery was clean lines and huge square tiles. This was smaller tiles, uneven, set by hand, with the cement between them crumbled to sand. Iron brackets stood out of the walls in rows, high above them, rusted and bare, holding nothing. Whatever they had held was gone.

Aiden turned slowly, his helmet light following the brackets, and didn't say anything at all.

Sarah's voice came over the wire. "Keep the scanner on. I'm laying it over the drawing as you go."

They walked.

A third of the way in, the floor dipped, and the dip was full of water. Still, black, flat as glass. It lay across the room from one wall nearly to the other, and when Jack put his light on it he couldn't see the bottom.

Aiden crouched at the edge. "It's not deep, for the island. It's a puddle. For us it's a pond."

Mark looked at it, and then at the dry strip running along the left-hand wall, between the water and the wall. "Ledge. One at a time. Back to the wall."

They went along it one at a time, backs to the cold wall, the water close beside their boots, black and still. Jack didn't look at it. He looked at the brackets far above.

Halfway along, Sarah's voice came over the wire. "Stop. Jack, the strip ahead of you. On the scan, it's lower than the rest, and it's darker. On the old drawing there's a drain in the floor right there, against the wall. That's where the water comes in."

Jack stopped. A few steps ahead, the dry tiles went dark and shining, and beyond the dark patch, they were dry again.

"Go wide of it. Step over it. Don't put a boot on it."

Aiden spoke from behind him, low. "And if anyone goes in, the water holds on. Like it held the pole. You won't climb out on your own."

They stepped over it, one at a time, a long stride with a hand on the wall. Nobody put a boot on the dark.

Past the water, the ceiling changed. A long crack ran across it, and along the crack a great slab of concrete had dropped and hung there, sagging, the height of a house lower than the rest. And below it, on the floor, lay what had already come down: a heap of broken concrete, each piece the size of a car, piled up against the wall.

Mark stopped them well short of it and looked up at the slab.

"That's going to come down. Not today, probably. But it's going to."

He pointed along the floor, past the heap, to where the room went on. "One at a time. On my count. Fast. Don't stop under it for anything."

He went first. Then Aiden. Then Jack, on Mark's count of three, running across the tiles with the heap on his left and the dark slab overhead, and he didn't breathe until he was out from under it.

Sarah's voice was steady. "All three. Good. Keep going."

Beyond the fallen ceiling, in a damp corner under the rubble, Aiden stopped again. There was something on the floor there. Pale, thin, papery shapes, lying in a heap, each one long and curved and jointed. And around them, scattered, the dry husks of other things. Wings. Legs. Bits of shell.

Aiden knelt and turned one of the papery shapes over with the end of his pencil. It was hollow. It had legs, all of them, empty.

"It's a skin. A centipede's. They grow out of them and leave them behind."

He looked around at the husks. "And these are what it's eaten. This is where it lives. It's a den. Not a way through. They come out into the gallery to hunt, and they come home here."

Jack looked at the corner, and at the shadow at the far end of the room where the pale thing had gone when the lamps came on.

"Is it still in here?"

"Somewhere. Back there, where it's darkest."

Aiden stood up. "It won't come toward the light."

"You keep saying that."

"I keep hoping it's true."

Something shifted in the dark at the back of the room, a dry rustle like paper. Nobody looked. They kept walking.

Sarah came in over the wire, and her voice had changed again. Not frightened. Something else.

"Jack. Everything fits. Every wall, every bracket, the water, even the crack in the ceiling. It's the drawing. The old one. It's exact."

She paused. "And it says the room goes on. Past the end of the sheet. Go to the end."

They went to the end.

The room narrowed a little. The tiles stopped. The floor became bare concrete, and then the light found a wall across the whole end of the room.

Not a wall. A door.

A great steel bulkhead filled the end of the chamber, from floor to ceiling, dark and riveted and pitted with rust. In the middle of it, far above them, was a wheel, the kind you turned to seal a hatch, its spokes as thick as tree trunks. And painted across the bulkhead, in the same faded stencil, was the same number they had seen inside the door.

Nobody said anything.

Mark lowered the lamp rack slowly until it stood on the concrete, and they stood behind it and looked up at the wheel.

Sarah's voice came through Paul's wire. "That's where the drawing says continued. Right there. Exactly there."

A pause. "Jack, this isn't the end of the room. It's the end of what anybody drew."

Jack looked at the wheel for a long time. He thought about the gallery, which had been exactly where the drawing said. About the opening, which had been on one drawing and then not. About the water and the den and the ceiling waiting to fall.

Mark spoke first.

"Nobody opens that."

Jack didn't argue. He found he didn't want to. "Nobody opens it."

Aiden let out a long breath. "I wasn't going to ask."

Mark was already turning the lamps away. "Photograph it. Scan it. Every rivet. Then we go back the way we came, one at a time, under the ceiling, around the water. And we leave this exactly as we found it."

They did. Aiden scanned the bulkhead from one side to the other, and Jack photographed it, and the wheel, and the number, until Sarah said she had enough. Then they went back. Under the slab on Mark's count. Along the ledge with their backs to the wall. Out through the door into the gallery, where the lamps had made a white patch on the tiles, and the pumps were still humming upstream, and the channel ran on in the dark as if nothing had happened.

Before they crossed back, Mark took one lamp off the rack and bolted it into an old bracket on the door frame, shining into the room, on its own battery, to be charged from the pump line by day.

Aiden watched the bottom pump camera from the outpost all that afternoon.

"They're staying in. Back by the den. Nothing's come out past the light."

He thought about it. "Mostly. Light draws insects. Insects draw hunters. We'll watch it."

That evening Doctor Mercer came to the house at eight, as she did, and took Sarah's pressure, and wrote it down.

"Thirty-eight weeks. Holding. Head down."

She looked at Sarah over her glasses. "You've had a busy week for a woman who's supposed to be sitting down."

"I was sitting down. On the couch. The whole time."

"You were running an expedition from the couch."

"Sitting down."

Doctor Mercer almost smiled. When she had gone, Sarah turned the tablet toward Jack. On the screen was the last photograph he had taken. The bulkhead, rusted and dark, with the wheel high up in the middle of it, and the faded number painted across it.

"Somebody's going to open that one day."

"Not us."

"No."

She looked at the wheel for a long moment. Then she put her hand on her stomach, where the baby was turning slowly, the way he did in the evenings now. "But somebody."
