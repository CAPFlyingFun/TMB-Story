# Chapter 25: One Shake

The leaf was heavier than anything in the drill.

Jack had expected it to lift like a branch. It didn't. When the padded fork took its weight, the whole blade pressed down on it, cold and soft and much heavier than it looked, and the pole bowed in his hands. Every bead along the edges trembled. The drop at the tip swung a little, the way a lamp swings on a chain.

Mark's voice was right at his ear. "Slow."

Jack pushed. The blade rose, a hand's width, then another. It bent around the fork like a wet sheet on a line. He could feel it straining to go back down.

"Now."

He let go.

The blade didn't fall. It sprang. It snapped up past where it had been and then back, out over the end of the road toward them, so fast that it whistled.

The drop at its tip didn't fall straight. It flew.

It came off the end of the leaf and straight at them, a round heavy shape as big as a person curled up, and hit the splash board with a boom that Jack felt in his teeth. The board shuddered. The water sheeted down the inside of it, thick and clear, and dropped into the trough. It rolled from one end to the other and back, slapping at the lips, climbing them and sliding back down.

It stayed in.

Then the rest came down. All along the blade, the small beads let go at once, a long shower of glass marbles. Most of them hit the asphalt in front of the trough and burst into flat, shivering puddles. A few hit the board. One came over the top of it and landed on Jack's shoulder like a wet sandbag and ran down his arm.

He looked down at his left hand.

The water ran down the pole toward the glove, fast and thin. It reached Paul's rubber collar and stopped. It gathered there, swelled and dripped off the edge of the cup onto the road. Not one drop went past it.

From the cart, very quietly, Jack heard Paul let out a breath.

Then the stems moved.

The shaken blade hadn't stopped. It swung back and forth, smaller and smaller, and the stem it grew from swayed with it. And the stem next to it. And the one above that, where the silk retreat hung.

The pale bag swayed.

Mark's voice cracked out, low and flat. "Stop."

Jack stopped. The pole was still in his hands, held up over the top of the board, the fork dripping above the trough. Mark stopped behind him. At the cart, the rope creaked once as Unit Four took up the slack, and then even the rope was still.

The retreat swayed. Slowed. Hung still.

Something moved at the opening. The tip of a leg. Dark, jointed, thick as Jack's wrist. It came out of the silk a little way and rested on the stem.

Jack didn't move. He didn't blink. The cold came up through his boots, and the water on his shoulder soaked slowly through his jacket, and he stayed exactly where he was.

Sarah's voice came over his wrist, so faint that he almost didn't hear it. "Four degrees. It's four degrees, Jack."

The leg stayed where it was for a long time. Long enough for Jack to count his own heartbeat. Twenty. Thirty.

Then it drew back into the silk, slowly, and was gone.

Nobody moved for another ten seconds. Then Mark's breath went out against the back of Jack's neck.

"Go. Lid. Slow."

Jack lowered the pole over the board and laid it on the asphalt. Then he and Mark went around either end of the trough to the lid. Jack looked down into it as he went.

The water lay in the bottom of the trough, clear and heavy, its surface curved up at the walls. And in it, near the far end, something small and dark was kicking. A gnat, the size of a cat, its wings plastered flat, beating its legs against the inside of the water. It must have come down with the shower.

Its buzzing was a thin, frantic whine. To Jack it sounded very loud.

Aiden's voice came on the channel, low and urgent. "Close it. Close it now. A thing kicking in open water is exactly what it looks for."

Mark took the first lid section and swung it over. It settled onto the rubber strips along the rim, slow and even. The water rose up to meet it from underneath and touched it, and held there, and the strips held too. Jack did the second section. Mark did the third. The whine of the gnat went on under the metal for a moment, small and muffled, and then it stopped.

Jack looked up at the retreat. Nothing showed at the opening.

Mark kept his eyes on the opening. "Lena. Time."

"Six thirty-eight."

Mark looked at the trough, then at the sky, then at the trough again. "Four minutes. We don't carry that. Not full. If somebody slips, it goes over, and we're standing in it. I said nothing gets dragged. I'd rather loud than wet. Four, throw me the spare line. Paul, help him pull. Everybody else, steady it. Walk it back."

Unit Four threw the spare line from the cart in one long, low loop, and it landed at Mark's feet. Jack and Mark unclipped from the main rope, ran the spare line through both handles on their own side of the trough and knotted it. At the cart, Unit Four and Paul and Paul's two men took hold of the other end. Jack and Mark set their hands on the lid.

"Pull. Slow."

The rope came tight. The trough moved.

It slid back across the asphalt on its steel feet with a long low grating sound, and inside it the water moved too. Jack felt it through the lid under his palm, a heavy weight rolling toward the back wall, then toward the front, thumping against the metal like something trying to get out. At every corner a little thin water squeezed out between the rubber and the rim and ran down the sides onto the road.

"Slow. Slower."

Halfway to the cart, the grass behind them changed color.

Jack didn't turn around. He didn't have to. He saw it in Mark's face, which had gone gold on one side.

Lena's voice came in, very calm. "Six forty-two. Sun's on the tops. You're off the edge. Keep coming."

The trough came the last few meters to the cart. Paul's men dropped the line and caught it by the handles. And then, at last, standing beside the cart with their backs against the cold white side of it, they turned around.

The sun had reached halfway down the wall of stems. Every drop it touched lit up at once, gold and blazing. Above the place where they had stood, the silk retreat glowed like a paper lantern.

The spider came out of it legs first, the way it had on the cold mornings. It climbed down the stem in short, careful steps, and stopped in the light, and was still.

It sat there in the sun. It didn't look at them. It didn't look at anything. It sat with its legs drawn in, warming up.

Paul was standing beside Jack, his hands back in his pockets. He watched it for a long time.

"That's the thing."

Jack didn't take his eyes off it. "That's the thing."

Paul cleared his throat. "It's smaller than I thought. No. It isn't. I was hoping."

On the way back to the station, nobody spoke for a long time. Then Jack remembered, and stripped the bag and the glove off his left hand on the back seat, and held it up to the window. Dry. The tape was dry. The stitches were pink and quiet under it.

His wrist buzzed.

Sarah's voice was warm. "Did you take it off?"

"I took it off."

"Good. I was about to call Doctor Mercer on you."

At the utility station, Paul backed the flatbed up to the loading platform. He had built a drain spigot into one end of the trough, a short brass spout with a handle. He knelt by it with a row of empty twenty-liter jugs waiting, and Lena with her clipboard, and Mark in the doorway where he always stood.

Paul turned the handle.

The water came out clear and cold and steady. It filled the first jug, and Lena marked it. Then the second. Then the third, and the fourth, and the fifth. Halfway up the sixth, it slowed to a trickle, and to a drip, and stopped.

Lena counted the jugs twice. Then she wrote the number down.

"A hundred and ten liters."

Jack looked at the row of jugs. A hundred and ten. Nine times what they had nearly drowned Aiden for. From one shake of one leaf. And it had been the smaller drop.

Paul sat back on his heels. He didn't say anything. He took the rubber strip at the corner of the lid between his finger and thumb, and pressed it down, the whole length of it.

Then Aiden's voice came through Lena's console. He didn't sound pleased. He sounded careful, the way he had sounded on the first morning, looking at the greenhouse glass.

"Doctor Bennett. I've been going back over the feed, up to where you closed the lid. The leg didn't come out when the stem moved. The stem had been moving for a good five seconds by then. It came out two seconds after that gnat hit the water and started kicking. It wasn't watching us. It wasn't watching the shake. It saw breakfast. And it was too cold to come and get it."

Aiden was quiet for a moment.

"Every leaf we shake is going to shake one down."
