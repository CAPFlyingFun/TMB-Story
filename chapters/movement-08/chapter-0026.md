# Chapter 26: Wet Asphalt

Aiden got to the station a quarter of an hour later, out of breath. He had a glass jar in one hand and a pair of long kitchen tongs in the other.

Paul was still kneeling by the trough. He looked at the tongs. "Those are mine."

"They were in the station kitchen."

"They were mine before that."

Mark lifted the first lid section himself, slowly, the way he had closed it. It came up clean on the rubber strips and didn't drag any water with it. There was hardly any water left to drag. A thin film lay across the bottom of the trough, and at the far end, pressed flat against the metal where the last of it had drained away, lay the gnat.

It didn't move. Its wings were stuck down along its back, and its legs were folded under it.

Aiden reached in with the tongs and lifted it out, carefully, the way Jack had once seen a jeweler lift a stone. It was the size of a cat and it weighed almost nothing. He laid it in the jar and screwed the lid on and held it up to the window.

Lena watched him over her clipboard. "What do you want it for?"

Aiden looked at the jar. "I want to know what it is. And what it was eating, before it got stuck in a drop. If the spider eats gnats, and gnats eat something, I'd like to know what the something is. Everything out there is eating something. We just don't know the list yet."

Mark had his whiteboard out again. He had wiped it down to the four words in the corner, Cold, Clear, Gray, Covered, and under them he had drawn a line down the middle.

"What worked."

Jack counted it off. "The board. It came straight at us, and the board took it."

Mark wrote Board.

"The collar. My glove came back dry."

Paul didn't look up. "Told you."

Collar. Lid. Strips.

Unit Four spoke from the doorway, a little hesitantly. "Walking it back on the rope. Nobody had to carry it."

Mark wrote Rope, and then he looked at Unit Four for a moment longer than he needed to. "That was a good thing to do twice. Tying off the hitch."

Unit Four went red and didn't say anything.

Mark moved the marker across the line. "What didn't."

Jack didn't need to think. "We set it in the wrong place. In the dark, we put it under the biggest drop, and the biggest drop is under the front door. We spent two minutes of gray moving it. Two minutes we didn't have."

Mark wrote Moved it. Lost time.

Lena turned her clipboard around. "And it leaked. Not much. Out of the corners, when you walked it back. There's a wet line from the end of the road to the cart. I saw it on the feed while you were coming in."

Paul sat back on his heels. "I'll seal the corners. And I'll build you a second one. Narrower. So two people can lift it full, if it comes to that."

Mark wrote Corners. Then he stood and looked at the board with his arms folded, and nobody said anything for a while.

Lena broke the silence. She didn't sound unhappy. She sounded like a woman doing a sum she had already done.

"A hundred and ten liters. I'm sending it to the kitchens. They've got pots big enough to boil it, and every liter they cook with today is a liter they don't take from the tanks."

She looked at the gauge on the wall, and tapped the glass. "Which brings me to the tanks. I read them at six. Twenty-five thousand. If we'd used thirteen-fifty a day since the sixth, it would say twenty-five six."

Jack did the math before he wanted to. "So we're using more."

"Fifteen hundred, give or take. People are being careful, and it still isn't thirteen-fifty. Somebody's mother needs a bath. Somebody's tomatoes are dying. At fifteen hundred, we have about sixteen days."

Sixteen days. Jack looked at the four words in the corner of the board, and then at the hundred and ten liters on Lena's clipboard. It was the best morning they'd had since the town shrank. It would keep the whole town in water for not quite two hours.

Mark had seen him do the math. "Say it."

"One shake is a hundred and ten liters. On a clear, cold morning. And there aren't clear, cold mornings every day."

Aiden turned the jar slowly in his hands. "And every shake brings one of these down. Kicking. Within sight of its front door."

Mark wrote that at the bottom of the board, and underlined it.

Then he turned around.

"Here's what I'll agree to. Not again today. One leaf near that retreat on any morning. Never the one under it. If you want more water than that, find me somewhere else to shake."

Lena's console chimed, and Sarah's voice came out of the speaker. She sounded wide awake.

"There's somewhere else. There's the end of every road."

Mark frowned at the speaker. "Nobody's going to the end of every road."

Sarah's voice sharpened a little, the way it did when she was right and knew it. "Nobody has to. There are cameras on all of them. Lena, when did anybody last look at the other feeds?"

Lena was already scrolling. She stopped, and scrolled back, and her face changed. "The sixth. Most of them. We put everyone on the western road after the spider."

"Then I'll look. From the couch. I'll find every leaf tip that hangs over asphalt, and every drop on it, and every bag of silk above it. Mark gets a list. He likes lists."

Mark looked at the speaker for a long moment. "Doctor Mercer's going to say that's work."

"Doctor Mercer said watching is fine. This is watching. It's just more of it."

Jack heard Doctor Mercer's own words in that, exactly. So did Mark. He uncapped the marker again and wrote, at the top of a fresh column: Other roads. Sarah.

Doctor Mercer came to the house at eight. She took Sarah's pressure, and then took it again, and wrote it down without a word. Then she looked at the notebook on Sarah's knee, which now had a map drawn in the back of it, every road in town with a small circle where it ended.

"What's that?"

"Watching."

"It looks like work."

"It's watching with a pen."

Doctor Mercer looked at her for a long moment, then at the number. "Same as last night. The little better is holding. I'll take that. Keep holding it."

She turned to Jack, peeled back the corner of the dressing on his hand, and frowned. Then she peeled it back a little more.

"It's dry."

"Dry as a bone."

"Somebody finally kept a bag dry." She pressed the tape back down. "Pink. No worse. Whose idea was the collar?"

"Paul's."

"Tell Paul I said thank you. Don't tell him I said it twice."

On her way out, she stopped by the crib. It had both its rails on now. It stood straight.

She looked at it, and almost smiled. "Better."

Jack sat on the floor with the instructions for a while after she'd gone, trying to work out what step eleven wanted from him and failing. Sarah lay on the couch with the tablet. She had started with the western road and meant to work her way around the whole town, one feed at a time.

She went quiet. Not the quiet of someone reading. The other kind.

"Jack. Come and look at this."

He sat on the edge of the couch. She had the western road up. Not the live feed. The recording from that morning, a little after seven. The timestamp in the corner read seven oh four.

The sun was full on the road end. The trough was gone. The puddles from the shower lay on the asphalt in front of the place where it had stood, flat and shining. From them, a thin, wet line ran back across the road toward the cart. The line the corners had left.

The spider was on the asphalt.

It had come down the stem, and onto the soil, and onto the road, in the short, low bursts he knew now. It stood over the nearest puddle with its face lowered into it. It was drinking.

Then it lifted its face, and moved to the next one. And the next. And then it was on the wet line, following it across the asphalt, three steps, stop, three steps, stop, toward the cart.

About halfway, the line ended, where the sun had already dried the road. The spider stopped there. It stood on the last of the wet for a long time. Its front end turned slowly, left and right.

Then it turned around and went back.

Sarah's voice was very quiet. "Nobody saw this. Not live. I'd switched to the station camera to watch you unload. Aiden stopped when you closed the lid."

Jack didn't say anything. Neither did she.

On the screen, the spider climbed the stem with nothing in its jaws and went into the silk. The road end was empty again, wet and shining in the sun.
