# Chapter 24: First Gray

The alarm on Jack's wrist went at a quarter past four, and he was awake before the second buzz.

The other side of the bed was empty.

He found Sarah on the couch in the dark, wrapped in the blue blanket, with the tablet glowing on her knees. Four camera feeds filled the screen. Three were black. The fourth showed the greenhouse door, lit by a single work lamp, and the round white face of the old thermometer screwed to the frame.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

She didn't look up. "So is he. He's been kicking since three. I think he knows."

"He doesn't know anything. He's the size of a melon."

"A melon that kicks. Sit down. Give me your hand."

She had the surgical glove and the clear plastic bag ready on the arm of the couch, and a roll of tape. She worked the glove over his left hand, slowly, so it wouldn't pull at the stitches, then the bag over that, and taped it twice around his wrist. Then she pressed the tape down all the way around with her thumb, the way Doctor Mercer did.

"Doctor Mercer said take it off the second you're back in the truck."

"I know."

"I'm telling you anyway. You're going to be cold, and wet, and pleased with yourself, and you'll forget."

He kissed the top of her head. "Be boring."

"That's my line."

Mark was outside at twenty to five, with the motor running and the headlights off. Behind him, so close that its bumper nearly touched, sat Paul's flatbed. The trough was lashed down on the back of it under a gray tarp, with the long pole strapped along its side.

Paul was in the driver's seat.

Jack leaned in at Mark's window. "I thought his crew was bringing it."

Mark kept his eyes on the road. "So did I."

Paul's voice came from the flatbed's open window, low and gravelly. "I built it. I'm not watching it go off the back of a truck on a camera. I'll stop at the cart. I know the rule."

Mark looked at him in the mirror for a long moment.

"The cart. Not one step past it."

"Not one step."

They drove by the streetlights as far as they went, the flatbed following without lights, a dark shape in the mirror. At the last streetlight, Mark stopped long enough for Aiden to get out with his flashlight and his notebook. He walked off toward the long, dark roof of the greenhouse without looking back.

A minute later, his voice came over the channel, very quiet. "I'm at the door. Five degrees. And every star I've ever seen."

Lena answered from the station. "Station hears you. Five, and clear."

Sarah's voice came on after it, softer. "I'm on the couch. I have the feeds. Nobody needs to worry about me."

Mark didn't answer that. He switched off the dashboard lights, picked up the small red lamp, and drove the last stretch slowly, by memory, with the lamp cupped in his hand and pointed straight down.

The white cart came out of the dark, pale and square. Mark stopped twenty meters back and cut the motor. Behind them, the flatbed rolled to a stop and went quiet.

The sounds came back the way they had two mornings before. The slow dry clicking in the stems. The hum that came and went. Somewhere far out on the island, a long creak, like a door that nobody would ever close.

Unit Four got out first and went straight to the cart, with the rope over his shoulder. He tied it off at the tow hitch, leaned his whole weight into it, and then did it again. Nobody had told him to do it twice.

Paul and his two men slid a pair of planks off the back of the flatbed and set them against the bed. The trough came down them on its short steel feet, slowly, with Paul at the bottom calling every inch in a whisper.

"Easy. Easy. Your end's high. Easy."

It touched the asphalt with a soft metal scrape that sounded, in that silence, like a car crash.

Everyone stopped. Nobody breathed.

The clicking in the stems went on, slow and dry, exactly as before.

Mark let out his breath through his nose. "Lift it. Don't drag it. Nothing gets dragged."

Jack, Mark and Paul's two men took a corner each. Paul stayed at the cart beside Unit Four, his hands pushed deep in his pockets as though he had to keep them there by force. They carried the trough forward in the red glow, one short step at a time, toward the place where the asphalt ended.

The channel gave a tiny click. Sarah's voice came through, barely above a breath.

"There's a crack across the road. On yesterday's footage, the big drop hung right over it. A meter past the cart's line, and a little to the right. Look for the crack."

Mark swung the red lamp down, slowly, across the asphalt. A dark seam ran across the road in front of their boots, wide enough to catch a heel.

"Got it."

They set the trough down on the far side of the crack, with the end of the road right in front of it and the tall splash board on their own side. It stood there like a wall between them and the grass.

Mark kept his voice low. "Number three on my list. The biggest drops are under its front door. If that one is, when the light comes, we move. Two meters, either way."

Jack nodded in the dark. They had argued it at the station the night before. You couldn't point a light up into the stems to check. You could only wait for the gray, and look.

Jack clipped onto the rope at the front, behind the board, with Mark behind him. Paul's men walked back to the cart without a word. Then there was nothing to do but stand there with the cold coming up through his boots and wait.

The sky went from black to charcoal.

Lena's voice came in, right on time. "Two minutes. Everyone still there?"

Jack kept his voice to a whisper. "Still here. Still boring."

Charcoal to gray.

The wall of grass took shape in front of them, stem by stem. The blades came out of the dark, pale and bending. And along them, one after another, the drops came out too, small ones along every edge like strings of glass, bigger ones higher up.

Then Jack saw the big one. It hung at the tip of a long blade, three meters up, heavy and round and perfectly still, right above the trough. Paul had built the trough for exactly that drop.

He let his eyes follow the blade back, up and into the stems.

The silk retreat hung there. It hung from the same stem as Jack's blade, not a meter above it, pale and quiet.

Behind him, Mark had seen it too.

On the channel, Sarah's voice was so quiet it was hardly a voice at all. "It's under the front door. Mark, I see it on the feed. There's another one, two meters left. Smaller. Nothing above it."

Jack found it. A shorter blade, bending lower over the road, with a drop at its tip that held maybe half the water of the first. Above it there was nothing but stems and gray. No silk.

Mark's voice was barely a breath. "Half the water."

Jack kept his eyes on the smaller drop. "Half the water. Nowhere near its front door."

Mark was quiet for one second. Two.

"We move it. Two meters left. Lift it. Don't drag it."

They called the two men back from the cart by hand signal, and the four of them took the trough by its corners and lifted. It was heavier than it had been in the dark. Jack's bagged hand slid on the cold metal, and he changed his grip. They carried it left, one short step, then another, with the board between them and the grass and the gray growing all the time. Jack counted every step in his head. He couldn't have said afterward how many there were.

They set it down under the smaller drop. The two men walked back to the cart. Paul hadn't moved from the cart, and he didn't now.

Aiden's voice came over the channel. "Four degrees. Not a cloud."

Lena's came after it. "Six thirty-four."

Somewhere out on the island, the robin started again, bright and sweet and ordinary.

Mark leaned close behind Jack, so close that Jack could feel the words on the back of his neck. "One leaf. One lift. One drop. Then the lid, slow. Then we're gone."

"Understood."

"Go."

Jack raised the pole over the top of the splash board. The padded fork rose into the gray, past the edge of the board, past the strings of glass along the blade, until it was under the leaf, a hand's width from the place where the tip began to bend.

He let it touch.
