# Chapter 14: Who Was It

Jack could feel the microphone stand under his good hand, cold and slightly crooked again where he had gripped it.

He could lie. It would be easy. A fault, a corrupted command, a machine nobody fully understood doing something nobody expected. Half the room would believe it, because they wanted to.

And whoever had done this would hear him say it, and know exactly how little he had found.

Sarah's hand touched his back, once, and stayed there.

He leaned toward the microphone. "Somebody did."

The hall didn't make a sound.

"TOMBS didn't fail last night. Somebody activated it on purpose. They did it with my credentials, and they did it while locking me out of my own system. It wasn't me. I don't know who it was yet."

A chair scraped near the back. Someone said a word under their breath that a child in the second row repeated, delighted, until her mother covered her mouth.

Paul Harlan didn't move. "Your credentials."

"Yes."

"So it was your password that did this."

"It was my password that somebody else used. That's not the same thing, and I know exactly how that sounds."

Paul looked around the room as if he were collecting witnesses. "It sounds like the lab is telling us the lab didn't do it."

Sarah stepped up to the microphone before Jack could answer. "It sounds like that because it's true. I was with him. When it started, we were both in the laboratory trying to stop it. We pulled the shutdown lever. It kept running."

"With respect, ma'am, you're his wife."

"With respect, Mister Harlan, would I be standing up here next to him if I thought he'd done it?"

A few people near the aisle shifted, and one of them, a woman in a lab coat buttoned wrong over a nightgown, nodded hard enough that her glasses slipped. Paul noticed. He didn't look convinced, but he didn't push it either.

He turned back to Jack. "What else do you know?"

Jack had been waiting for that one too.

"More than I'm going to say on this microphone."

That got a reaction. Voices rose all over the hall, and somewhere outside, through the glass doors, a man on the lawn shouted something angry that didn't carry.

Jack raised his good hand and kept it up until the room settled. "Listen to me. Every speaker in this settlement is carrying my voice right now. That's what we wanted. One message, everyone hears the same thing. But it means that if the person who did this is in this town, they're hearing it too."

He didn't let himself look along the front rows. He wanted to.

The hall went quiet in a new way.

"I'm not going to tell them what we've found. I'm not going to tell them what we're looking for. When I know who it was, you'll know. All of you. That's a promise."

Paul studied him for a long moment. "And until then, the lab investigates the lab."

"No. Security investigates. Mark Jones and his officers. They report to the whole settlement, not to me."

Over by the doors, Mark straightened. "News to me. Fine."

Paul grunted. It wasn't agreement. It might have been the shape of one.

"I want to see what they find. Me, not a summary."

"You'll see everything that won't tip them off. Which is most of it."

Paul weighed that. "Most."

"Most."

Paul sat down.

Jack breathed out for what felt like the first time since he had climbed onto the stage. The microphone picked it up and carried it to every speaker in town, and somewhere in the third row a man laughed once, not unkindly.

The questions came faster after that, but smaller, the way a storm breaks up into rain.

A woman near the front stood up holding a toddler on her hip. "What about food? I've got a two-year-old who eats like a grown man."

"The kitchens and the stores are counting everything this morning. I don't have a number for you yet. When I do, it'll come over these speakers, the same as this."

"Is there enough?"

"Today, yes. Beyond that, I won't guess."

She nodded slowly and sat, the toddler grabbing for her collar.

A young man in the back didn't bother standing. He just called out, loud and flat. "I don't believe any of it."

Heads turned.

He was maybe twenty, in a soccer jersey and bare feet, arms crossed. "No offense, Doctor. A video of a spider. Grass out the window. I could make that video. I'll believe it when I walk to the end of the road and look."

Mark answered from the door before Jack could. "Nobody walks to the end of the road."

"Then how am I supposed to believe you?"

Jack looked at the screen above him, then lifted his wrist. "Lena, can you give him the western road? Live."

"Give me five seconds."

The greenhouse footage vanished. In its place, a camera mounted high on a utility pole, looking down the western road where it ran out of asphalt. A parked maintenance cart sat at the very end, ordinary and white and a little dusty. Beyond it, the road stopped, and the earth began, and a wall of green stems rose out of it, each one thicker than a man's waist, climbing past the top of the frame. Something small and dark crawled along one of the stems. Then it opened a pair of wings, and the whole room flinched, because it was the size of a dog.

The young man's arms came uncrossed.

"That's live?"

"That's live. That's the cart Facilities parks there every night. You've walked past it."

He had. Jack could see it in his face.

Nobody said anything for a while.

The woman whose daughter was in Boston was the next to stand, and Jack braced himself, but she didn't ask about her daughter. She asked something else.

"What do we do? Right now. Today. Not the rules. What do we actually do?"

Jack looked out at sixty faces and thought about the ones he couldn't see. He thought about how little he actually had to give them.

Then he gave them what he had. "Today, we count. Water, food, medicine, fuel, everything. We find out exactly what came with us and what didn't. If you have a skill, tell security, and they'll find you something to do with it. If you don't, stay home, check on your neighbors, and keep the lights off at the edge tonight. Nobody's going to fix this by lunchtime. But nobody's going to face it alone, either."

It wasn't a speech. It didn't sound like one. But a few people nodded, and the woman from Boston sat down with her shoulders a little straighter than before.

Doctor Mercer had been standing at the side of the stage for some time, arms folded. She caught Jack's eye and tapped her watch.

Jack leaned into the microphone one last time. "That's all I have. The utility station will post the water limits by noon. Lena, you can turn me off."

"Gladly."

The speakers clicked.

The hall erupted into a hundred private conversations at once. People surged toward the stage with questions they hadn't wanted to ask in front of everyone. Mark and two of his officers moved in front of it, polite and immovable. Aiden pressed himself flat against the wall, as far from the crowd as he could get.

Sarah let out a long breath beside Jack. "Well. Nobody threw a chair."

"The day's young."

"That was the right answer. About keeping things back."

"Paul didn't think so."

"Paul thinks it was the right answer too. He just doesn't like it." She glanced across the room, where Paul Harlan was already holding court in a ring of people from the machine shop. "I'd rather have him asking questions than not."

Jack's wrist buzzed. Not the public channel. Lena's private one.

He turned away from the crowd and lifted it. "If you're calling to review my performance, I'd rather wait."

"It was good. That's not why I'm calling."

Something in her voice made him straighten. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's broken. I want to be clear about that. I've just been watching the water system while you talked."

"And?"

"And the second you said the word restricted, the whole settlement turned on its faucets. Kitchens, showers, laundry, bathtubs. I've never seen a draw curve like it. We've used more water since then than we did in the entire night."

Jack closed his eyes. "People are filling their bathtubs."

"Every bathtub in town, from the look of it. And every pot, every bottle and every bucket they own."

Sarah had heard enough to understand. She put her hand over her mouth, and Jack honestly couldn't tell whether she was horrified or trying not to laugh.

"We told five hundred people the water was running out."

Lena sounded almost admiring. "And five hundred people did the most reasonable thing in the world."

Jack looked out at the crowded hall, at all the faces that had just been told the truth and believed it.

"How fast is it going down?"

"Fast enough that if I don't do something in the next ten minutes, the limits I post at noon are going to be a lot smaller than the ones I promised you."
