# Chapter 16: The Water Clock

Paul Harlan didn't wait for Jack to reach the door. "You shut off my shop."

"Lena shut off every nonessential industrial line last night. Yours was one of them."

"Nobody asked me."

"Nobody asked anybody. It was the middle of the night and the town had just shrunk."

The technician beside Paul took the opportunity to slip back inside the building. Jack didn't blame him.

Paul jabbed a thumb over his shoulder, roughly toward the workshops. "My lathes run on coolant. Coolant is water. Half my machines are sitting there with jobs clamped in them, and if they sit dry long enough, I'm going to lose parts I can't make again. Not with what's left in this town."

"How much water do you need?"

Paul opened his mouth, then closed it again. He clearly hadn't expected that question.

"Not much. Not every day."

"Then tell Lena. She'll put it on the list. But I'm going to be honest with you, it won't be at the top."

"Where will it be?"

Sarah answered from the vehicle, where Mark was helping her out. "Below drinking, below medical, below cooking. Above washing cars."

Paul looked at her, then at the careful way she was standing, then back at Jack. Some of the anger went out of his shoulders. Not all of it.

"I'm not trying to steal anybody's drinking water."

"I know."

"My night shift was on the floor when that machine went off. They didn't get home in time to fill any bathtubs. They're going to get the same slow faucet as everybody else, and some of their neighbors are going to be sitting on a bathtub full of water."

Jack hadn't thought about that. He looked at Sarah, and could see she hadn't either.

"Come inside. You should hear the numbers."

Paul hesitated, then followed.

The utility station was all concrete and pipe and humming machinery, warm in a way that the rest of the town hadn't been all morning. Lena was already there, standing in front of a wall of round brass gauges with a tablet in one hand, a pen in her teeth, and her hair tied back in a knot that had clearly been redone several times since eleven o'clock.

She took the pen out of her mouth. "Oh good, everybody. Sit down, Sarah."

"I've been told."

"Then do it."

Sarah sat in the nearest chair. Mark leaned in the doorway, as he did in every doorway, as though doorways were his natural habitat.

Lena tapped the largest gauge. "Two main tanks. Drinking water. When they're full, they hold about five hundred thousand liters together. Plus the fire reserve, which nobody touches unless something's burning."

Paul whistled under his breath. "That's a lot."

"It would be, if they were full. They weren't. They're never full at night."

She drew a curve on her tablet and held it up.

"We use water all day. The tanks drop. Then every night, between midnight and five, they refill from the reservoir."

Jack saw it before she said it. "And TOMBS cut the intake at eleven."

"At eleven. Right before the refill. The tanks were close to the lowest they ever get."

Sarah leaned forward in her chair. "How much is in them now?"

Lena checked the gauge, then her tablet, then the gauge again, as if the second look might change the answer. "About three hundred and fifty thousand liters. After this morning."

Mark frowned. "That still sounds like a lot."

"Normal use is about a hundred thousand a day. Showers, laundry, cooking, the greenhouse, the shops. Everything."

The room was quiet for a moment.

Paul did the math out loud, the way people do when they want someone to tell them they've done it wrong. "Three and a half days."

"At normal use. Yes."

Jack looked at the gauges. Half a dozen needles, steady, unremarkable, telling the whole town how long it had.

"So we don't use water normally."

Lena nodded. "Emergency guidance says a person can get by on about twenty liters a day. Drinking, cooking, a little washing. That's not comfortable. But it's enough."

Jack ran it in his head. "Five hundred people."

"Ten thousand liters a day for homes. Medical needs its own, about three thousand. The kitchens, another two. Call it fifteen thousand a day. Toilets get flushed with a bucket of used washing water, not from the faucet."

Jack frowned at the curve. "That leaves the greenhouse. Aiden irrigates every morning."

"From the drinking tanks. It's a big piece of normal use. Bigger than the kitchens."

Paul spoke up without being asked. "Then don't use drinking water for it."

Everybody looked at him.

"The wastewater plant treats everything that goes down the drains, doesn't it? Not to drinking quality. But clean enough for plants."

Lena nodded slowly. "It does. The treated water goes out to a holding tank and gets released."

"Then stop releasing it. Run a line from that tank to the greenhouse irrigation. I can make you the fittings. My night shift can lay the pipe."

Lena was already scribbling. "That would take the greenhouse off the drinking water entirely."

"Give me my night shift and a day."

Sarah did the last of it in her head faster than anyone. "Fifteen thousand a day. Twenty-three days."

"A little over three weeks. If nothing leaks. If nothing breaks. If nobody cheats."

Mark grunted from the doorway. "Somebody always cheats."

"Then a little less than three weeks."

Jack turned it over. Three weeks was not forever. But three weeks was not three days, either. It was time to think. Time to try things.

Jack looked at him for a long moment. It was the same man who, less than two hours ago, had stood up in front of the whole town and asked who had done this to them.

"You'll have them. And your coolant goes on the list."

Paul nodded once, and didn't say thank you, and didn't need to.

Sarah tapped the arm of her chair. "That leaves Paul's night shift. And everyone else who didn't fill a bathtub."

Mark sounded like he already knew the answer. "Can we take the water back?"

Sarah shook her head. "Knock on five hundred doors and tell people to drain their bathtubs into a truck? We'd lose more trust than water."

"Then what?"

Sarah thought about it, chin resting on her hand. "We don't take it. It's theirs. Whatever's already in a bathtub or a bucket is that household's own. But nobody gets more than twenty liters a day from the faucets, whether they filled a bathtub or not. And we put a drinking-water point at the community center, where anyone who runs short can fill a jug. No questions. Just their name in a book."

Paul considered that. "So the people who filled their tubs got ahead."

"For a few days. After that, everybody's the same."

"It's not fair."

"No. It's just less unfair than anything else we could do this morning."

He thought about it, then shrugged. "My guys can live with that."

Lena wrote it all down in a hand that got smaller and faster as she went. "Two supply windows a day. An hour in the morning, an hour in the evening. Full pressure during those windows, a trickle the rest of the time. Twenty liters a person. Medical and kitchens on their own allowance. Toilets flushed with washing water. Greenhouse on treated water once Paul's line is in. Drinking point at the community center."

It took the rest of the morning to turn all of it into rules. Lena called medical and the kitchens for their real numbers. Paul walked the greenhouse lines and came back with a list of parts. Sarah argued with the wording from her chair until it said exactly what it meant, and nothing it didn't.

Lena looked up from the final draft. "It's eleven fifty-two."

Jack nodded at her tablet. "Then you're on."

Lena walked to the console in the corner, the one that tied into every speaker in the settlement, and opened the channel. Her voice went out across the whole town, calm and plain and almost bored, the voice of someone reading a bus timetable.

She read the limits once, slowly. Then she read them again.

When she closed the channel, the station was quiet except for the hum of the pumps.

Sarah wasn't looking at the gauges anymore. She was looking past them, out the small, high window on the station's far wall, where a slice of the morning sky showed between two pipes. Above the rooftops beyond it, the tips of the grass moved in a breeze they couldn't feel through the concrete.

Jack knew that look.

"What?"

She didn't answer right away. "We're counting the tanks."

"That's what we're supposed to be counting."

"I know. But we're only counting the water inside."

Jack followed her eyes to the window, to the grass. Then he remembered the southern edge in the dark, and a bead of water clinging under a blade, rising nearly to his chest.

"Last night. At the edge. There was a drop of water out there as tall as I am to the ribs."

Sarah nodded slowly. "One drop. On one blade." She looked back at the window. "How many blades are out there, Jack?"
