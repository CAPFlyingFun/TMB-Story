# Chapter 15: Bathtubs

Jack pushed through the side door of the hall into a narrow corridor that smelled of floor polish and old coffee. The noise of the crowd dropped to a muffled roar behind him.

"Lena. What can you do in ten minutes?"

"Short of shutting the water off entirely? I can drop the pressure. Block by block. The faucets still run, they just run slowly. Very slowly."

"How slowly?"

"Filling a bathtub would take most of the morning. Filling a glass would take about as long as it does now if you're patient."

Sarah came through the door behind him, with Doctor Mercer right behind her like a shadow that carried a medical bag. "What happens to the people who didn't fill anything yet? The ones who were actually listening to the part about restrictions?"

Lena's voice was steady. "They get a slow faucet like everyone else. Nobody gets cut off. Nobody gets ahead any further than they already are."

Jack thought about it for the length of one breath. "Keep full pressure at medical."

"Already planned on it."

"The kitchens, too. And your station."

"Done. Housing blocks first, then the workshops. Mark's people are going to get calls."

"Lots of calls?"

"Every single person in town who was halfway through filling something."

Jack winced. "Do it."

Somewhere under the floor, faintly, a pipe knocked as the pressure changed.

Mark leaned into the corridor from the hall, his radio already chattering. "Somebody want to tell me why my officers are suddenly getting reports that the water's broken?"

"It's not broken. It's slow on purpose."

"Can I tell them that?"

"Tell them it's slow so there's enough for everybody. Tell them the limits come at noon."

Mark listened to his radio for a moment, then sighed. "Unit Four says a man in Block C is very upset about his bath."

Sarah's mouth twitched. "Tell Unit Four that a man in Block C is going to have the cleanest emergency in the settlement."

Mark turned away and keyed the radio, and Jack heard him say almost exactly that, only more politely.

Doctor Mercer had waited for all of this with remarkable patience. Now she stepped in front of Jack.

"Medical. Now."

"Lena needs me at the utility station."

"Lena has your wrist. The utility station has walls. Neither of them is going anywhere. Your hand is bleeding through a rag that has now been through a greenhouse roof and a town meeting."

Jack looked down. She wasn't wrong. A slow red stain had crept out from under the edge of the cloth.

"It's small."

"Then it will take very little time to fix."

The medical center was a five-minute walk. Doctor Mercer set the pace, and it was fast.

The waiting room was busier than Jack had ever seen it. A man with a sprained wrist. Two older women who had come in together because one of them had fainted on her porch and the other refused to let her come alone. A teenager with a nosebleed who kept apologizing. Every one of them looked up when Jack walked in, and every one of them recognized him.

Nobody said anything. That was almost worse than the hall.

Doctor Mercer steered him into a treatment room and sat him on the edge of a bed. She unwound the rag without ceremony.

The cut ran diagonally across the back of his left hand, shallow at one end and deeper than he would have liked at the other.

"Glass?"

"The greenhouse roof."

"Of course it was."

She cleaned it, and it stung enough that he stopped talking for a while.

"Four stitches. Keep it dry. Which, today, should be easy for everyone."

Sarah laughed from the doorway, then tried to turn it into a cough.

Doctor Mercer didn't look up. "You're next. Don't think I've forgotten."

"I would never."

The stitches took five minutes. Doctor Mercer worked fast and talked even faster, asking Jack how many hours he had slept, how much he had eaten, and when he had last had water, and making a small disapproving sound at each answer.

Halfway through the third stitch, Lena's voice came quietly from his wrist. "Draw's down to half what it was ten minutes ago. Complaints are up about four times. I'd call that a trade."

"Keep it there."

"Planning on it."

Then it was Sarah's turn.

The scanner hummed. A gray shape bloomed on the screen beside the bed, and then the sound came, quick and steady through the speaker, like someone running very lightly on a wooden floor.

Jack had heard it before. He had heard it a dozen times. It still went straight through him.

Sarah let out a breath she had clearly been holding since eleven o'clock the night before. "There you are."

On the screen, the gray shape shifted, and a small knot of shadow pushed outward.

Doctor Mercer raised an eyebrow. "He's annoyed with you."

"He's been annoyed with me all night. He kicked me during the activation."

"Good. A baby who's annoyed is a baby who's paying attention."

Doctor Mercer moved the scanner a little higher.

"Heartbeat's strong. Size is right where it should be. Movement's good."

Jack realized his good hand was gripping the edge of the bed. He let go.

Then Doctor Mercer wrapped a cuff around Sarah's arm and watched the numbers, and her expression didn't change, which was how Jack knew something had.

"What?"

"Your blood pressure's a little high."

Sarah sat up on her elbows. "How high?"

"High enough that I'd like to see it lower, and not so high that I'm going to panic about it. You've been on your feet for nine hours in a crisis. I'd be more worried if it were perfect."

"So it's fine."

"So it's a little high. I want you resting. I want you drinking your full share of water. And I want to check it again tonight."

"Resting where?"

"Somewhere with a chair."

Sarah looked at Jack. Jack, wisely, said nothing at all.

She turned back to Doctor Mercer with the expression she usually saved for conference panels. "The utility station has chairs."

"The utility station has a crisis."

"Every building in this town has a crisis. At least at the utility station, I can be useful while I sit in one of its chairs."

Doctor Mercer looked at her for a long, flat moment. Then she looked at Jack. "Is she always like this?"

"Since the day I met her."

"Fine. Mark drives you. You sit. You do not climb anything, carry anything, or argue with anyone for more than five minutes at a time. If you feel a headache, dizziness, anything strange with your vision, you call me. Not him. Me."

"Deal."

"It wasn't an offer."

Doctor Mercer pulled the cuff off Sarah's arm and shook her head, but Jack could have sworn the corner of her mouth moved.

Mark was waiting outside with the vehicle running. Jack helped Sarah into the back seat, and for once she let him.

Lena's voice came through his wrist as they pulled away. "Draw curve's flattening. You'll want to see this in person. I'm heading to the station now. The gauges there are older than the sensors, and I trust them more."

"We're on our way."

"Good. There's something else you should know before you get here."

"Go ahead."

"The machine shop's water was shut off last night with the rest of the industrial lines. Somebody just noticed."

Jack glanced at Sarah. "Let me guess."

They turned the last corner, and the utility station came into view, a squat concrete building with tall pipes running along one side. A small knot of people stood outside its door. In the middle of them, broad and gray-bearded and very much awake, Paul Harlan stood with his arms folded, arguing with a man in a technician's jacket who looked like he would have paid good money to be somewhere else.

Paul saw the vehicle, and stopped arguing, and waited.

Sarah sighed from the back seat. "Five minutes. I'm allowed to argue for five minutes."

"Then I'll go first."
