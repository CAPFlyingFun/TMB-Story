# Chapter 17: Dew

Lena didn't need to be asked twice. She pulled the western road camera onto the station's big monitor and dragged the timeline back to dawn.

"How far?"

Sarah leaned forward in her chair. "Half past six. When the light first hit the grass."

The image jumped. Gray light, the white maintenance cart at the end of the road, and beyond it the wall of stems. At first Jack didn't see anything different.

Then the sun came up behind the camera, and the grass caught fire.

Every blade was hung with water. Beads of it clung along the edges of the leaves and gathered at the tips, round and heavy and shining, some no bigger than a fist, some as tall as a man's chest. The low sun passed straight through them, and the whole wall of grass lit up like a chandelier.

Mark let out a long, low whistle.

Lena let it play forward. As the light rose, the beads shrank. They didn't fall. They simply grew smaller and smaller, like they were being breathed in, until by nine o'clock the grass was dry and green and ordinary again.

Sarah spoke quietly. "Every morning. That's there every morning."

Jack stared at the frozen frame Lena had stopped on, six-forty, the light right through the biggest drops. "Lena, can you get Aiden?"

"He's on his way over with Unit Four. Paul's crew is at the greenhouse measuring for the new line, and Aiden didn't trust them not to drown his seedlings."

Aiden arrived ten minutes later, out of breath and still slightly muddy. He looked at the screen, and his face changed completely. For the first time all day, he didn't look frightened. He looked interested.

"Oh. That's dew. Some of it. And guttation."

Mark raised an eyebrow. "Gutta-what?"

"Guttation. At night, plants push extra water up through their roots, and it comes out at the tips of the leaves. Those big ones at the ends, those are the plant's own water. The little ones along the edges are dew from the air."

Sarah leaned toward the screen. "Is it drinkable?"

"The dew's basically clean. The guttation drops have minerals and sugars in them. You'd want to test it. Filter it, at least."

He hesitated.

"And you'd want to be very careful getting it."

Jack turned. "Why?"

Aiden pointed at the biggest drop on the screen. "Because insects drown in it. All the time. In the greenhouse, I find gnats stuck in drops on the leaves every morning. The water grabs them, and they can't push their way back out. That one comes up past your chest. If you fell into it, I'm not sure you'd get out either."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Jack did the math in his head, then did it again on Lena's tablet because he didn't trust himself on no sleep. "Call that one most of a meter across. Even if I'm generous with the shape, it holds more than two hundred liters."

Lena looked up sharply. "One drop?"

"One drop. That's ten people's water for a day. And that screen was covered in them."

Sarah had her hand pressed flat against her stomach, the way she did when she was thinking hard. "So we have three weeks of water in the tanks, and more water than we could ever use sitting just past the end of the road every morning."

Mark pushed himself off the doorframe. "Sitting past the end of the road, inside a forest full of things the size of cars. Nobody walks past the end of the road. I said it this morning. So did you, on every speaker in town. Now you're going to stand there and tell me we're going out?"

"Not today."

"When?"

Jack looked at the frozen frame again. The light through the water. Two hundred liters in a single drop, and the jumping spider somewhere in that same grass, warming up, waiting for something fast enough to look like food.

"Not tomorrow, either. This morning we ran in because we didn't have a choice. This time we plan it."

"Plan what, exactly?"

"A small team. Roped together. On radio, every two minutes, the way you and I did last night. The very edge of the western road and no farther."

"And bring back what?"

"One jug. And a sample for Aiden to test. That's all. If it works once, we know how to do it again."

Mark folded his arms. "So when?"

"First light. When the water's there and the spider's still cold. Back before the sun's off the grass. Nine o'clock at the latest."

"And if the spider's not cold?"

"Then we stand very still and try not to look like flies."

"You said that this morning."

"It worked this morning."

Mark didn't laugh. But he didn't say no, either. "The day after tomorrow. I want a whole day to plan it. I want to know every single thing that can go wrong before I let anyone stand next to one of those."

"Agreed."

Aiden had gone very quiet. He was looking at the screen as though it had asked him a question. "I could tell you which plants those are. The ones with the biggest drops. If I could see them up close."

Jack heard what he wasn't saying. "You want to come."

Aiden went red. "I want to not be useless."

Sarah looked at him for a long moment, the way she looked at an experiment she hadn't decided about yet. "You weren't useless this morning. You knew what it was. Nobody else did."

Paul's voice crackled over Lena's radio before anybody could decide anything. "Measured the greenhouse end. I'll have treated water on those plants by tonight. Tell the kid his tomatoes will live."

Aiden let out a breath that was half a laugh.

Lena keyed the radio. "Thank you, Paul."

"Don't thank me. Put my coolant on the list."

"It's on the list."

"Higher."

The radio clicked off.

The afternoon went the way afternoons go when nobody has slept. Jack answered calls. Sarah, in her chair, answered more of them than he did, and twice Mark had to remind her that her five minutes of arguing were up. Lena posted the first supply window for the evening. Somewhere in the middle of it all, Jack fell asleep sitting upright against a warm pipe and woke an hour later with Sarah's jacket over him and no memory of either.

It was nearly dark when Mark finally drove them home.

The house was exactly as they had left it, the night before, when this had all been an ordinary late shift. Two coffee mugs in the sink. A baby blanket folded on the arm of the couch, still waiting for a crib that wasn't built yet. Jack stood in the doorway and looked at it all for a long moment, as though he were seeing somebody else's home.

Doctor Mercer came at eight, as promised, and wrapped the cuff around Sarah's arm in the kitchen. She watched the numbers settle.

"Better."

"Good."

"I said better, not good. Still a little high. I'll be back tomorrow night. Until then, rest. Real rest. Not chair rest."

"I rested all afternoon."

"You ran a water department from a chair."

Sarah didn't argue with that, which was how Jack knew how tired she was.

After the doctor had gone, they sat on the couch in the dark with the outward-facing lights switched off, the way everyone along the edge had been asked to. Through the window, past the rooftops, the grass stood black against a sky full of stars.

Sarah leaned against his shoulder. "You're going out there."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it like one. "To the end of the road. Not one step farther."

"You said that about the boundary last night."

"And I came back."

"With a fingernail story and a cut hand."

"Four stitches. Mercer was very proud of them."

She was quiet for a while. Then she took his good hand and pressed it flat against her stomach, and after a moment something on the other side pushed back, firm and annoyed and very much alive.

"He wants you to come back by nine."

"Tell him I promise."

"You tell him. He heard your voice on every speaker in town this morning. He'll recognize it."

Jack bent his head, close, and said it quietly enough that only the two of them would hear.

He thought about the hall. Sixty faces, and nearly five hundred more behind the speakers. One of them, maybe, had sat and listened to him promise to find them.

Far out past the edge of town, something heavy came down in the grass, once, and then the night was still again.

Neither of them moved. After a while, Sarah's breathing slowed, and her hand went loose over his, and Jack sat in the dark and listened to the island and didn't sleep at all.
