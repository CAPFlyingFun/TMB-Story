# Chapter 37: Line by Line

Doctor Mercer's list was two pages long, handwritten, and she read it out in the empty examination room the way Lena read gauges. Every line. No adjectives.

Sarah sat on the examination couch with her feet up. Jack stood by the window, because there was only one other chair, and Doctor Mercer was standing in front of it.

"I can deliver a baby. I've delivered a good many. I want you both to hear that first, because the rest of this is going to sound worse than it is."

She turned the page. "Here's what I haven't got. I haven't got a second pair of trained hands. A delivery should have two people who know what they're doing, and this town has one, and it's me."

Sarah didn't take her eyes off the page. "Go on."

"I have some of the drug we give after a birth to stop the bleeding. Not as much as I'd like, and some of it's close to its date. I have a handheld monitor for the baby's heart, the kind you hold against your belly for a minute. I don't have the kind that watches it all the way through labor."

She turned the page again, more slowly. "And if he comes out and doesn't breathe on his own straight away, I have a bag and a mask and my own two hands. That's all."

The room was very quiet.

Jack found that he was holding the windowsill.

Sarah was already reaching for her notebook. "Four things."

Doctor Mercer looked at her. "I'm not finished."

"Then five things. I'm writing them down."

"Most babies don't need any of it. Most of them come out loud and pink and furious and need nothing from anyone except a blanket."

Doctor Mercer put the list down. "I'm telling you because I'd rather have every one of them and not use it."

Sarah was writing. "A second pair of hands. Somebody in this town has done this before. Not as a doctor. A nurse. Somebody who's had five children. Somebody who was a paramedic before they came here. We find out who. The bleeding drug. You said some of it's close to its date. Does the pharmacy here have anything else that does the same job?"

"Some. Not much."

"Then we count it, and you tell us exactly how much you'd want."

Her pen moved. "The heart monitor. Jack builds things. Paul builds things. The handheld one works. Can it be made to keep working? Strapped on, wired to a screen?"

Doctor Mercer opened her mouth, and closed it again. "Possibly."

"And the breathing."

Sarah stopped writing. She looked at the last line for a long time. "That one I don't know yet."

"No. Neither do I."

"Then that's the one we start on first."

Doctor Mercer looked at her for a long moment, and then at Jack.

"You're doing it again."

Sarah didn't look up. "Somebody has to."

The next morning, the back room of the community center had the good chair in it before anyone arrived. Jack had carried it over himself. When the others came in, Sarah was already in it with the notebook open on her knee, and Mark looked at the chair, and at her, and sat down on the edge of the table without a word.

Sarah didn't wait for them to settle. "Eight o'clock. Every morning. Here. Twenty minutes. Everyone who runs something. I keep the list."

Lena put her clipboard down. "Water first?"

"Water first."

"Second test was clean. Third one's this afternoon. The line ran nine thousand eight hundred yesterday."

Lena looked at the ceiling while she worked it. "Drinking tanks are down to a hundred and eight thousand. If the third test is clean and Doctor Mercer says yes, the gallery water stops being washing water and starts being water. It doesn't change the sum. We still use more than we pump. But it means every liter of it can go anywhere it's needed."

Paul spoke from his crate. "And if I put a second pump in the same channel, and a second line beside the first one, they stop going down at all."

Mark frowned. "Another seventy hoses?"

"Another forty. I'll run it to the hydrant main on the southern road and let the pipes do the rest. Two weeks."

Sarah wrote it down. "One week."

Paul looked at her. "Ten days."

"Ten days."

"Food."

Sarah turned a page. "Six weeks at careful portions, from the seventh. That's the middle of April."

Aiden had been waiting with his hands clasped between his knees. "The greenhouse has been on treated water from the plant since the first week, rationed. It's been growing salad and keeping the tomatoes alive. Now there's gallery water too. I can water everything. And if somebody gives me the back hall of this building and a hundred grow lights, I can put beds in it. Beans. Potatoes. Things that fill you up."

Lena didn't even look up. "The power's there. The microgrid can take it if the streetlights at the edges stay off."

"They're staying off."

"Then you can have your hundred lights."

Sarah wrote Back hall. Aiden. "Houses."

Mark had his own list. "The last block on every road. Twenty-two families. They live where the night hunter walks and where the slugs come out. I want them farther in. There are empty houses. People who were off the island when it happened."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Those houses had been empty since the town shrank. Nobody had wanted to say why.

Sarah said it for them, quietly. "Their owners aren't coming back. Not soon. We ask. We don't tell. Anybody who wants to stay, stays, with the rules."

"Pell won't move."

"Then Pell stays. Unit Four can check on him."

By the end of the twenty minutes the list ran to three pages, and every line had a name next to it.

Aiden's third test came back clean at four. Doctor Mercer read the numbers standing in the station with her coat still on, and then signed the bottom of the sheet.

"Drinking water. Boiled, for the first week, until I'm sure. Then out of the tap."

Lena opened the valve that afternoon that sent the gallery line into the main tanks. Nobody cheered. It wasn't that kind of moment. Jack saw her stand with her hand on the wheel for a while after she had turned it, as though she wanted to feel it through the metal.

On the twenty-second, twenty families moved. Paul's flatbed made eleven trips. Unit Four and Mark carried furniture with people Jack had never spoken to, past houses whose blinds had been down since the town shrank. Mr. Pell did not move. He stood at his front window with the beans, and when Unit Four knocked to ask, he said no, politely, and offered him tea.

Unit Four drank the tea.

That night Sarah lay on the couch with the notebook on her stomach, three pages of names and jobs. The crib stood finished in the corner, straight and plain, with a folded blanket in it that nobody had used yet.

Jack sat down on the floor beside her. "The breathing line. You said start there."

"I did."

"Where do you start?"

She turned the notebook to the page and looked at the line, with nobody's name next to it yet.

"With people. Five hundred of them. Somebody in this town has kept a baby breathing who wasn't supposed to. A nurse who left nursing. A grandmother. A paramedic who came here to fix pumps."

She closed the notebook. "We don't know, because nobody's ever asked. So tomorrow Lena asks. Over every speaker in town."

"And if nobody comes?"

"Then we build it ourselves."

She put her hand on her stomach. "But somebody will come."
