# Chapter 39: Four Things

The three volunteers sat in a row in the medical center's waiting room like people called in for a job interview. Doctor Mercer looked at them over her list for a long time before she said anything.

"Thank you for coming. I'm going to ask you each one question, and I want the true answer, not the helpful one."

The former paramedic was a big quiet man who had spent nine years on ambulances and four at the wastewater plant. He said he had delivered three babies, all of them in the back of a vehicle, all of them in a hurry. The woman from the kitchens had four children and had been there for the births of two grandchildren. She said she knew what labor looked like from the bed and from the chair, and that she didn't faint.

The third was Nurse Hale, from the medical center's own night shift. She was small and gray haired and very still, and she had worked on a maternity ward for eleven years before she came to the island.

Doctor Mercer turned to her last. "Why didn't you ever tell me?"

Nurse Hale lifted one shoulder. "You never asked. And I came here to stop doing it."

"Will you do it again?"

"Once."

She looked across the room at Sarah, who was sitting by the window with her notebook. "For her. Yes."

Doctor Mercer wrote something on the list and drew a line through the first gap. Then she turned to the other two. "You'll be runners. When it starts, you fetch, you carry, you boil, you hold the light, and you do what she says before you do what I say. Can you do that?"

The paramedic nodded. The woman from the kitchens said she had been doing what other people said for forty years and could manage it for one more night.

The pharmacy was a narrow room with a locked door and a refrigerator that hummed. Doctor Mercer and Nurse Hale went through it shelf by shelf. Jack held the list, and Sarah sat on a stool in the doorway with her notebook, writing down every number they said.

"The injection."

Doctor Mercer held up a small box. "This is what I give after a birth, so the womb clamps down and stops bleeding. I've got this much. Two of them go out of date next month."

Nurse Hale was already reaching to a higher shelf. "And these."

She took down a brown bottle and turned it so the label faced Doctor Mercer. Doctor Mercer read it, and her face changed.

"I didn't know we had that many."

"Tablets. They do the same job, slower. They don't need a refrigerator, and they keep for years. On the wards where I trained, when the injections ran out, this is what we used."

Doctor Mercer counted them twice, the way she took Sarah's pressure. Then she wrote the number down. "With these, and the injection, I've got enough. Enough that I'm not afraid of it."

She took one injection out of the box and put it in a small envelope. She wrote on the envelope and put it at the back of the refrigerator on its own. "That one is Sarah's. Nobody touches it for anybody else unless it's a life."

Sarah wrote it down without being told. Then she looked up. "That's two."

The third gap took Jack and Paul a day and a half.

Doctor Mercer's heart monitor was a small gray handheld box with a probe on a cable. You held the probe against the belly with gel. The box played the baby's heartbeat out of a little speaker, fast and swishing, like a horse galloping a long way off. It was good for a minute. It was no good for twelve hours.

Paul turned it over in his big hands on the machine shop bench. "So it needs to stay where you put it."

"And it needs somebody to listen to it the whole time, or something to do the listening."

"Strap's easy."

Paul held up a length of soft webbing from a box of old harnesses. "Something to do the listening's your end."

Jack's end took most of the night. He opened the box, found where the sound went to the speaker, and ran a wire from there to an old tablet. Then he sat with the tablet and wrote a program that listened to the swishing and counted it. Every beat, a dot. Every minute, a number. The dots made a line across the screen, and the line moved.

On the afternoon of the thirty-first he took it home.

Sarah lay back on the couch and pulled her shirt up, and Jack smoothed the gel on, and Paul's soft belt went around her and buckled at the side. The probe sat against her skin, held there.

For a moment, there was only hiss.

Then the sound came out of the tablet, fast and steady, galloping. The dots began to march across the screen. The number in the corner flickered, settled, and held.

A hundred and forty.

Sarah put her hand over her mouth.

Jack didn't say anything. He watched the line. He watched it for a long time. Every dot was a beat of a heart he had never seen.

Sarah spoke through her fingers. "He's all right. Jack. Look at him. He's all right."

"He's all right."

That evening Lena called. Paul's second line had gone in at first light: forty more lengths of hose, and a second set of stages hung on the other side of the shaft. The two lines had run together for the first time, all day.

"Seventeen hundred and forty."

Her voice was very steady. "Pumped. Today. That's more than we use."

The next morning, the first of April, she read the drinking tanks at six and called again before Jack was out of bed.

"It went up."

"How much?"

"Not much. A hundred and eighty. But it went up."

She was quiet for a moment. "It's never gone up. Not once. Not since the fifth."

At the morning meeting, Sarah drew a line through the third gap on her list. Then she drew one through the word Water at the top of the page that had been there since the first meeting. Then she sat looking at the one line left.

Doctor Mercer saw where she was looking. "That one's the one I care about. The other three are about making sure nothing goes wrong. This one's about what to do if something does."

She folded her arms. "If he comes out and doesn't breathe well on his own, I have a bag and a mask. That's good for a few minutes. It's not good for an hour. And I have nothing that helps a baby breathe for himself, for longer."

Nurse Hale had come to the meeting, on Doctor Mercer's invitation, and had said nothing in it until then.

"There's a way."

Everyone looked at her.

"I saw it used where there were no machines. Not here. A long time ago, somewhere that couldn't afford the machines. You need oxygen, which you have, from the concentrator. You need tubing, and a bottle of water, and something to hold the tube under the water at the right depth. The water pushes back. It keeps the baby's lungs open, so he doesn't have to work so hard."

She looked at Doctor Mercer. "It's simple. It isn't a toy. But it works, if somebody builds it right."

Doctor Mercer was very still.

"Show me."

Paul uncrossed his arms. He looked at Jack, and Jack looked back at him.

Paul nodded slowly. "Tubing and a bottle. I can build that."
