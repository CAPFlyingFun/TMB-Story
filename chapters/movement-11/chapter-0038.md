# Chapter 38: Covered

At noon on the twenty-third, Lena's voice went out over every speaker in town, even and unhurried.

"This is the utility station. Doctor Mercer is asking for help. If you have ever delivered a baby, or helped deliver one, or cared for newborns, as a nurse, as a medic, as a parent, as anything, please come to the medical center today or tomorrow. That's all. Thank you."

Jack heard it on the couch, with Sarah's feet in his lap.

Sarah tipped her head back against the cushion. "As anything. That's good. I didn't tell her to say that."

"She's been listening to you."

By evening, three people had come. A man who had been a paramedic for nine years before he took the job at the wastewater plant. A woman from the kitchens who had four children, the last one born in the back of a car. And a nurse from the medical center's own night shift, who had worked on a maternity ward a long time ago and had never mentioned it because nobody had asked.

Doctor Mercer called at nine. She didn't say much. "Three. That's three more than yesterday."

On the morning of the twenty-fifth the sky over the grass was white and low and heavy. Aiden came into the back room at eight with his notebook already open.

"It's going to rain. Properly. Not like the drizzle on the eleventh."

He looked around the table. "I don't know what that looks like, here. Nobody does."

Mark didn't hesitate. "Everyone in by ten. Pumps off, outpost shut. Nobody past any road end. Nobody outside at all. A drop that size hits you, it doesn't make you wet. It knocks you down."

Paul was on his crate. "Mark bolted the bottom pump to the floor when they set it, and every platform's clamped to its rung. If the water comes down the shaft, it comes down. They won't go anywhere."

Sarah had the notebook open on her knee, and she was looking past all of them at the window.

"Lena. How much rain falls on a roof?"

Lena blinked at her. "Depends on the rain."

"How much falls on all of them?"

Jack saw Lena do the sum, and saw her face change halfway through it. "A lot."

"Then we don't let it run into the drains."

Sarah turned a page. "Every container in town with a lid. Bins, barrels, the big kitchen pots, the coolers. Under the downpipes. Filled from the gutters and closed the moment they're full. Lids on."

She looked at Aiden. "Covered."

Aiden almost smiled. "Covered. So nothing breeds in it and nothing comes to drink. A mosquito lays eggs in open water. Out here, the thing that hatches would be as long as I am."

Mark wrote it on the board. Downpipes. Lids. Then he looked at Sarah over the top of the marker. "Who tells five hundred people to go out and stand under their gutters?"

"Nobody goes out. The downpipes come down against the walls. People put the bins at the back doors, fill them through the door, drag them in, and shut the door."

She shrugged. "Lena tells them. She's very calm."

It started at eleven.

Jack heard the first drop before he understood what it was. A single heavy blow on the roof, like someone had dropped a full bucket from a height. Then another. Then a third, on the street outside the window. It burst on the asphalt in a flat explosion of water as wide as a car, and spray hit the front of the house.

Then they were coming down everywhere.

The noise was enormous. It wasn't rain on a roof. It was hammers. Every drop was a blow, and they came faster and faster until the blows ran together into one long roar, and the whole house shook with it. Through the window the street had vanished behind a gray wall of falling water. Drops hit the asphalt and burst and ran together, and in a minute the street itself was a river, running fast and brown and deep, and climbing the step of the house across the road.

Sarah had her hands over her ears. She was laughing, a little wildly.

"Jack. Jack, the downpipes."

At the back door, the gutter had given up. Water came off the edge of the roof in a solid, clear sheet, the whole width of the house, roaring. He got the first bin under the edge of it. The water hit the bottom with a boom and filled it so fast he barely had time to get the lid on. The second bin. The third. He dragged each one in over the step, and shut the door with his shoulder and stood with his back against it, soaked to the waist, listening to the roar.

Up and down the street, through the gray, he could hear other back doors banging shut.

Lena's voice came over his wrist, and for once she wasn't calm. "Station's taking water through the vents. Small. I've got it. Everybody stay in."

Then Unit Four, from home, because nobody was at the outpost. "Paul says the wire down the shaft is still live on his meter."

Then, a few minutes later, Paul himself, slow and gravelly. "There's a waterfall down my shaft, right onto my platforms. That's what the grate's for. That's what it's always been for. Don't anybody go and look."

It rained for an hour and twenty minutes. Then it stopped, as suddenly as it had started, and the silence afterward rang.

The town came out slowly. Everything shone. Water stood at the doors and ran knee-deep down the streets to drains that couldn't swallow it fast enough, and somewhere on the next block a cracked window was being taped. Out past the end of every road, the grass bowed under the weight of the drops, glittering, heavy, in the first thin sun.

By evening, Lena had the numbers.

"Rooftops. The bins, the barrels, everything people caught. About fourteen thousand liters. Every lid we had. The roofs threw away a hundred times that."

She tapped the clipboard. "It needs boiling. It's not tested. But it's fourteen thousand liters, in one afternoon, in buckets."

Aiden had been on the wire to the outpost the moment Mark let anyone near it. "The channel's the highest I've measured. It's running fast. It'll drop again, but the ground's full. It'll stay up for days."

Mark wrote both numbers on the board. Then, under them, he wrote a single word.

Covered.

He underlined it.

On the twenty-sixth, Lena read the drinking tanks at six in the morning, the way she had every morning since the town shrank. Then she called Jack.

"It's the twenty-sixth."

"I know."

"At seventeen thousand a day, today's the day it would have read zero."

She was quiet for a moment. "It says seventy-two thousand. Not zero. Seventy-two. And the rain bins will carry the kitchens for two days."

Jack didn't say anything for a while. Neither did she.

"Paul's second line is half laid. When it's in, I think the tanks go up. Not down. Up."

"Lena."

"I know. I'm not celebrating. I'm writing it down."

That evening, someone knocked at the door.

It was Mr. Pell. Jack had never met him. He was a small, very upright man in a cardigan, with a paper bag held in both hands. Unit Four was standing behind him at a respectful distance, as though he had walked him all the way across town.

"You're Doctor Bennett."

"Jack."

Mr. Pell held out the bag. "Jack. Your security man drinks my tea. He tells me things. He told me about the baby."

Sarah had got up from the couch. She came to the door with one hand under her stomach.

Mr. Pell looked at her, and his face went soft. "My wife made this. Years ago. For a grandchild we never got."

He took it out of the bag. A small blanket, white, knitted in a pattern of little raised squares, soft and slightly yellowed at the edges. "She'd want it used. Not kept. Used."

Sarah took it. She held it against her for a moment without saying anything at all.

"Thank you, Mr. Pell."

Mr. Pell nodded once, briskly, the way Doctor Mercer did. "Thirty-five weeks, your man says."

"Today."

"Good. That's good."

He turned to go, and stopped, and looked out at the shining street. "My beans liked the rain. Through the window. I've never seen them so pleased."

When he'd gone, Sarah stood by the crib for a long time. Then she shook out the blanket and laid it in. She smoothed it flat over the mattress with both hands, very carefully, the way you would set something down that you meant to stay.
