# Chapter 40: The Drill

The breathing device was the smallest thing Paul had built since the town shrank, and he built it more carefully than anything.

Nurse Hale stood at his elbow in the machine shop and described it again, slowly, while he drew it on the back of a supply sheet. Oxygen from the concentrator, mixed with ordinary air so the baby didn't get too much. Soft tubing to a mask the size of a walnut. And then the part that made Paul frown: a second tube coming back out, running down into a plastic bottle of water, held under the surface at a measured depth.

"The water pushes back."

She tapped the drawing. "The baby breathes out against it. It keeps the little air sacs in his lungs from collapsing between breaths, so he doesn't have to open them again every time."

Paul looked at the bottle. "And the deeper the tube, the harder it pushes."

"Yes. So the tube has to be held at exactly the right depth. Not about right. Exactly. Mark the bottle."

Paul marked the bottle. He marked it with a scribe and a ruler, and then he made a clamp out of a scrap of brass that held the tube at the mark and would not let it slip, and he tested the clamp by shaking the bottle until Nurse Hale told him to stop.

Then she brought out the doll.

It was a training doll from the medical center's cupboard, a soft pink thing the size of a newborn, with lungs that rose and fell when you squeezed a bulb. She fitted the tiny mask to its face. Paul opened the oxygen.

In the bottle, bubbles began to come up. Small, fast, steady.

Nurse Hale watched them for a long time without speaking. Then she nodded.

"That's right. That's what it looked like."

Jack spent the rest of the day on the warming box. A clear storage crate with the lid cut back, a heating pad from the medical center's supply room laid in the bottom under a folded towel, and a thermostat from the greenhouse that Aiden had pulled out of a seed tray. He set it to the number Doctor Mercer gave him and left it running, and checked it every hour with a thermometer until it held.

On the second of April, Doctor Mercer did the thirty-six-week check. She did it in the examination room with the belt on, so the heartbeat galloped out of the tablet the whole time, and with her hands, slowly, pressing in at the top of Sarah's belly, then the bottom, then the sides.

"Head down."

She took her hands away. "Where he should be. A good size. Not too big."

She glanced at the tablet. "Hundred and forty. Same as Jack's line said."

Sarah let out a long breath. "So he's doing everything right."

"He's doing everything right. You're doing everything right."

Doctor Mercer looked at the crate on the side table, and the bottle with its brass clamp. "And for once, so is the town."

The drill was on the night of the third.

Doctor Mercer had written it like a play. Every person, every step, every minute. She handed out copies at the morning meeting and told everyone to read them twice and then not look at them again.

At ten that night, Sarah sat on the couch at home, the way she would. Jack called the medical center from his wrist.

"It's started. Five minutes apart."

"Copy."

Doctor Mercer's voice, crisp. "Bring her."

Mark was outside before Jack had finished the sentence. He had parked two doors down, with the engine running. Jack helped Sarah out and into the back seat, and Mark drove the route they had chosen: down the inner streets under the streetlights, nowhere near the edge. Jack watched the clock on his wrist.

"Four minutes."

Mark didn't take his eyes off the road. "Four minutes if nobody's in the street. Three and a half if I don't stop for anybody."

At the medical center, the doors were already open. The paramedic was there with a wheelchair. The woman from the kitchens was at the stove in the staff room, and the big pots were already on. Nurse Hale had the delivery room lit, and the warming box on, and the bottle filled to its mark.

Doctor Mercer stood in the middle of it with her arms folded and watched them all, and said nothing, and took notes.

Then, at ten twenty, without warning anyone, she lifted her wrist.

"Lena. Now."

Every light in the medical center went out.

Somebody gasped. In the dark, Jack heard the concentrator wind down, and the hum of the warming box stop. He heard his own heart. He heard Sarah's hand find his arm.

One second. Five. Eight.

Then, somewhere below them, a deep rough engine coughed and caught. Eleven seconds after the lights went out, they came back on. The concentrator wound back up. The warming box clicked and began to hum. Sarah had the belt on for the drill. On the tablet, running on its own battery, his heartbeat went on galloping as if nothing had happened.

Doctor Mercer looked at her watch. "Eleven seconds."

She wrote it down. "Lena. Thank you. Put it back."

Lena's voice came over her wrist, faintly appalled. "You could have told me you were going to do that to your own building."

"Then it wouldn't have been a test."

They ran it again at midnight, from the couch to the delivery room. This time nobody asked anybody what to do. The runners ran. The water boiled. Nurse Hale had the bottle at its mark before Doctor Mercer asked. When the lights went out again, nobody gasped.

Afterward, Doctor Mercer sat on the edge of the delivery bed with her copy of the play in her lap, and for the first time since Jack had known her, she looked tired in a way that wasn't worried.

Doctor Mercer spoke to nobody in particular. "I was afraid of that night. Not the baby. The night. The dark and the town and nothing to fall back on."

She folded the paper in half. "I'm not afraid of the night anymore. I'm only afraid of the things nobody can plan for. Which is what every doctor is afraid of, everywhere. That's not a reason to stop. That's just the job."

Nurse Hale put a hand on her shoulder, briefly, and took it away again.

On the morning of the fifth, Sarah sat at the kitchen table with the notebook open and nothing left on the list. She turned the pages back and forth. Water, crossed out. The four gaps, crossed out. Food, a date in the middle of April with a question mark and Aiden's name. Jack watched her read it all twice, and then close it, and then open it again.

"I don't know what to do with my hands."

"You could rest."

She gave him a look.

Jack tried again, more doubtfully. "You could rest."

She picked up her wrist instead.

"Aiden. It's Sarah. That gnat in your jar. You said you wanted to know what it was eating."

She pulled the notebook toward her and turned to a clean page. "Did you find out?"
