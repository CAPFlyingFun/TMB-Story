# Chapter 34: What's in It

Aiden had a jar on a pole. It was one of Paul's, a clamp at the end of an aluminum rod with a screw-top jar held in it, and he lay flat on his stomach at the edge of the channel and lowered it over the side, slowly, the way Jack had once lifted a leaf.

"Nobody stands near the edge. If I slip, you pull my boots. You don't come in after me."

Mark knelt and took hold of his ankles without being asked.

The jar touched the water and tipped and filled. Aiden brought it up, screwed the lid down, and wiped the outside with a cloth he then put in a bag of its own. He did it three times. Three jars, three bags, three labels.

Sarah's voice came over the wire. "While he's doing that. Jack, turn around. Slowly. All the way."

He turned. His helmet light slid across the tiles, and the seams, and into the dark.

"Aiden's scanner is drawing the gallery. It goes on. Both ways, past where the drawing stops caring. And there's something on the far wall I want you to see."

He found it after a moment, far across the gallery, where his light was nearly too thin to show anything. The wall rose pale and smooth, and low down in it, there was a dark shape. An opening. Square cornered. Black.

"That's not on the drawing. The drawing has that wall as solid all the way along. I've marked it."

Her voice was very even. "Nobody goes near it."

Mark didn't look away from Aiden's boots. "Nobody was going to."

Jack looked at the black square for a long moment. Then he turned his light away from it.

Aiden was screwing the lid on the third jar when Sarah's voice changed.

"Mark. Don't move. On your camera. The wall behind the cage. Something's on it."

Mark didn't move. Jack did. He turned his head, slowly, the way he had learned on the road.

Something pale was coming down the wall of the gallery, beside the shaft. Long and flat and a pale yellow-gray, with legs. So many long legs that it seemed to ripple rather than walk. Its front end lifted from the concrete and swayed, two long feelers searching the air, and then it came down a little farther, toward the noise of them.

It was longer than the truck.

Aiden's voice was barely there. "Centipede. They hunt in the dark. They're fast."

Mark let go of his ankles. "Into the cage. Walk. Don't run."

They walked. Jack's whole body wanted to run, and he didn't let it. Aiden came behind him with the jars clutched to his chest, and Mark came last, facing the wall. Halfway there, Aiden's boot caught a tile seam. He stumbled, and one jar slipped out of his arms, hit the tiles, and burst.

The sound went up the gallery like a gunshot.

The centipede moved. Not a body length. All of it, at once, rippling down the wall toward them faster than anything that size should move.

The centipede had stopped. Its feelers swung toward them, and away, and toward them again.

They were in. Jack slammed the gate, and the latch didn't catch. He slammed it again. It caught.

Mark had the wire. "Four. Up. Now."

The cable went tight. For a terrible second nothing happened, as Unit Four took up the slack at the top by hand, a turn at a time. Then the cage lifted off the floor and swung, and Jack grabbed the rail. Below them, the floor dropped away. The pale shape on the wall came down another body length onto the tiles, right where the cage had been sitting, and its feelers touched the place, and touched it again.

Then the shaft walls closed around them, and he couldn't see it anymore.

They were back on the asphalt a little before five. Paul was standing at the end of the road with the lifeline coiled over his shoulder. He counted them as they came off the soil. One. Two. Three. Four. Then he counted them again.

"Two jars?"

Aiden held them up. "Two jars. The third one's down there. It can stay there."

The next afternoon, Aiden came to the station with three clean jars and a notebook full of numbers, and stood in front of Mark's board like a student in front of an exam.

"It's hard water. Full of minerals, from running over all that concrete. That's not a problem. It tastes of chalk, that's all."

He turned a page. "It has bacteria in it. Not a lot. Ordinary ones. What you'd find in any water that's run over the ground and into a drain. Nothing my kits can find that a filter and chlorine and boiling won't deal with."

Doctor Mercer had come from the medical center to hear it. She stood by the window with her arms folded.

"Your kits."

"My kits. They're not a laboratory."

"No, they aren't."

She looked at the numbers in his notebook for a long time. "So here's what it is. It's not drinking water. Not yet. First it washes. It flushes toilets. It waters the greenhouse. Every liter of that is a liter that doesn't come out of the tanks. Then it gets treated, and you test it after it's treated, three times, three days in a row, clean every time. Then I'll talk about drinking."

Lena was already writing. "The station has chlorine. For cleaning the tanks. And there's the old irrigation tank behind the building. Nobody's used it since they put the new line in. It's separate from the drinking tanks. Nothing mixes."

Sarah's voice came from Lena's console. She had been listening the whole time. "Aiden. When it doesn't rain. Does the channel stop?"

Aiden looked at his notebook as though the answer might be in it. "It hasn't rained since the eleventh. It was running full on the fifteenth. Drains like that pick up water out of the ground, not just off the top. It'll drop in a dry spell. I don't think it stops."

"Then we measure it. Every day. Before anyone counts on it."

Mark wrote it on the board without being asked. Measure the channel. Daily.

Then he turned to Paul. "And how does it get from down there to up here?"

Paul had been waiting all day to be asked. He stood up off his crate.

"No single pump in this town will lift water up that shaft. Sarah's scanner says it's over six hundred meters. A good pump does a hundred. So we do it like a mine. A pump at the bottom pushes up to a drum on a platform. The next pump takes from the drum and pushes up to the next. Electric, every one, because an engine down there fills that tunnel with exhaust and then Aiden's meter says no. A generator in a shed at the grate. Seven pumps, if I can find seven."

He spread his hands. "Then the hoses. Every hose in town. Fire station, greenhouse, my shop. Up the shaft, along Four's stakes to the road end, down the southern road to the station. It's a long way. But we've got a lot of hose."

"How much water?"

"Three liters a minute. Maybe. If the hose holds."

Lena's pen moved. "Three a minute. Only while people are out there with it. They walk the line first, they're at the grate by nine, they have to start back by half past two to be off the soil by five. Call it nine hundred liters on a good day."

Jack heard the sum before she said the rest.

"It's not fifteen hundred. It doesn't replace the tanks."

Lena looked up at the board. "But nine hundred a day we don't take out of them is the difference between running out at the end of this month and running out weeks later. Weeks we don't have now."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Mark uncapped the marker, and under Drawings, he wrote a new line.

Pump. Hose. Nine hundred. Daylight.
