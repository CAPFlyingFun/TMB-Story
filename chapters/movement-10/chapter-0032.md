# Chapter 32: Past the Asphalt

The end of the southern road looked like every other road end. Asphalt, then a crack, then the soil, then the wall of stems. Jack had stood at one like it twice in the last week.

He had never stepped off one.

Paul's sled sat on the last meter of asphalt, long and low on two wide runners. The frame, the winch and the cage were lashed down on it under a tarp, with the relay radio strapped on top in its orange case. Four harnesses ran from its front bar. Jack had one on. So did Mark, and Aiden, and Unit Four.

Paul walked around it once more, pulling on every strap. "Pull together. If one of you stops, all of you stop. If it tips, let it tip. I can build another sled. I can't build another you."

Mark tightened his helmet strap. "Sarah."

Her voice came into Jack's ear, close and clear, as though she were standing beside him. "I've got all four of you. I've got the road camera. Aiden, your scanner's drawing. I can see the grate, and I can see everything between you and it, as far as the camera reaches. Nothing's moving."

"Lena?"

"Relay's on. Your lifeline's tied to the light pole. Paul's crew has the other end."

Mark looked at the soil, and then at the sky. It was clear and getting warmer. The wolf spider had been down its hole since first light, across town on the western road. The jumping spider had its silk on the western road too. On the southern road, in six mornings of Sarah's footage, nobody had found any silk at all.

Jack knew that didn't mean there wasn't any.

Mark said it quietly, to no one in particular. "Nobody past the end of the road."

Then he stepped off the asphalt.

Jack stepped off beside him.

The ground gave under his boot. Not much. Enough. It was dark and crumbly and soft on top, and harder underneath, and it wasn't flat anywhere. Clods of dirt as big as cars sat in it at every angle, and between them ran little valleys, and in the valleys the soil was damp and dark.

Behind them, Paul's crew leaned on the back of the sled and pushed. It slid off the asphalt with a long scrape and settled into the soil on its runners.

Mark's voice was low. "Pull."

They pulled.

It was harder than the drills. It was harder than anything. The sled came forward a step and stopped, then came forward again. Jack felt it in his shoulders and his legs, and in his left hand, where the new pink skin pulled tight every time he gripped the harness strap. Beside him, Aiden was already breathing hard.

Sarah's voice came in. "Bear left. There's a valley between two clods, and it's flat, and it goes most of the way."

They bore left. The valley was flat. It was also wet. Ten steps in, the runners sank into the dark soil to their tops, and the sled stopped as if someone had nailed it down. They pulled. It didn't move. They pulled again, and Aiden's boots slid out from under him.

Mark looked at the sled, and then at the tarp lashed over it.

"Tarp off. Under the front."

They dug the soil away from the front of the runners with their hands, and pulled the tarp out flat in front of them, and hauled the sled up onto it. It came. On the tarp it slid. They dragged the tarp forward under it, a body length at a time, until the valley floor was dry again.

Aiden sat in the soil, breathing hard. "That was ten minutes."

"That was ten meters."

Mark was already folding the tarp. "We'll do it again on the way back."

A little farther on, the lifeline went tight behind them. Unit Four looked back along it. "That's the end. Paul's crew's at the end of it."

Mark looked at the grate, still a long way ahead between the stems, and at the rope. "Tie it off to a stem. It shows the way back. Past here, we've got the stakes."

The first stem came up beside them a few minutes later, and Jack stopped looking at the ground for a moment and looked up at it. From the road, the grass had always been a wall. In here, it wasn't a wall. It was columns. Green and pale and smooth, rising straight out of the soil, so tall he had to tip his head all the way back to see where they bent. The light came down between them in long slanting bars.

Aiden had stopped pulling to look up. "It's like a cathedral."

Mark didn't look up. "Cathedrals don't eat you. Pull."

A dead leaf lay across the valley ahead, brown and curled, like the roof of a house that had fallen in. They couldn't go around it. They went over it slowly. The sled scraped up one side, tipped on the curl, and came down the other with a hollow crunch that made all four of them stop.

Something moved under the leaf.

Dozens of things. Small and pale and quick, as big as rabbits. They came out from under the brown edge all at once and sprang, every one of them, high into the air. They landed and sprang again, away in every direction, until they were gone among the stems.

Jack's heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth. "What were those?"

Aiden was laughing, a little helplessly. "Springtails. They live in the leaf litter. They eat mold. They're harmless. They're the most harmless thing on the island."

"They jumped."

"That's why they're called springtails."

Unit Four bent down and planted a short stake in the soil beside the leaf, with a strip of orange cloth tied to the top. He had a bundle of them on his back. He had planted one every twenty steps since the asphalt.

Mark watched him do it. "Good."

They went on. The light moved. The sled scraped and stopped and scraped. Jack stopped counting the clods.

Then Sarah's voice came in, much quieter than before.

"Everybody stop."

They stopped.

"Don't look around yet. On the road camera, there's something crossing in front of you. Right to left. About as far ahead as the leaf was behind you. It's big. It's black. It's walking."

Jack didn't look around. He looked straight ahead between two stems, and after a moment, he saw it. It came out of the shadow of a clod and into a bar of light, and it shone. A long, hard, black body, glossy as a car's paint, on six jointed legs, walking steadily across the valley in front of them with its feelers sweeping the soil. It was as long as a car. Longer.

Aiden barely breathed it. "Ground beetle. They hunt on the ground. Things smaller than them."

Mark didn't move his lips. "Are we smaller than it?"

"Yes."

Nobody said anything else.

The beetle kept walking. It didn't turn. Its feelers swept the soil in front of it, left, right. Once they touched the far side of the leaf and stopped, and Jack thought his heart would stop with them. Then the feelers moved on. The beetle walked on. It went between two stems on the left, and its black shine went dark in the shadow, and then it was gone.

Sarah's voice came back after a long time. "It's off the camera. I can't see it anymore. Go when you're ready. Go slowly."

They went slowly.

It was a little after noon when the stems opened out, and Jack saw the grate.

It lay in the soil between two clumps of grass, exactly where the drawing had put it. Up close it wasn't a square in the dirt. It was a gate laid flat. Its bars were as thick as tree trunks, dark and pitted under a skin of old dirt, and between them the gaps were wide enough to walk through. And below the gaps, there was nothing. A straight square shaft, going down into the dark, farther than his helmet light could reach.

The air over it was cool and damp, and smelled of stone and water.

Jack stood at the edge of the first bar and looked down.

Behind him, Unit Four planted the last stake. Mark unbuckled his harness and set the relay radio on the bar beside the grate, and switched it on.

Lena's voice came in, crackling, then clear. "Relay's up. Station hears you."

Then Sarah's, from somewhere very far away, and right in his ear.

"I see it, Jack. I see what you see."

She was quiet for a moment. "Now don't fall in."
