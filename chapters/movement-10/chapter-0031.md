# Chapter 31: The Grate Breathes

Sarah had the southern road on the tablet before Jack was awake. When he came out, she didn't say good morning. She turned the screen toward him.

"Watch the bars."

Aiden's voice came quietly from the speaker. "Four degrees. Clear. Every star."

The road end was coming out of the dark the way it always did now, the stems first, then the drops along them. Down in the soil, the square of the grate lay black between its two clumps of grass.

And between its bars, something was moving. Not an animal. Something pale and thin, rising slowly out of the dark between the bars, curling, drifting sideways into the stems, and fading.

Jack sat down on the edge of the couch. "Is that smoke?"

"It's breath." Sarah didn't look up. "Aiden, tell him."

"It's air coming up from underground. Down there, it's warmer than up here this morning, and it's wetter. When it meets the cold, it fogs. Like your breath on a winter morning." Aiden paused. "Something down there is wet, Doctor Bennett. Wet enough to breathe like that."

They watched it until the sun came. The mist thinned, and thinned, and was gone.

At ten, they met in the back room of the community center, because it had a table big enough and a door that shut. Doctor Mercer came straight from the medical center and sat by the window. Paul sat on the edge of a desk with his arms folded. Aiden had a notebook on his knee with the gnat jar on top of it like a paperweight. Lena had brought the morning reading.

"Thirteen days."

Nobody needed it said twice.

Sarah had the drawing up on the wall screen beside the camera frame. The seam, the gallery, the sump drawn full of dark blue. And beside them, the bars in the soil, with the last of the morning's mist caught between them in the still she had saved.

Mark stood at the end of the table. "Here's the problem. The grate is past the end of the road. Everybody in this town has been told nobody goes past the end of the road. We said it. It was the right thing to say."

Paul didn't unfold his arms. "Then it was the right thing to say until it wasn't."

"Maybe." Mark looked at Sarah. "How far?"

Sarah had worked it out from the camera. "By the camera, from the road end to the grate is about as far as from our house to the station. Through grass, and soil, and whatever's lying on the soil. On flat asphalt that's a ten-minute walk. Out there, I don't know."

Doctor Mercer spoke for the first time. "And then down."

"And then down. The shaft under the grate is drawn straight. Deep, for us. Paul?"

Paul unfolded himself off the desk. "I've seen the drawing. I've seen the bars. You can walk between them, they're that far apart. Two of them, I'll sit a frame across. A winch on the frame, a cable off the winch, and a cage on the cable, so nobody's hanging on a rope over a hole. Three in the cage, and one on top running the winch. I'll have it built tomorrow."

Mark held up a hand before Paul could go on. "Then we go the morning after. And your crew stays at the road end. Not past it."

Paul looked at him for a long moment. "At the road end."

Lena had her own list. "Radios won't go through that much concrete. I'll put one relay at the road end, and you carry one to the grate. Past the bottom of the shaft, you might lose us. You might not. Nobody's ever tried."

Aiden held up his notebook. "And I want to test the air before anybody breathes it. The laboratory has a gas meter. And I've got water kits at the greenhouse. Bacteria, salts, anything strange. If there's water, I want to know what's in it before it goes near a cup."

Doctor Mercer turned from the window. "Who's going?"

The room went quiet.

Jack had known the answer since the bars came up on the screen. He said it anyway. "Me. Mark. Aiden, because he knows what he's looking at. And Four on the winch."

Doctor Mercer looked at his left hand. "The stitches come out today. Not tomorrow. Today, in my office, so I can see how it holds. And nobody goes down that shaft until the gas meter says so. Not one step."

"Agreed."

She turned to Sarah. "And you. You're not going."

Sarah's voice didn't change at all. "I know I'm not going. I'm running it."

Mark frowned. "Running it."

"From the station. With Lena. Every one of you wears a helmet camera, and I watch all four. I've got the drawings. I've got the road-end feed. I'll know where you are, and what's around you, before you do." She looked at Mark. "You like lists. I'll be the list."

Mark looked at her, and then at Jack, and then back at her. Something in his face eased.

"You'll be the list."

Doctor Mercer stood and picked up her bag. "In a chair."

"In a chair."

Paul built the cage the next day. Jack went to see it in the afternoon. His left hand had been bare since the day before, still pink and stiff and strange in the cold air of the machine shop. The cage was a square of welded pipe, waist high on three sides and open on the fourth, with a gate that latched. Above it, a short steel frame stood waiting on the floor, with the winch bolted to the top and a drum of cable beside it.

Paul slapped the frame. "It sits across two bars. Doesn't care how wide they are, I made it adjustable. Cable's rated for ten of you. I only want three."

Unit Four was walking slowly around the cage, looking at every weld. He stopped at the gate and lifted the latch, and let it drop, and lifted it again.

"It's good."

Paul glanced at him. "I know it's good."

"I'm just checking."

"Check twice. I would."

That night Jack sat on the floor by the crib with the last page of the instructions. Step eleven. He finally understood it. The mattress board went in last, not first, and it slid in from the side.

He slid it in. It went.

Sarah watched him from the couch. "You did it."

"Five days."

"Six. But who's counting."

He sat back against the couch beside her legs. For a while neither of them said anything. Outside, the street was dark. The last streetlight at the end of every road was off now. The town was quieter at night than it had ever been.

"Jack."

"Mm."

"You're going past the end of the road."

"I know."

"I'll be watching every step. Every single one." Her hand found the back of his neck and stayed there. "So you don't get to be brave. You get to be boring. You stop when Mark says stop. You don't go down until the meter says. And when you get there, you look, and you come back."

"Look, and come back."

"Say it."

"I'll look. And I'll come back."

She left her hand where it was. Under it, he could feel his own pulse, and after a while, against his shoulder through the blanket, something else. A small, determined push.

"He agrees with me."

"He always agrees with you."

"He has good judgment."
