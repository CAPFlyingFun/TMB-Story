# Chapter 33: Down

Paul's frame went across the grate in four pieces. Jack and Mark bolted the legs to two of the bars, one on each side of the gap, and Unit Four slid the crossbeam between them and cranked it out until it sat tight. Then the winch went on top, and the cable went over the wheel, and the cage hung beneath it over the dark.

Unit Four put both hands on the winch handle and turned it a quarter turn. The cage dropped a hand's width and stopped dead. He turned it back, and down again, and back, before he said anything at all.

"It holds."

Aiden had the gas meter out of his pack. It was a small yellow box with a screen and a long tube, and he hung it from the bottom of the cage by its strap, alone.

"It goes first. We go down when it comes back up. Not before."

Unit Four lowered it. The cable ran off the drum and over the wheel, slowly, for a long time. Jack watched it go. He couldn't see the bottom. He couldn't see the meter after the first minute, only the cable going down into the dark, straight and thin and steady.

Then it went slack.

"It's at the bottom."

Unit Four didn't let go of the handle. "How long does it need?"

"Five minutes. Then bring it up."

Five minutes was a long time to stand on a bar over a hole. Jack spent it looking at the shaft walls, as far down as his helmet light reached. Old gray concrete, stained in long streaks where water had run down it for years. Iron rungs set into one wall, far apart, one above another, each one thicker than two men are tall and rough with rust. Pale roots had come through a crack near the top and hung down into the dark like ropes.

Unit Four brought the meter up. Aiden took it in both hands and read it out loud, slowly.

"Oxygen's normal. Carbon dioxide's a little higher than up here. That's what you'd expect underground. Nothing flammable. Nothing poisonous."

He looked up. "It's air. It's damp, but it's air."

A new voice came over the channel. Doctor Mercer, from the medical center. She had promised Sarah she would listen.

"Say the oxygen again."

Aiden read it again.

There was a short pause. "Then go. And if that box makes any noise at all, you come back up."

Sarah's voice came over the channel. "Jack. You said you'd look and come back. Go and look."

The cage swung a little when Jack stepped into it, and then steadied. Mark came in after him, then Aiden with the meter clipped to his chest. Unit Four latched the gate and checked it, and checked it again.

Paul's voice came over the channel from the road end, rough. "There's a wire taped to that cable. A plug on the cage. If the radio goes, you plug in. You'll hear us."

Jack found the plug, a small black socket wired to the corner post. "Got it."

"Then go down."

Unit Four turned the handle. The bars of the grate went up past Jack's face, thick and dark and pitted, and then they were above him, a black grid against the bright sky, getting smaller.

The shaft closed around them.

It was quiet. The winch creaked above them, fainter and fainter. The cage turned slowly on the cable, and the walls went past, concrete and rust and roots. A rung went by, huge and cold. A long time later, another. Water was dripping somewhere, slow, heavy drops that took a long time to hit anything.

Sarah's voice came in his ear. "I see you. I've got all three of you. You're about a quarter of the way. You're doing fine."

Then, halfway, her voice broke into crackling.

"Jack, you're—"

Static. "—say again—"

Then nothing at all.

Mark said what Jack was thinking. "That's the concrete."

The light from the top was a small gray square now. Below them the dark went on. Jack watched the walls and counted rungs. Four. Five. It felt like being lowered down the inside of a skyscraper, with no floors.

Then the cage jolted and stopped, and settled, and the cable above them went slack.

They were down.

Mark found the plug and pushed in the lead from his radio. For a second there was only hiss. Then Sarah's voice came back, small and tinny through Paul's wire, and closer than anything had ever sounded.

"Jack? Jack, say something."

"We're down. We're all down."

He heard her breathe out. "Okay. Okay. Lights. Show me."

Jack opened the gate and stepped out of the cage onto the floor of the gallery, and lifted his helmet light.

It didn't reach the roof.

Above them, the dark went up and up and curved over, somewhere far beyond the light, like the inside of a sky. Ahead of them, the floor ran away into the dark in both directions. It was made of tiles. Pale gray tiles, square, each one as wide as a parking lot, laid edge to edge with seams between them as wide as a street gutter. Jack's light slid across three of them before it gave up.

Aiden turned slowly in place, his own light sweeping the floor. "The drawing said a tunnel."

Mark's voice was very low. "It is a tunnel. For people the size it was built for."

Then Jack heard it. He had been hearing it since the cage stopped, and hadn't known what it was. A low, steady, endless sound, like wind in trees, or a road a long way off. Coming from ahead of them, in the dark.

He walked toward it. One tile. Two.

The floor ended at an edge. Below the edge, a long, straight channel cut down the middle of the gallery, wider than a highway. And in the channel, catching his light and throwing it back in a thousand moving pieces, was water.

It was running. Dark, and clear, and moving steadily from his right to his left, downhill, rippling over itself, silver where the light touched it. He could see the bottom of the channel through it, pale tiles under moving water.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Sarah, very quietly, through Paul's wire. "That's the breath. That's what was breathing."

Aiden had crouched at the edge with his light pointed down. "Don't touch it. Not yet. Not a hand, not a boot. I don't know what's in it."

Jack crouched beside him and looked at the water going by, on and on, out of the dark and into the dark.

"Lena. Are you seeing this?"

Lena's voice came back over the wire, slow, as though she were afraid to say the wrong number.

"I'm seeing it. I can't give you a flow rate from a picture."

She stopped, and Jack heard her pen. "But it's more than we use."
